**The famous San Francisco sourdough bacterium turns up in starters all over the world.**

The bacterium is named after San Francisco (*Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, since renamed *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*), and bakers have long claimed you can't get real SF sourdough without SF air. In 2021 the Global Sourdough Project (Landis et al., in the journal *eLife*) sequenced about 500 home starters, mostly from the US and Europe. Geography explained very little of what lived in them. The SF bacterium showed up in starters far from California.

What did matter was which microbes ended up together and how they affected each other. Some yeasts and bacteria reliably appeared as pairs, and certain pairings went with how fast a starter rose and how it smelled. An earlier study by the same group (Reese et al., 2020) found bakers' hands carried unusually high levels of lactic acid bacteria, so the baker partly becomes the starter.

**The twist:** your starter's character comes from its history, not its location: your flour, your feeding schedule, your kitchen temperature and your hands. Two bakers in the same city can grow very different cultures, while you and someone in Tokyo could grow nearly the same one. Over time you're selecting your own ecosystem without meaning to.

**One extra second on New Year's 2017 crashed part of Cloudflare's DNS.**

At midnight UTC on January 1, 2017, a leap second was added and clocks read 23:59:60. Cloudflare's DNS server, written in Go, worked out how long things took by subtracting one wall-clock reading from another. Across the leap second that subtraction came out negative. The negative number went into a random-number call that requires a positive input, and the program panicked. For some customers, DNS lookups that needed this code path failed until a fix rolled out, which took hours to fully deploy.

The underlying bug: wall-clock time can jump backward, from leap seconds, NTP corrections or someone changing the system clock. A monotonic clock only ever moves forward. Go's `time.Now()` didn't include a monotonic reading until Go 1.9 in August 2017, and the Cloudflare outage was a big reason that change was made.

**The twist:** your Flutter app has the same trap. `DateTime.now()` follows the wall clock and can jump. `Stopwatch` is monotonic. If you measure timeouts, debounces or "last fetched N seconds ago" with `DateTime.now()` differences, you're relying on the clock never moving backward. Leap seconds themselves are on the way out: in 2022 the world's measurement authorities voted to stop adding them by 2035, largely because of bugs like this.

1) **Deeper:** Google's "leap smear," which avoids the problem by slowing every clock down slightly for a day instead of adding a second
2) **Sideways:** why GPS time and UTC are now 18 seconds apart and will stay that way
3) **Wildcard:** the 1983 Air Canada jet that ran out of fuel mid-flight because of a pounds-vs-kilograms mix-up and glided to a safe landing

**Adding a road can make everyone's commute slower, even when nobody does anything irrational.**

This is Braess's paradox, described by mathematician Dietrich Braess in 1968. Picture two routes between two towns. Each has one fast stretch whose speed doesn't depend on traffic and one stretch that gets slower as more cars use it. Drivers split evenly between the routes. Now build a free shortcut linking the two traffic-sensitive stretches. Every driver, acting sensibly on their own, takes the shortcut. Those stretches clog up, and everyone ends up slower than before the shortcut existed. Removing an option makes the whole system faster.

It also works with physical objects. In 1991 Joel Cohen and Paul Horowitz published a demonstration in *Nature*: a weight hangs from a network of springs and strings, and cutting one string makes the weight rise.

**The twist:** there's a precise bound on how bad this gets. Tim Roughgarden and Éva Tardos proved in 2002 that when delay grows in proportion to traffic, everyone choosing selfishly is never more than 4/3 as slow as the best centrally planned routing. That figure has a name, the "price of anarchy." It's a known, finite cost, and the shortcut paradox is a case where it hits.

1) **Deeper:** why Seoul tore down an elevated highway in the early 2000s to restore the Cheonggyecheon stream, and what happened to traffic
2) **Sideways:** the springs-and-strings demo explained, so you can build one on a kitchen table
3) **Wildcard:** why prediction markets can become less accurate when more traders join
