**Your AI may be deleting one of the few words that help listeners remember what comes next.**

In 2002 the psychologists Herbert Clark and Jean Fox Tree argued that "uh" and "um" aren't noise. They're real words that speakers choose, even if not consciously. In their analysis of recorded conversations, "uh" tended to come before short delays and "um" before longer ones. On that account, "um" means roughly *hang on, this will take a second*.

Listeners seem to pick up on that. In lab studies, people who heard a hesitation like "thee, uh…" expected the speaker to name something new or unusual. In a 2007 brain-recording study by Martin Corley and colleagues, words that came right after an "er" were recognised better in a surprise memory test later on. Somehow the hesitation made people pay closer attention.

**Possibly the most widely understood word on Earth started as a bad joke about spelling.**

In the late 1830s, Boston newspapers had a fad for abbreviating deliberate misspellings. Think of it as 1830s texting slang. "O.W." stood for "oll wright", "K.Y." for "know yuse" (no use), and "O.K." for "oll korrect". The earliest known printed "O.K." appeared in the *Boston Morning Post* on 23 March 1839, in a joking piece by its editor, Charles Gordon Greene. The linguist Allen Walker Read tracked this down in a series of papers in the 1960s, by reading through old newspapers page by page.

Nearly all those abbreviations died out. "O.K." survived because of an accident of timing. In 1840, President Martin Van Buren ran for re-election, and his nickname was "Old Kinderhook", after his hometown in New York. His supporters formed "O.K. Clubs", and the joke suddenly had a second meaning and a national campaign spreading it. Opponents then put about a smear that Andrew Jackson, Van Buren's mentor, had spelled "all correct" as "oll korrect", which kept the word in the papers even longer. Read found no evidence that Jackson ever did.

**The twist:** most of the origin stories you'll still hear are wrong. One says it comes from the Choctaw word "okeh", which Woodrow Wilson reportedly used to write on documents. Others credit Scottish "och aye", or Greek "ola kala". They lasted because they *sound* more dignified than "a newspaper pun that a re-election campaign happened to need". A word, like a meme, seems to need two hosts to survive: something that gets it noticed, and then something that gives people a reason to keep using it.
