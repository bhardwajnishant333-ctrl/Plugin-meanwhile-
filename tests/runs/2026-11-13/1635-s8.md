**The friction law from physics class doesn't hold for your climbing shoes.**

The classic rule says friction depends only on how hard two surfaces are pressed together, not on how much area touches. Leonardo da Vinci wrote it down, and Amontons published it in 1699. In 1950, Bowden and Tabor showed why it usually works. When two hard surfaces meet, only tiny high points actually touch, often less than 1% of the area you'd see. Press harder and those points flatten, so the real contact area grows with the load. The apparent area cancels out.

Climbing rubber is soft enough to break that. It sinks into the rock's texture, so the real contact area gets close to the full area of the sole. Its grip also comes from two other sources:
- **Adhesion:** molecular stickiness wherever rubber actually touches rock.
- **Hysteresis:** energy lost as the rubber deforms around each grain and springs back a little late.

So for rubber, more area really does mean more friction. That's the physics behind smearing: drop your heel and get as much sole on the rock as you can.

**The twist:** in 1963 Grosch showed that rubber's grip depends on sliding speed and temperature in linked ways. Cooling the rubber shifts its grip in the same way as sliding it faster. So "send temps" (cold, dry days that climbers wait for) aren't only about sweat. Your shoes are a slightly different material in October than in July.

**Sticking is easy for a gecko. Letting go is the hard part.**

A gecko's toes are covered in hair-like bristles called setae, roughly 14,000 per square millimetre. Each one splits at the tip into hundreds of spatulae about 200 nm wide. Those tips get so close to the surface that van der Waals forces, the weak attraction between any two molecules, start to add up. There's no glue and no suction, which is why it works on glass. In 2002, Autumn's lab showed the grip holds on surfaces that repel water as well as ones that attract it. Humidity does add some grip, but it isn't the main mechanism.

The numbers get silly fast. A single seta can hold about 200 µN. Multiply that across all of them and you get the often-quoted claim that one gecko could hold over 100 kg. That figure is a theoretical maximum, and a gecko only uses a small fraction of it.

**The twist:** the grip only switches on when the toe is loaded in shear, meaning pulled slightly along the surface toward the body. Change the angle of a seta to about 30° and the grip drops to almost nothing. To let go, geckos peel their toes up from the tip, like removing tape, which takes almost no force. That's why they can run up glass instead of getting stuck to it. The sticking and the release are controlled by the same thing: the direction of the pull.

You already do this. On a sloper you only hold on while you keep the right body angle, pulling slightly down and in. Change the angle and you're off. Stanford engineers built gecko-style pads on the same principle, and in 2015 a 70 kg person used them to climb a glass wall.
