**Meanwhile:**
Whisper wants to know which language the audio is in before it writes a single word, and code-switched speech doesn't have just one.

**How it works:** the decoder prompt is `<|startoftranscript|>`, then one language token, then `<|transcribe|>` or `<|translate|>`. Unless you force a language, the reference `transcribe()` picks it once from the first 30-second window and keeps it for the whole file. With Hinglish, that one token pulls the output in a direction:

- With `<|hi|>`, English words often come out transliterated into Devanagari.
- With `<|en|>`, the Hindi parts can get quietly translated into English rather than transcribed. That's a known failure mode; I can't give you a rate.

The pretraining data probably made this worse. The original paper describes dropping audio–transcript pairs where the detected spoken language didn't match the transcript language. Mixed-language clips were likely to be filtered out or mislabelled, so the model rarely saw clean examples of switching. I'm confident about the filtering, not about how much it removed. Large-v3 added another 4M hours of audio labelled by large-v2, so v2's habits carried into those labels.

**The twist:** your fine-tune is mostly teaching a convention for writing Hinglish, not a language. The decision that matters is the script rule in your labels. Are English words in Latin script or in Devanagari? If your training corpora disagree, the model learns to switch between the two scripts at random, and that looks like noise. Your WER will also count "computer" vs "कंप्यूटर" as an error even when the model heard the word correctly. Transliterate the hypothesis and the reference into one script before scoring, or you'll misread how well the run is doing.

1. Deeper: how to tell real recognition errors from script mismatches in your eval set, using CER vs WER after transliteration
2. Sideways: why Whisper invents text on silence, and why long Hinglish pauses could trigger it
3. Wildcard: the linguistics of code-switching. Speakers follow surprisingly strict rules about where in a sentence they're allowed to switch

**Meanwhile:**
You switch languages all day, and you almost certainly never put English grammar around Hindi words. You do the reverse without thinking about it.

**The rules:** Shana Poplack's 1980 paper (the title is literally "Sometimes I'll start a sentence in Spanish y termino en español") proposed two constraints from Puerto Rican speakers in New York:

- **Equivalence:** switch only at points where both languages' word order agrees.
- **Free morpheme:** never switch between a word stem and its suffix.

Hindi–English breaks both. Hindi is verb-final and English isn't, and you'll hear "files" as "filon" or "files-ko". Carol Myers-Scotton's Matrix Language Frame model (1993) fits better. One language is the **matrix**: it supplies the grammatical skeleton, meaning word order, postpositions, verb endings, and the small function words. The other language mostly contributes content words dropped into the slots.

You can hear it in the light-verb pattern: "maine file upload kar di", "usko convince karo", "meeting postpone ho gayi". The English verb arrives bare, and Hindi *karna/hona* carries the tense, gender and agreement. You almost never hear the mirror image, a Hindi verb stem with English "-ed" on it.

**The twist:** "code-switched" sounds like two languages taking turns, but most Hinglish is Hindi grammar with English vocabulary. That helps your fine-tune in two ways:

- `<|hi|>` is the right frame much more often than a 50/50 mix would suggest.
- The hard tokens aren't whole English phrases. They're the joins: English stems with Hindi endings ("filon", "settings mein") and the *kar/ho* that follows an English verb. Pull those out of your eval set and score them separately.

1. Deeper: the cases where the matrix language flips mid-conversation, and what triggers it (topic, the listener, or emotion)

3. Wildcard: why people tend to swear and count in their first language even after decades of living in another one
