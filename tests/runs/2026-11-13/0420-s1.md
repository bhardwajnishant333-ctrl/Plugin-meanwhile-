**A truncated sinc filter overshoots by about 9%, and adding more taps never makes that number smaller.**

When you cut a sinc filter off at N taps, you're multiplying it by a rectangular window. The frequency response then ripples near the cutoff, with a peak of about 9% of the jump. A longer filter pushes those ripples into a thinner band right at the edge, but the peak stays the same height, even with infinitely many terms. This is the Gibbs phenomenon. In 1898 Albert Michelson's mechanical harmonic analyzer, which summed 80 Fourier terms, kept drawing this overshoot. He suspected the machine was at fault. J. Willard Gibbs explained it in letters to *Nature* in 1898 and 1899. It later turned out Henry Wilbraham had worked it out in 1848, and almost nobody noticed.

The twist: tap count and stopband quality are mostly separate settings. With a rectangular window, the stopband attenuation tops out around 21 dB no matter how long the filter is. The window shape decides how deep the stopband goes, and the length decides how sharp the transition is. In a Kaiser window, that depth is set by β. So when you review the port, look at the window function and its β (or whatever the C code uses), not just the tap loop. If the window gets dropped or slightly mistranslated, the code still compiles, still sounds roughly fine, and still measures as wrong.

**Scrabble's point values are a 1930s measurement of newspaper English, and they have never been recalibrated.**

Alfred Mosher Butts was an architect who lost his work in the Depression. While designing the game that became Scrabble (first called Lexiko, then Criss-Crosswords around 1938), he counted how often each letter appeared in front pages of *The New York Times*, and that count set both how many tiles each letter got and what each tile scored. He made one deliberate change: he cut the S tiles to 4, below what its frequency called for, so that plurals wouldn't be too easy. James Brunot licensed the game in 1948, named it Scrabble, and kept the 100-tile set. The values have stayed the same since.

The twist: Butts measured how often letters show up in running prose, but what matters in play is how useful a letter is within the list of valid words, and those are different things. Later dictionaries added short words like QI, ZA, XU and ZO, which make the "hard" letters much easier to play. People who have recalculated the values from modern word lists, such as Joshua Lewis's Valett project, generally find that Z, X and Q come out worth less than 10 and 8. I don't trust my memory of their exact numbers, so I won't quote them. Top players already count this in: a Z on a board full of hooks is easy points, not a problem tile.

Constants measured from one corpus long outlive that corpus. If any of the C filter's coefficient tables are precomputed for a particular sample rate or quality setting, they're in the same position as Butts's tile values.
