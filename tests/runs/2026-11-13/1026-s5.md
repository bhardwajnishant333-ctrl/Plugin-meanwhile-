**In 2008, a team in Rome found that the classic way games simulate flocks doesn't match how real flocks hold together.**

Craig Reynolds' "boids" (SIGGRAPH 1987) is the model behind almost every game flock. Each bird reacts to all the neighbours within a fixed radius. Ballerini and colleagues (STARFLAG project, PNAS 2008) filmed starling flocks over Rome with stereo cameras and rebuilt each bird's 3D position. They found that each bird tracks its nearest 6 or 7 neighbours however far away those neighbours are. The rule is "the closest seven", not "everyone within X metres".

**The twist:** the "closest seven" rule is why the flock doesn't break apart. When a falcon dives and the flock spreads out, a fixed radius suddenly contains nobody, and boids-style flocks split into pieces. A fixed count of neighbours stays connected at any density. In their simulations, the count-based rule held together under the kind of disturbance that broke the radius-based one. Those were starlings, but your dunlin wheeling over the mudflat face the same problem. In Godot terms, an Area2D radius check is the boids rule, and "sort by distance, take the first 7" is the bird's rule.

**The 7 may not be a brain limit at all. It may be the cheapest number that lets the flock agree.**

Most people's first guess is a cognitive limit, since animals and people can track only a handful of moving objects at once. In 2013, Young, Leonard, Cavagna and colleagues (PLoS Computational Biology) tested another explanation using the real Rome flock data. They treated the flock as a network of neighbours passing a noisy signal along and asked: how many neighbours should each bird watch to reach consensus most reliably, given that watching more costs more effort? The best value came out at about 6 or 7, which matches the observed number. It also depended on flock shape: starling flocks are thin sheets, and that geometry is part of why 7 comes out on top.

A related result from the same group (Cavagna et al., PNAS 2010) is that changes in heading are correlated across the whole flock, however big it is. A turn that starts at one edge reaches the far edge, so the flock acts more like a single body than a crowd. Their later work (Attanasi et al., Nature Physics 2014) measured turns crossing a flock at roughly tens of metres per second, faster than a simple chain of reactions would predict. I'm sure of the finding but less sure of the exact speed.

**The twist:** if 7 is an optimum for a given flock shape rather than a fixed limit, the right number of neighbours should change with the shape. A flat flock and a deep 3D ball should settle on different numbers. For your game, the number of neighbours is a setting to tune against the flock's shape, not a constant to copy from a paper.

**The riskiest lines in this port may be settings your project never wrote down.**

project.godot only stores settings you changed. Anything left at its default gets whatever the new engine's default is. I believe 2D default gravity is the main one here: 98 in Godot 3 and 980 in Godot 4. Check the docs for your exact versions before you rely on that. If your player script reads gravity from ProjectSettings and you never changed it, your character just got ten times heavier, and no line in the diff shows it.

Godot 4's CharacterBody2D also changes how movement feels:

- **Floor snap.** Godot 3 needed `move_and_slide_with_snap`, and you had to zero the snap vector when jumping or the character stuck to the floor. Godot 4 replaces this with `floor_snap_length`, and the snap switches off on its own while the body is moving upward. Old workarounds for jumping can now fight the engine.
- **Leaving moving platforms.** `platform_on_leave` is new and defaults to adding the platform's velocity when you jump off. If Godot 3 didn't do that for you, jumps off moving platforms will now carry extra speed. Check this one against your own 3.x build.
- **`move_and_slide()` takes no arguments now.** It reads and writes the `velocity` property directly. Code that relied on the value the old call returned can quietly behave differently.

**The twist:** a correct port can still feel wrong, because "feel" lives in engine defaults as much as in your code. Reading the agent's diff won't catch it. Measuring will: in both builds, record jump height at the top of the arc, time in the air, and how far one tap of run moves you, then compare. Ten minutes with a stopwatch overlay will catch what a code review can't.

**One shorebird's survival has been written into a fishing rule: in Delaware Bay, the number of crabs people can catch is set partly by counting red knots.**

The rufa red knot flies from Tierra del Fuego to the Canadian Arctic, up to about 15,000 km each way. My "10,000 km" in the last list was too low. In mid-May to early June the birds stop in Delaware Bay, timed to horseshoe crabs coming ashore on the high spring tides to lay eggs. In roughly two weeks a knot can nearly double its body weight on those eggs before flying on to the Arctic.

The mechanism is in the bird's gut. Theunis Piersma's work showed that knots shrink their digestive organs before long flights to save weight, and rebuild them on arrival. A bird that lands with a shrunken gizzard needs food that is soft and easy to digest, and a beach covered in crab eggs is exactly that. Hard-shelled clams aren't, until the gizzard has grown back.

In the 1990s crabs were heavily over-harvested as bait for eel and whelk traps, the egg supply fell, and rufa knot numbers crashed. The US listed the bird as threatened in 2015. Since 2012 the Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission has used an "Adaptive Resource Management" model, in which the allowed catch of crabs, female crabs especially, depends partly on red knot population estimates. Horseshoe crab blood is also used in the LAL test that checks injectable drugs and vaccines for bacterial contamination. Synthetic replacements exist, and how widely regulators accept them is changing. I'm not sure of the current US status.

**The twist:** what limits the knot isn't the 15,000 km. It's whether one stop has enough food in one two-week window. The system fails at its tightest refuelling point, not its longest stretch. The same is true of builds, pipelines and migrations.
