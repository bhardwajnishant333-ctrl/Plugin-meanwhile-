**Bobby Fischer, famous for being impossible to deal with, invented the clock rule most of online chess runs on.**

**How it works:** Fischer patented a digital chess clock that adds a fixed amount of time after every move you make. The US patent was granted in 1989; I believe the number is 4,884,255, but treat that as likely, not certain. The clock was first used at a major event in his 1992 rematch with Spassky in Yugoslavia. Before that, clocks only counted down. A won position could be lost on time in a scramble, and long games were often adjourned overnight. With an increment, you always get at least a few seconds per move, however low your clock runs.

David Bronstein had a competing idea, the "Bronstein delay". It also gives you a few free seconds each move, but any of those seconds you don't use are lost instead of added to your clock. US Chess often uses delay; FIDE and Lichess use Fischer increment. That's the "+2" in 3+2.

**The twist:** A Fischer clock works like a token bucket, the rate limiter behind many APIs, except it refills per move instead of per second. With increment, it rarely can, because you only win on time if the other player can't move at all, even with the extra seconds.
