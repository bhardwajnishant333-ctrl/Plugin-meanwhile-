**Medieval Europe's greatest trading network ran for centuries without the bookkeeping trick its Italian rivals relied on.**

By the 1300s, Florentine and Venetian firms were using double-entry bookkeeping: every transaction is posted twice, as a debit and a credit, so the books can be checked against themselves. If they don't balance, something went wrong. Hanseatic merchants mostly didn't bother. Their ledgers were closer to lists of who owes whom, kept partner by partner. Hildebrand Veckinchusen, who traded between Bruges and the Baltic around 1400, is the usual example. His surviving books (held in the Tallinn archive, I believe) are dense records of debts with no balancing structure. He ended up bankrupt and jailed for debt in Bruges. Historians still argue about whether the Hansa was behind the times or simply didn't need the check. Their trade ran through relatives and trusted partners in a small number of trading posts. When you trust the counterparty, a second set of entries looks like overhead.

**The twist:** logical replication works the way the Hansa kept books. The subscriber applies what the publisher sends and doesn't check that the two sides end up equal. Lag reaching zero means the replica has caught up with the stream. It doesn't mean the two databases match. On a 12 → 17 cutover, the classic thing the stream never carries is **sequence values**. Logical replication doesn't copy them, so the new cluster's sequences are still at their starting values. Your first insert after cutover can collide with a primary key that already exists. The Italian-style fix is cheap: before flipping traffic, compare row counts or checksums on your hottest tables, and run `setval` on every sequence (with some headroom).

**Your lag graph is probably measuring the one part of the replication that isn't working hard.**

When you create a subscription with `copy_data = true`, the main apply worker doesn't copy any data itself. It hands tables to tablesync workers, and by default only 2 run at a time (`max_sync_workers_per_subscription`). Each tablesync worker opens its own replication slot on the publisher, takes a snapshot and COPYs its table. It then replays the changes made to that table since the snapshot until it catches up with the main worker. Only then does it hand the table back. Meanwhile the main slot moves along happily, because it has almost nothing to apply for tables still being copied. The lag graph can sit near zero while one worker is an hour into COPYing your biggest table.

**Where the real progress is**, on the PG17 subscriber:
- `pg_subscription_rel.srsubstate` shows each table's state: `i` (init), `d` (copying), `f` (copy done), `s` (catching up), `r` (ready). You aren't done until every row is `r`.
- `pg_stat_progress_copy` shows bytes and tuples copied for each table.

**The twist is on the PG12 side.** Each sync slot holds WAL on the publisher from the moment its COPY starts. PG12 has no `max_slot_wal_keep_size` (that arrived in 13), so nothing caps it. A long copy on a write-heavy primary keeps all that WAL until the copy finishes. The graph worth watching is WAL retained per slot on the old primary:

```sql
SELECT slot_name, pg_size_pretty(pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_current_wal_lsn(), restart_lsn))
FROM pg_replication_slots;
```

**A trade network with no army, no king and no capital beat the Count of Flanders by walking out of town.**

The German merchants at Bruges, the main market of northern Europe, governed themselves through a Kontor. Its elected aldermen settled disputes among members, set the rules and could fine or expel any member who broke them. Its real power came from leaving. In 1358, after complaints about seized goods and broken privileges, the Hanseatic towns declared an embargo on Flanders and moved the whole Kontor to Dordrecht. Flemish cloth towns lost their Baltic buyers, and by 1360 Flanders had restored and widened the merchants' privileges. The Hansa used the same move again in 1388 and 1451. It applied the same pressure internally too. A member town that broke ranks could be "unhansed," meaning expelled. Cologne was thrown out in 1471 for siding with England and readmitted in 1476.

**The twist:** in 1994 the economists Greif, Milgrom and Weingast used this exact case to explain why rulers *wanted* strong merchant guilds. A prince could always cheat one foreign merchant. He couldn't cheat one without losing all of them, and that made his promises believable, so trade grew. Sheilagh Ogilvie argued the opposite in 2011: guilds like this were mostly cartels that kept outsiders out and raised prices, and the "enforcement" story flatters them. The argument isn't settled. Both sides agree on the mechanism, though: the threat only worked because the merchants' assets could be moved. Goods and credit could leave Bruges in a season. A cloth industry couldn't.

The same goes for your cutover. It's safe only to the extent you can walk back out. A reverse subscription from 17 back to 12 after the flip is what makes leaving possible.
