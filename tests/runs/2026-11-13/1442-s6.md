**The microbes that start your hot sauce aren't the ones that finish it. A jar that goes quiet is often just changing hands.**

Lacto-ferments go through a succession of microbes. This is best documented for sauerkraut, in decades of work by Carl Pederson and others. *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* usually takes over first. It's heterofermentative, so it turns sugar into lactic acid plus CO2, ethanol and acetic acid. That's where your bubbles and the slightly funky smell come from. As the pH drops, it acidifies its own home until it can't grow. More acid-tolerant lactobacilli take over, mainly *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* (renamed from *Lactobacillus* in 2020). Its main pathway turns sugar into lactic acid alone, with no gas. That's what pushes the sauce to its final sharp acidity.

The twist: "it stopped bubbling" doesn't mean "it died." Often the gas-making crew has acidified itself out of a job and the quiet, more acid-tolerant crew is finishing. Mango adds a lot of sugar, so expect a more vigorous early phase and a higher chance of a white film. A flat white film is usually kahm yeast. Fuzzy, raised or colored growth is mold, and that's a different conversation. Check by smell and pH, not by bubbles. Under about 4.6 is the safety line for acidified foods. Most finished ferments land well under it, often around 3.5.

**Your language is named after a Dane who worked out in 1909 why phone calls stall. His math says a server at 90% busy is twice as slow as one at 80%.**

Agner Krarup Erlang worked for the Copenhagen Telephone Company and wanted to know how many lines an exchange needed. His 1909 and 1917 papers founded queueing theory, and traffic intensity is still measured in "erlangs." Ericsson's language in the 1980s is generally described as named after him, with a convenient double meaning as "Ericsson Language."

The core result is brutal. In the simplest single-server queue (M/M/1), the average time a request spends in the system is the service time divided by (1 − utilization):

- 50% busy: 2x the service time
- 80% busy: 5x
- 90% busy: 10x
- 99% busy: 100x

Latency doesn't climb steadily as load grows. It stays flat for a long time, then shoots up near the end.

The twist for your migration: a LiveView process per user scales well, but the dashboards will probably all call some shared GenServer for stats, a cache or PubSub fan-out. That one process is a single-server queue with a mailbox. Each call costs 1ms of work? At 900 calls/sec it's 90% busy and every caller waits about 10ms on average. Add 9% more traffic and the wait becomes about 100ms. The spare capacity you leave on that process is your latency budget.

2) **Sideways**: Little's Law, and how to estimate any queue with only a stopwatch
3) **Wildcard**: why one long supermarket line beats many short ones, and why most stores still don't do it

**Meanwhile:**
**London beat cholera by building the right fix for the wrong reason.**

In the hot summer of 1858, the Thames was effectively an open sewer and the smell got so bad it was called the Great Stink. MPs hung curtains soaked in chloride of lime in the Houses of Parliament. The ruling theory was miasma: disease spread through bad air. So Parliament moved fast to remove the *smell*, and Joseph Bazalgette got the go-ahead for intercepting sewers that would carry waste east, downstream of the city. He famously sized the pipes far bigger than the projections required.

The miasma theory was wrong. John Snow had already shown in 1854, by mapping deaths around the Broad Street pump in Soho, that cholera spread through contaminated water, and he was largely dismissed. But sewers that moved waste away from the drinking water happened to cut the real route of transmission. The 1866 outbreak hit hardest in East London, an area not yet fully connected, and that helped make the waterborne explanation hard to deny.

The twist: a fix that works isn't proof that your explanation was right. The miasma-driven sewers worked because the theory's prescription happened to overlap with the true cause. Nobody could tell until a case arrived where the two came apart.
