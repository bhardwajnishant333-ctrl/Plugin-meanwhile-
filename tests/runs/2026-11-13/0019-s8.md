**The language your app runs on is named after a man who never wrote a line of code. He counted phone calls.**

Agner Krarup Erlang was a Danish mathematician at the Copenhagen Telephone Company. In 1909 he showed that incoming calls follow a Poisson distribution. In 1917 he published the Erlang B formula, which tells you how many lines you need so that only a given share of callers get a busy signal. When Ericsson built a language for telephone switches in 1986 (Joe Armstrong, Robert Virding and Mike Williams), they named it after him. "ERicsson LANGuage" was a convenient double meaning.

His formula shows that capacity doesn't scale linearly. At a 1% busy-signal target:
- 10 erlangs of traffic (an erlang is one line kept busy full-time) needs 18 lines, so each line is busy about 55% of the time.
- 100 erlangs needs about 117 lines, busy about 85% of the time.

Ten times the traffic needs only about 6.5 times the capacity.

**The twist:** one big shared pool beats many small ones, because random spikes in different places tend to cancel out. That makes the BEAM's single scheduler pool across all your cores more than a convenience. It's Erlang's 1917 maths showing up in the runtime. The usual way people break it is splitting things into many small fixed pools that each sit idle at different times.

1. **Deeper**: why the BEAM preempts every process after about 4,000 "reductions" instead of letting one hog a core
2. **Sideways**: the same Poisson maths explains why stacking 100 subs only gives you 10× the signal-to-noise in your astro shots
3. **Wildcard**: the 1990 AT&T outage, when one misplaced `break` took down long-distance calling across the US for 9 hours

**Meanwhile:**
**AT&T's long-distance network went down because of its own recovery code.**

On 15 January 1990, a 4ESS switch in Manhattan hit a minor fault and reset itself. It took about 4 seconds, which is routine. When it came back it told its neighbours "I'm back online" and started sending call traffic again.

A month earlier, AT&T had rolled out a software update to all 114 of those switches. The C code for handling incoming messages had a `break` inside an `if` that sat inside a `switch`. It was meant to leave the `if`, but it left the whole `switch` block, so the code skipped a step and overwrote data that was still in use. The bug only triggered when a second message arrived within about 10 milliseconds of the first, which is exactly what happens when a recovered switch starts sending traffic in bursts.

So each neighbour crashed, reset itself, came back and announced "I'm back", which crashed its own neighbours. The whole network kept knocking itself over in waves for about 9 hours. Roughly half of long-distance calls failed, often reported as around 70 million calls. Engineers fixed it by rolling back to the previous software. (The overall story is well documented. The exact counts vary between sources.)

**The twist:** no single switch was broken. Each one ran its recovery routine correctly, and the outage only existed in how they interacted. Recovery code runs least often, so it gets the least testing, and it is also the code most likely to run on every node at once. Your app has a version of this: after a deploy, every LiveView client reconnects within seconds of the others. The mount path and the reconnect backoff in the new `phoenix.js` are your "I'm back" message, so they're worth checking when the agent finishes.

**You're right, it's jitter. The surprise is which kind wins, and that jitter alone doesn't save you.**

In 2015 Marc Brooker of AWS simulated clients fighting over one resource and compared retry strategies:
- **Plain exponential backoff:** clients still retry in synchronised waves. Their gaps get longer, but they stay in step.
- **"Equal jitter"** (wait half the backoff, then a random amount up to the other half): better, but clearly not the best.
- **"Full jitter"** (wait a random time from 0 to the backoff cap): the best on total work done, even though some clients retry almost immediately. Most engineers expect it to lose because it seems reckless. It wins because spreading retries out matters more than holding them back.

The less obvious part came later. Brooker wrote that backoff mainly helps *closed* systems, meaning a fixed number of clients waiting on their own requests. In an *open* system, where new requests keep arriving whatever happens, one client backing off barely changes the load the server sees. The real damage comes from retries multiplying across layers. If each of 5 layers makes 3 retries, a single user action can turn into up to 4⁵ = 1,024 attempts at the bottom. The real fix is a retry budget, such as a token bucket that stops all retrying once failures pass a threshold. AWS SDKs later added retry quotas along these lines. (I'm confident of the overall argument but not of the exact SDK details.)

**The twist:** jitter spreads load out over time but doesn't reduce it. Only limiting retries reduces it. For your upgrade: as far as I know, the `phoenix.js` socket's default `reconnectAfterMs` is a fixed schedule (10, 50, 100… up to 5000 ms) with no jitter. After a deploy every client follows the same schedule, so they reconnect in waves. A one-line custom `reconnectAfterMs` with random jitter is cheap insurance. Check your version's defaults before relying on my memory.

1. **Deeper**: metastable failures, where a system stays down after the original trigger is gone (a 2021 HotOS paper named the pattern)
2. **Sideways**: why astronomers deliberately add jitter by "dithering" between sub-exposures, to stop fixed-pattern sensor noise from adding up
3. **Wildcard**: Mariner 1 and the "missing hyphen", the most-told bug myth in software history
