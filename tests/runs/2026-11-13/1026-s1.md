**The voltage from your thermocouple doesn't come from its junction. It comes from the wire.**

Most diagrams show a magic voltage source at the welded tip, but that's not where it comes from. The Seebeck voltage builds up along the length of each wire, wherever that wire passes through a temperature gradient. Metrology texts, NIST's thermocouple material among them, are blunt about it: the junction just joins the two wires electrically and sits at the temperature you want to read. It produces nothing by itself.

Here's how it works. Each metal makes a small voltage per degree of gradient it crosses. Two different metals make different amounts. Your MAX31855 (or similar) reads the difference between the two wires, then adds cold-junction compensation for the temperature at its own terminals. If both wires are uniform all the way along, the total depends only on the two end temperatures. That's why the junction story works well enough to fool almost everyone.

**Why it matters:** the error comes from wherever the steep gradient is, not from the tip. In a roaster that's where the probe passes through the hot drum wall or faceplate. A section of wire that's kinked, oxidized or work-hardened there adds its own wrong voltage. A clean bead in the beans won't save you. If your bean-temp reading drifts or acts strangely after a probe gets bent, that's where to look before blaming your new PID code.

1) **Deeper:** Type K wire drifts on its own between roughly 250 and 550 °C as its crystal structure slowly reorders. Does your environment-temp probe live in that range?
2) **Sideways:** integral windup. Your heater maxes out during charge, so what does the integral term do while it's stuck?
3) **Wildcard:** what chalk actually does to grip on the wall (the friction studies don't fully agree)

**Meanwhile:**
**Your integral term acts as if your heater has no maximum.**

When the PID asks for 140% power, the heater gives 100% and the controller isn't told. The error stays large, so the integral keeps adding it up, minute after minute, as if the extra 40% were arriving. When the temperature finally catches up, all that stored integral has to unwind first. Until it does, the heater stays on hard, and that's your overshoot.

**Why your roaster is the worst case:** at charge you dump cold beans into a hot drum, and bean temp drops to the turning point. The error is huge and the heater sits at 100% for minutes. A plain PID builds up enough integral during that time to blow past your target later in the roast.

**The two standard fixes:**
- **Clamping (conditional integration):** stop integrating while the output is maxed out and the error would push it further. Brett Beauregard's Arduino PID library does a simple version: it limits the integral term to the output range. Your old sketch probably didn't, if it was hand-rolled.
- **Back-calculation:** feed the gap between what you asked for and what the heater actually gave back into the integrator: `i += ki*e*dt + (u_sat - u_raw)/t_t * dt`. The integral then follows the real heater instead of the imagined one. Åström & Murray's *Feedback Systems* covers this, including how to choose `t_t`. It's about 3 lines in your Rust loop.

**The twist:** windup isn't a bug in PID. The bug is that the controller can't see the actuator's limit. Any loop that can't observe its own limits, from a rate limiter to an autoscaler with a ceiling, builds up the same pent-up debt.
