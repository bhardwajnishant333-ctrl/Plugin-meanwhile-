In a letterpress shop, a "font" was a physical thing: one full set of metal letters in one typeface, one size and one style. It was cast in a foundry, and the word comes from the French *fonte*, meaning "something melted and cast." 12-point Caslon italic was one font. 14-point Caslon italic was a different one, kept in its own case. Capitals lived in the upper case and small letters in the lower one, which is where "uppercase" and "lowercase" come from.

When printers wanted more space between lines, they slipped thin strips of lead between the rows of type. That's "leading," and designers still use the word for line spacing on screens today.

**The story that QWERTY was designed to slow typists down probably isn't true, and the real history is stranger.**

The layout came from Christopher Latham Sholes, a newspaper man in Milwaukee. He reworked it again and again from the late 1860s until Remington began selling the Sholes & Glidden Type-Writer in 1874. His early prototypes had keys in roughly alphabetical order, laid out like a piano.

So why did he move them? Two forces:

- **Jams, but not the way the story says.** The typebars sat in a circle under the machine. When two neighbouring bars struck one after the other, they could tangle. Splitting up common letter pairs was meant to let typists go fast *without* jamming. The goal was speed, not slowness.
- **Telegraph operators.** In 2011, two Kyoto researchers, Koichi and Motoko Yasuoka, argued that Morse code operators shaped the layout, because they were some of the first serious typists. Their example: in American Morse, "Z" sounds very close to "SE", which may be why those letters sit together. It's an argument rather than settled fact, but it fits the patent records better than the "slow them down" tale.

There's a detail for your collection too. On the first Sholes & Glidden machines, the typebars struck the paper from underneath, so the typist couldn't see what they were typing until they lifted the carriage. Those machines also typed only in capitals.

**The twist:** a later layout, Dvorak, became famous as the "better" keyboard. But many of the studies proving it was better were run by August Dvorak himself, who held the patent. Economists Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis made this point in a 1990 paper called "The Fable of the Keys." QWERTY may not be a famous mistake after all. It may simply have been good enough, and it got there first.

**For about a thousand years, Europe's most trusted ink has been slowly eating the documents it was written on.**

The ink is iron gall ink. You crush oak galls, the hard little balls oaks grow around wasp eggs, to get tannic and gallic acids. Then you mix in iron sulfate, which was sold as "green vitriol" or "copperas," plus gum arabic to thicken it. It goes onto the page pale and darkens over the next few days as the iron reacts with air. That's why it looked trustworthy: it soaked into the fibres and turned black, and you couldn't scrape it off cleanly. Leonardo used it, and so did Bach and countless lawyers and clerks.

**The mechanism:** most recipes used too much iron and left acid behind. Spare iron speeds up the breakdown of cellulose, the fibre that paper is made of, and the acid weakens it further. Over the centuries the ink browns, cracks and finally falls out, leaving letter-shaped holes. Conservators call it "ink corrosion." Bach's original music manuscripts in Berlin are famous victims. In the 1990s, Dutch conservators developed a calcium phytate bath that binds the loose iron, and it's still a standard treatment.

**The twist:** the very things that made it the best permanent ink are what make it destructive. It bonds into the paper and keeps reacting, so on the worst pages the more ink there was, the bigger the hole. If you've ever seen lace-like holes in an old letter or ledger, the writing ate them.
