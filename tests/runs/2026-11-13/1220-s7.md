**A juniper can be mostly dead and still be in perfect health. That's not damage, it's how the tree is built.**

Junipers, like Great Basin bristlecone pines, have a strongly *sectored* water system. The wood carries water in fairly straight lines, so a given root mostly feeds one strip of trunk and the branches above that strip. Water doesn't get pooled and shared across the whole tree. In a harsh place, when a root dies (from drought, erosion or rockfall), only its sector dies with it. The cambium there dries out, the bark peels away, and you're left with a narrow ribbon of live bark spiralling up through bare deadwood. This is called *strip-bark* growth. Your live veins and shari are the bonsai version of the same thing.

In the 1950s the tree-ring scientist Edmund Schulman found that the oldest bristlecones weren't growing in good sites. They were in the harshest, driest ones, and they were mostly strip-bark trees. One of them was "Methuselah", cored in 1957 at roughly 4,800 years old. In his words, adversity begets longevity. Some Sierra junipers are thought to be 2,000 to 3,000 years old, but I'm less sure of those numbers because a hollow or strip-bark trunk is very hard to date.

**The twist:** the oldest trees may last because they were never built as one connected system. A tree that shares water across its whole trunk can die all at once. A sectored tree can only lose one piece at a time. Engineers later gave this idea a name: bulkheads, which means splitting a system into compartments so one failure can't sink the rest. Junipers had it first. Each time you carve a live vein you're shaping the tree's failure lines, not just how it looks.

**A tree has to pick between plumbing that moves a lot of water and plumbing that doesn't break. Junipers picked the plumbing that doesn't break.**

Water moves up a tree under tension: it's pulled from the leaves, not pushed from the roots. If the pull gets too strong, as it does in drought, an air bubble can snap into a conduit, and that conduit goes dead. This is called embolism. Wide conduits carry far more water, because flow rises with roughly the fourth power of the radius. But they also take on air more easily, especially after freeze-thaw cycles, where bubble size scales with conduit width.

Oaks and other broadleaf trees use *vessels*, tubes that can be hundreds of micrometres wide and sometimes metres long. They move a lot of water fast. Conifers like junipers use *tracheids*, cells only a few millimetres long and a few tens of micrometres wide, so water has to pass through thousands of small valves called pits. Conifer pits have a plug called a torus that seals shut when a neighbouring tracheid fills with air, so the bubble stays in one cell. Junipers rank among the most drought-proof woody plants ever measured. Some can keep water moving at tensions that would fail most trees, though I'm not confident of exact megapascal figures for particular species. The cost is slow, narrow plumbing. Less water means less photosynthesis, and less photosynthesis means slow growth. Sectoring adds another layer of safety on top of all this.

**The twist:** in 2016, Gleason and around 40 co-authors (*New Phytologist*) compiled data on hundreds of woody species and found the safety-versus-efficiency trade-off is surprisingly *weak*. No species has both. But many have *neither*: they're slow and fragile at once. So the trade-off is a ceiling, not a line. You can't be fast and safe, but being slow doesn't make you safe for free. Junipers earned their safety with the valves and the sectors, not just by being slow.

For your trees, this is why a juniper takes so long to recover from root work. Its plumbing was never built to regrow capacity quickly.

`@hono/node-server` does have a named export `serve`, and has in every 1.x version. So Node is probably loading a different file than you think, or a CommonJS build whose exports it can't detect. Coming from Deno, these are the most likely causes, most likely first:

**1. A `node_modules` folder left over from Deno.** If `deno.json` had `nodeModulesDir` set, Deno built `node_modules` its own way (symlinks into `node_modules/.deno/`), possibly with old versions. Node can resolve that to the wrong entry point. Delete it and reinstall with npm:
```sh
rm -rf node_modules deno.lock package-lock.json
npm i hono @hono/node-server
```

**2. An old or mismatched version.** Check which version is installed and what Node actually loads:
```sh
npm ls @hono/node-server hono
node -p "require.resolve('@hono/node-server')"
node --input-type=module -e "import('@hono/node-server').then(m => console.log(Object.keys(m)))"
```
If that last command prints `[ 'default' ]` and no `serve`, Node is getting a CommonJS file with no named exports it can detect. Upgrade to the latest version, or as a temporary workaround:
```ts
import nodeServer from '@hono/node-server'
const { serve } = nodeServer
```

**3. Import paths left over from Deno.** Check that nothing still maps that name through an import map, a `package.json` `"imports"` field, a `tsconfig` `paths` entry, or a vendored copy. `tsconfig` paths are ignored when Node runs `.ts` files directly, so an alias that worked under Deno can quietly point Node somewhere else.
