**Vite's dev server doesn't bundle your app, and that's both why it's fast and where your migration can break.**

CRA ran webpack, which built one bundle before your browser saw anything. Vite works differently in dev. It sends each source file to the browser as a native ES module, and the browser fetches your imports one at a time. That's why it starts almost instantly, because it compiles a file only when the browser asks for it.

There's a catch, and Vite's own docs use it as their example: `lodash-es` is made of more than 600 small internal modules. Served unbundled, one import would set off 600+ HTTP requests and stall the page. So Vite pre-bundles your `node_modules` dependencies with esbuild. It also converts CommonJS packages to ESM at the same step. Your own code stays unbundled.

The twist is that `vite build` uses a different pipeline. For a long time that was Rollup. Newer Vite versions are switching to Rolldown to make dev and build match, but I'm not sure which one your agent installed. Where the two differ, an app can work in `vite dev` and break in production. The usual causes are CommonJS default imports (`import x from 'cjs-pkg'` gives you `{ default: x }` in one mode but not the other) and some dynamic imports.

So when the agent says it's done, run `vite build && vite preview` and click through the dashboard. The migration isn't proven until the built version works.

**In chess, more preparation usually wins. Chess960 was built to make preparation worth almost nothing.**

Bobby Fischer announced the variant in Buenos Aires in 1996. His complaint was that elite chess had turned into a memory contest, where top players prepared opening lines twenty or more moves deep with computers and the game was often decided before anyone had to think.

The numbers explain why 960 breaks that. Two bishops on opposite colours give 4 × 4 = 16 options. The queen then has 6 squares, and the two knights have 10 ways to fill the remaining 5. The last 3 squares go rook, king, rook. That's 16 × 6 × 10 = 960. The standard starting position is one of them: #518 in Scharnagl's numbering. If the position is drawn at random, a line you've prepared for one setup applies to about 1 game in 960. Your best preparation is worth roughly a thousandth of what it is in normal chess.

You can see the effect at the top. At the first official FIDE Fischer Random World Championship in 2019, Wesley So beat Magnus Carlsen 13.5 to 2.5 in the final, against the world's best player.

**Fischer's best invention may be the clock, not the variant.**

Before Fischer's clock, a game had a fixed amount of time per period. Near the end you could have a completely winning position and still lose because your flag fell while you hurried through moves. Fischer filed US patent 4,884,255 on 5 August 1988, and it was granted in November 1989. It describes a digital clock that adds time to your clock after every move: the increment. It was first used at his privately organised 1992 rematch with Spassky, and FIDE used it at the 1998 World Championship. Today most top tournaments use it. Every time control written as "3+2" is Fischer's clock, including the online 960 you play at night.

What the increment changes: you keep the time you've saved, and each move tops it up by a fixed amount. You can spend a lot on one critical move, and you always get a minimum for the next one. A frantic scramble with seconds left becomes a game where a lost position still has to be beaten move by move.

**Twist:** that's a token bucket, the algorithm used to rate-limit APIs. The bucket holds a stock you can spend in bursts, it refills at a steady rate, and the refill guarantees a minimum. Fischer built a rate limiter into a chess clock years before most developers would have known the term. If your admin dashboard calls an API that throttles requests, there's very likely a token bucket behind it. A "3+2" game and "100 requests, plus 10 per second" are the same design.

1) Deeper: Bronstein delay versus Fischer increment, and why US Chess long favoured delay
2) Sideways: the leaky bucket, the token bucket's sibling, and why the difference decides whether your API calls get rejected or just slowed down
