**The Baltic Dry Index isn't built from a single price anyone actually paid.**

Every working day, the Baltic Exchange in London asks a panel of shipbrokers what a standard ship would charge on a set of standard routes *today*. The panel gives its best professional judgment, whether or not a deal was signed on that route. Those estimates are averaged into route rates, and the rates for three ship sizes (Capesize, Panamax, Supramax) are combined into the BDI. Here's the twist: billions of dollars in forward freight agreements (FFAs) settle in cash against those assessed numbers. Real money changes hands based on what brokers *say* a voyage would cost. It works for three reasons. The ships really are standardized, the route definitions are strict, and a broker who drifts from the market loses credibility on the panel. Where trades are thin and every deal is a bit different, a well-run consensus of opinions can be more useful than a list of actual prices.

**Meanwhile:**
**LIBOR had a defense against a lying bank, and it was useless against a few banks shading their numbers slightly in the same direction.**

To set USD LIBOR, 16 or so banks each submitted the rate at which they thought they could borrow. The administrator threw out the top and bottom quarters and averaged the middle. That trimmed mean handles a single wild outlier well. The traders who rigged it, most famously Tom Hayes at UBS and later Citi, didn't send wild numbers. They asked contacts at several banks and brokers for a basis point or two in their favour. Small, matching nudges land inside the trimmed middle, so the filter never saw them. Barclays settled in 2012 for about £290M across UK and US regulators, and UBS paid about $1.5B. LIBOR was then wound down: most rates stopped at the end of 2021, and USD LIBOR in June 2023.

The Baltic runs the same kind of panel, so it has the same weakness. Its defenses are structural rather than statistical. It has written rules for how each route must be assessed, it can question any submission, and it is now a regulated benchmark administrator. **Meanwhile:**
**Goodhart never said "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."** That line comes from the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, writing in 1997 about university audits.

**What Goodhart actually wrote:** in a 1975 paper on UK monetary policy, the Bank of England economist Charles Goodhart said: *"Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes."* The background was the Bank's effort to control broad money (£M3) in the early 1970s. In 1973 it brought in the "corset," which penalised banks whose interest-bearing deposits grew too fast. Banks found ways around it, most notably the "bill leak": lending moved into bank-accepted bills that never showed up as deposits. Measured money growth behaved and the actual credit kept growing anyway. The measure wasn't wrong. The link between money and spending broke because people changed their behaviour once the measure became the target. Robert Lucas published a more formal version of the same idea, the Lucas critique, in 1976.

**The twist:** the original is sharper than the popular version. A metric doesn't go bad on its own. It goes bad because someone is pushing on it. During a fine-tune, two things are pushing. The optimizer pushes on training loss, and you push on eval loss every time you use it to pick a checkpoint or tweak a hyperparameter. Eval loss at 0.64 in epoch 2 looks healthy and tells you the model hasn't started overfitting. But it's still token-level cross-entropy. The regularity you really care about is "lower loss means more correct invoice fields," and that can break quietly in epoch 3: loss keeps creeping down while field-level exact match flattens out. The model gets better at predicting the boilerplate around the fields rather than the fields themselves. Keep a held-out set you score on extraction accuracy and never tune against.
