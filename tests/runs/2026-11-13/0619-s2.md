A correction first: in the last options I called it a 1950s study. It wasn't. The work is from 1946 and 1973, and the famous finding about random positions turned out to be slightly wrong.

**The mechanism:** in 1946 Adriaan de Groot, a Dutch psychologist who was also a strong player, showed real game positions to players for a few seconds. Grandmasters rebuilt them almost perfectly, while weaker players got only a fraction right. In 1973 Chase and Simon repeated the test with a control: pieces placed at random on the board. With random positions, the master's edge seemed to disappear, and he did about as badly as the beginner. Their explanation was "chunks": masters see familiar groups of pieces, like a castled king or a fianchetto, as single units. Simon later estimated that a master has stored somewhere around 50,000 to 100,000 of these chunks. That figure is a rough model estimate, not a measurement. In 1996 Gobet and Simon found that masters still keep a small advantage even on random boards, because random boards still contain a few chunks by accident.

**The twist:** at 1+0 you have almost no time to calculate, so bullet mostly tests how many chunks you can recognize. The same effect shows up with programmers. A 1981 study by McKeithen and colleagues found that experienced programmers recall real code much better than novices do, and most of that edge goes away when the lines are scrambled. This also says something about reviewing the diff Claude is about to give you. Code written in patterns you know reads quickly. Code in unfamiliar patterns turns you back into a beginner, even when it's correct.

**Meanwhile:**
You don't blunder because you missed something. You blunder because you recognized something, and the position was one piece different from what you recognized.

**The mechanism:** this is called the Einstellung effect. Luchins first showed it in 1942 with water-jar puzzles: once people learned a method that worked, they kept using it even when a much shorter method was right in front of them. In 2008 Bilalić, McLeod and Gobet studied it in chess with eye-tracking. They gave experts positions that had a familiar mate (a smothered mate) and also a shorter, less familiar one. The experts said they were looking for alternatives, but their eyes kept going back to the squares of the familiar pattern. In that setup their effective strength dropped by roughly three standard deviations, which is about the level of much weaker players. I'm confident about the direction of that result and less sure of the exact size. In bullet, that recognition fires in about 200 milliseconds and you premove before anything checks it.

**The call-out:** your losses probably aren't spread randomly. My guess is they cluster in positions that look like a pattern you know but have one difference: a defender one square over, a pin that isn't really there, a back rank that has an escape square. The more you play, the more confident those wrong moves get. You can test this. Lichess lets you export your games, so tag your last 30 losses with "missed something" or "saw something that wasn't there". I'd bet the second group is bigger.
