**Meanwhile:**
Every CAN node keeps two error counters and can decide on its own to leave the network. No master tells it to go.

**How:** each controller keeps a Transmit Error Counter (TEC) and a Receive Error Counter (REC).
- A failed transmission adds **8** to the TEC. A successful one subtracts **1**. Receive errors mostly add 1 to the REC.
- While both counters stay at 127 or below, the node is **error-active**. It can send dominant error flags that destroy the frame on the wire for everyone.
- Once either counter passes 127, the node is **error-passive**. Its error flags are recessive, so they can no longer damage other nodes' traffic, and after each of its own transmissions it has to wait an extra 8 bit times.
- Once the TEC passes 255, the node goes **bus-off** and stops driving the bus completely. To come back it must see 128 runs of 11 recessive bits.

**Twist:** the 8:1 ratio sets a tipping point. If p is the fraction of a node's transmissions that fail, the TEC changes on average by 8p minus (1 minus p) per frame. That turns positive once p goes above **1/9**. A node that fails more than about 11% of its sends will drift toward bus-off even though most of its frames get through. The protocol assumes that the node seeing errors first is probably the faulty one. A node with a bad sample point therefore takes most of the blame, and if bus-off recovery is automatic it can drop off and rejoin repeatedly. From outside that looks like random frame loss, not a timing fault. While Claude is chasing it, it may be worth checking whether the counters or bus-off events are being logged and whether automatic recovery is turned on.

1) Deeper: sample points, and why two nodes with "correct" timing can still disagree about a bit

In 1983 a brand-new Air Canada 767 ran out of fuel at 41,000 feet. The arithmetic everyone did was correct. Only the units were wrong.

**How:** on 23 July 1983, Flight 143 was flying from Montreal toward Edmonton. The 767 was one of the first aircraft in the fleet to measure fuel in metric units, during Canada's switch to metric. The plane's fuel gauge processor was faulty, so the crew measured the tanks by hand with dipsticks and converted litres to mass. They used 1.77, which converts litres to *pounds*, instead of about 0.8, which converts litres to *kilograms*. The flight needed 22,300 kg of fuel. The figure they worked from was really in pounds, so the aircraft carried less than half the fuel it needed. Several people checked the calculation, and every check got the same wrong answer. Both engines stopped over Manitoba. Captain Bob Pearson happened to be an experienced glider pilot. First Officer Maurice Quintal remembered a former air force base at Gimli. They glided down, used a forward slip to lose height, and landed on a runway that was partly being used as a drag strip that day. The nose gear collapsed, and no one was seriously hurt.

**Twist:** checking the work more times didn't help, because each check reused the same wrong number. Repeating a calculation catches arithmetic mistakes, but not a wrong assumption fed into it. CAN timing is the same kind of problem: a bit-timing calculation can be done perfectly and still be wrong if the assumed peripheral clock isn't the clock the CAN block actually gets.
