**Option pricing was worked out five years before Einstein explained Brownian motion, and in 2020 the oil market had to go back to that 1900 version.**

In 1900, Louis Bachelier, a student of Poincaré, defended a thesis called *Théorie de la spéculation*. In it he modelled Paris bond prices as a random walk and priced options on them. That was five years before Einstein's 1905 Brownian motion paper used similar maths. Hardly anyone read it until economists found it again in the 1950s.

His model had a flaw that later work fixed. The price moves in fixed amounts rather than by a percentage, so nothing stops it from going below zero. Black-Scholes (1973) fixed this by using log prices, so the price can never be negative. For about 50 years that was treated as the correct answer.

Then on 20 April 2020, the May WTI crude contract settled at **−$37.63**. Storage at Cushing, Oklahoma, the delivery point, was close to full, and holders had to pay someone to take the oil. Black-Scholes can't represent a negative price at all, since it would need the log of a negative number. Around then, CME switched its pricing model for some energy options to **Bachelier's**.

**The twist:** the improvement over Bachelier worked by building in an assumption that negative prices were impossible. Bachelier's cruder model never assumed that. When the impossible case happened, the "wrong" model was the only one that could still produce a number. A model's assumptions decide which outcomes it can represent, and the safeguard you added to stop the weird case can be exactly what breaks when the weird case happens.

**In pure bf16, a fine-tuning update can round to exactly zero. The weight never changes, and no error tells you.**

The two 16-bit formats trade range against precision in opposite directions:

- **fp16:** 5 exponent bits and 10 mantissa bits. The largest value is 65,504, and gradients smaller than about 6e-8 become zero. That's why fp16 training needed loss scaling (Micikevicius et al., 2018): you multiply the loss up so small gradients stay above the underflow point.
- **bf16 (Google Brain's "brain float"):** 8 exponent bits, so it covers the same range as fp32 and doesn't need loss scaling. The cost is only 7 mantissa bits. Near 1.0, the gap between neighbouring values is 2⁻⁷, so any change smaller than about 0.4% of a weight's size gets rounded away.

Apply that to a fine-tune. With Adam, each step moves a weight by about the learning rate. At lr = 1e-5 and a typical weight of about 0.02, that's a 0.05% change, almost 10x smaller than bf16's rounding gap. If the weights themselves are stored in bf16, `w + Δw == w`. The loss stays flat and every kernel reports success.

That's why FSDP's `MixedPrecision` separates `param_dtype` (what the GPU computes in) from the stored, sharded parameters the optimizer updates, which you want in fp32. The bf16 copies exist only for the forward and backward passes. `reduce_dtype` is a separate setting, and it decides whether the gradient sum across your 8 GPUs is done in bf16 too.

**The twist:** this is the Bachelier and Black-Scholes story again. fp16 can't represent tiny gradients, and bf16 can't represent tiny changes to a weight. Each format has a case it can't represent, and when that case comes up it doesn't crash. It rounds to zero. A fine-tune at low learning rate is exactly where bf16's blind spot sits.

A 30-second check for your run: log `(w_after - w_before).norm() / w.norm()` for one layer over a few steps. If it reads exactly 0.0, your updates are being rounded away.

1. **Deeper:** stochastic rounding and Kahan summation, two tricks that keep updates smaller than the rounding gap from vanishing.
2. **Sideways:** commodity futures prices only move in fixed minimum ticks, and the tick size changes what strategies can make money.
3. **Wildcard:** the Patriot missile in 1991, where a 0.000000095-second rounding error per clock tick added up to a miss.

**Making a market's minimum price step bigger can make trading worse. The step size decides whether traders compete on price or on speed.**

Every futures contract has a tick, the smallest step its price can move. For CME crude oil (CL) it's $0.01 a barrel, which is $10 per 1,000-barrel contract. For the E-mini S&P it's 0.25 index points, or $12.50.

Here's the mechanism. When the tick is large relative to the price, the gap between buy and sell quotes usually sits at exactly one tick. You can't offer a better price than the next trader, because there's no smaller price step to quote. So everyone posts at the same price, and fills go to whoever joined the queue at that price first (FIFO). Your position in the queue becomes the thing worth paying for. That's the economics behind Spread Networks, which spent a reported ~$300M in 2010 on a straighter fibre route from Chicago to New York to cut milliseconds, and was then beaten by microwave links.

The SEC tested this. In the 2016–2018 Tick Size Pilot, ticks on about 1,200 small-cap stocks went from $0.01 to $0.05, on the theory that wider spreads would attract market makers and help small companies. In the studies I know of, spreads and trading costs went up, and liquidity didn't clearly improve. The programme ended without being extended.

The allocation rule changes the game too. Some interest-rate futures (Eurodollars were the classic case) split fills **pro-rata** by order size instead of first-come. There the race is about size, so traders post orders far larger than they actually want, knowing they'll only get a fraction filled.

**The twist:** the bf16 point from last time again. How finely a quantity is divided decides what can be expressed. In bf16, small updates get rounded to nothing. In a market with a coarse tick, small price improvements can't be quoted, so the competition moves to speed or size.

**The biggest corner of the silver market wasn't broken by sellers. The exchange changed its rules so buyers couldn't open new positions.**

In early 1979, silver traded around $6/oz. Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt, Texas oil heirs, had been building a huge long position in futures and physical metal. Estimates often put it at a large share of the world's deliverable silver, though the exact figure is disputed. Their key move was **taking delivery** instead of rolling contracts forward. Each time they took delivery, the shorts had to find real metal, and the bars the Hunts took went into vaults and stayed there. By January 1980, silver hit about $50.

The squeeze ended because the exchange stepped in. In January 1980, COMEX raised margins and limited position sizes. Then on 21 January it went **liquidation-only**: you could close positions but not open new ones. That stopped new buying, so the price could really only go down. The Hunts had borrowed heavily to keep buying. On 27 March 1980, "Silver Thursday," they missed a margin call of roughly $100M at their broker, Bache, and silver fell to about $10.80 that day. A bank consortium eventually extended about a $1.1B loan to prevent a wider default. Years later, a 1988 jury found the Hunts liable for conspiring to manipulate the market.

**The twist:** in commodities, the exchange is effectively a player in extreme moves, because it can change the rules when a position gets big enough to threaten the system. It has happened again since. In March 2022, the LME **cancelled** billions of dollars of nickel trades after the price spiked above $100,000/t in a single morning. If you hold an options or futures position that only pays off in an extreme squeeze, you also need to predict what the exchange will do when that squeeze arrives.
