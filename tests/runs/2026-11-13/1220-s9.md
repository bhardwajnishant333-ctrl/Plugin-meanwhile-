**A bombe never found the right key. It ruled out wrong ones very fast, and whatever was left over got checked by a person.**

**1. The crib.** A cryptanalyst would guess a piece of the plaintext, such as `WETTERVORHERSAGE` ("weather forecast") or a routine sign-off. Enigma could never encrypt a letter as itself. So you slid the crib along the ciphertext and threw out every position where a crib letter lined up with the same cipher letter. That usually left only a few places the crib could fit.

**2. The menu.** Each crib letter paired with its cipher letter, at a known position in the message, gives you one link, such as E↔T at position 4. Draw all the links and you get a graph of letters. The key part is **loops** (closed cycles). With a loop, a wrong guess about the plugboard runs back into itself and shows a contradiction. With no loops, a wrong guess just spreads out and never gets caught. A good menu had several loops.

**3. The run.** The usual figures for a standard British bombe: 108 drums, wired as 36 Enigmas working at once. For each wheel order it stepped through all 17,576 rotor positions in roughly a quarter of an hour. Treat that run time as approximate. At every position the electrics took one assumption, "letter A is plugged to letter X", and followed what it implied all the way around the menu.
- **Turing's insight:** if the assumption is wrong, the contradiction tends to make every other assumption look true as well. All 26 lights come on, which tells you it's wrong. So the machine tested all 26 possibilities at once and only stopped when the picture made sense.
- **Welchman's diagonal board** used the fact that plugboard pairs work both ways (if A is plugged to X, then X is plugged to A). That cut false stops a lot and meant weaker cribs could still be used.

**4. The stop.** When the drums halted, the operator, usually a Wren, wrote down the rotor positions and the plugboard pair the machine suggested. Most stops were false. Someone tested each one by hand on a **checking machine**, an Enigma copy. If the test decrypted to German, the key went to Hut 6 or Hut 8, where messages were decrypted on modified Typex machines.

**The twist:** the bombe proved things by contradiction, in hardware. It didn't need to know what the right answer looked like, only that a wrong one would contradict itself. The real skill was in building the menu. A crib with no loops gave you a fast machine that couldn't rule anything out.
