**The strongest material any animal makes is a limpet's teeth.**

Limpets graze by scraping algae off bare rock with a tongue covered in tiny teeth. Those teeth are packed with very thin fibres of goethite, a natural iron oxide. In 2015 a University of Portsmouth team led by Asa Barber measured them and found they were stronger than spider silk, which had held the record. The teeth also get worn down by the rock, so the limpet keeps growing new ones and moving them forward.

**Your celadon colour depends on the air in the kiln as much as on the glaze.**

A celadon only has a small amount of iron in it, usually 1 to 3 percent. How that iron behaves depends on how much oxygen it gets during the firing:
- **Lots of oxygen** (an electric kiln, or a gas kiln fired "clean"): the iron stays rusty and gives yellow, amber or brown.
- **Starved of oxygen** ("reduction", where a gas or wood kiln is choked so the flame goes smoky): the fire pulls oxygen out of the iron, and the iron melts into the glass as that soft blue-green.

So muddy brown usually means one of these:
1. **Not enough reduction.** In an electric kiln this is almost certain, since they fire with plenty of air.
2. **Air got back in while it cooled.** The surface can turn back toward brown.
3. **Too much iron, or a glaze on the thick side.** It slides toward tenmoku-style browns and blacks.
4. **Titanium sneaking in** from some clays or ingredients. Iron plus titanium tends to give a dull yellow-grey-green.

I can't tell which one it is without knowing your kiln. If you fire electric, though, that's the likely answer.

**The twist:** Song dynasty potters at Longquan in China made the most famous celadons this way about 900 years ago, in long wood-fired "dragon kilns" climbing up hillsides, and they made the colour by smothering the fire on purpose. So the jade green you're after comes from a struggling, smoky fire, not a clean one.
