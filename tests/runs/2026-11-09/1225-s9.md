**The thing that makes a bell ring well is the same thing that makes it crack.**

**The alloy.** Bell metal is roughly 77–80% copper and 20–23% tin. With that much tin, a hard, brittle intermetallic (the delta phase, roughly Cu₄₁Sn₁₁) forms between the copper-rich grains. The metal is very stiff and loses very little energy inside itself (high Q), but has poor fracture toughness. Engineering bronze with about 10% tin is much tougher, but sounds dull.

**The hammer.** Each blow puts a very high, very local contact stress on the soundbow. Big Ben strikes the hours, 78 blows twice a day, or 156 a day on the same spot. That's textbook fatigue.

**Big Ben's case.** The bell cracked in 1859, and the two sides blamed each other. Denison said the casting was porous; the Whitechapel founders said the hammer was far heavier than their specification. As I recall, about twice as heavy, but I'm not certain of the exact weights. The fix around 1863 turned the bell an eighth of a turn and fitted a lighter hammer.

**Twist:** with a bell you can't have both the ringing and the toughness, so the standard fix is to manage where the damage lands.

1. **Deeper:** how a cracked bell gets repaired today by welding, not recasting
2. **Sideways:** the Tacoma Narrows Bridge and other cases where low damping is the failure, not the feature
3. **Wildcard:** why the note you hear from a bell (the "strike note") isn't actually one of its frequencies
