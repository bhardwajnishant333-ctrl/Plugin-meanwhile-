**A mountain range that never existed stayed on maps of Africa for about 90 years.**

In 1798 James Rennell, the most respected geographer in Britain, drew the map for Mungo Park's account of his travels in West Africa. Park had seen some hills far off in the distance. Rennell also had a theory about the Niger: he thought it flowed east and dried up inland, so it needed a big barrier to keep it from reaching the coast. He drew the hills Park saw as one continuous east–west range and called it the Mountains of Kong.

Other mapmakers copied Rennell, because copying the best source was how the trade worked. The range spread through the atlases of the 1800s and kept growing taller and longer as it went. In fact, by about 1830 people knew the Niger reaches the sea, which removed the reason the mountains were drawn in the first place, but the range stayed on maps anyway. It only came off after the French explorer Louis-Gustave Binger reached Kong in the late 1880s and found no mountains at all.

**The twist:** the error didn't last because nobody checked. It lasted because every copy agreed with every other copy, and that agreement looked like proof. Ninety years of atlases all came from one guess. Your cutover has the same trap. Logical replication copies whatever is in the source, mistakes included. It also leaves sequences and DDL behind in 14→17, so the new side can fall behind in ways replication won't show you. If the replica agrees with the primary, that tells you the copy is faithful, not that the data is right. The only real check is an independent one, like row counts or checksums run per table on both sides.

**43 seconds of network partition cost GitHub 24 hours and 11 minutes of degraded service.**

On 21 October 2018, during routine work to replace a failing optical link, GitHub's main US East Coast data center lost its connection to the rest of the network for 43 seconds. Their failover tool, Orchestrator, uses Raft consensus to decide when to fail over. The nodes it could still reach, on the West Coast and in East Coast public cloud, formed a quorum, saw the primary as down, and promoted West Coast MySQL primaries. Everything Orchestrator did matched its design.

Then the link came back, and they had two problems. First, the East Coast primaries had taken a few seconds of writes that never reached the West Coast. Those writes were now on the wrong side of a split. Second, the application servers were still in the East but sending every write across the country to the new West Coast primaries, and the added latency hurt performance badly. GitHub chose data integrity over getting back up fast. They restored from backup and replayed the gap carefully. Restoring several terabytes took most of a day.

**The twist:** a correct failover was the problem. Orchestrator could tell which nodes were healthy, but it knew nothing about where the application ran. Quorum answers "who is alive?" Nobody had told it to also ask "who should be primary, given where the traffic comes from?" Their fix included teaching Orchestrator that topology, so it no longer promotes across regions.

This applies directly to your cutover. CloudNativePG will promote a replica automatically inside a cluster. Ask your agent what in your setup can promote, fence, or redirect traffic on its own during the cutover window, and what it does if the source and target ever both accept writes.

1. **Deeper:** why "pick consistency over availability" took them 24 hours instead of 24 minutes, and why restore time is the number that really sets your recovery time (RTO)

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**Army ants can walk themselves to death in a circle, and every ant in it is following its rules correctly.**

Army ants are almost blind. Each one follows the pheromone trail left by the ant in front of it and adds its own scent on top. That rule works very well for a raid column of hundreds of thousands. But if a group gets cut off and the front of the column happens to curve back onto its own tail, the trail closes into a loop. Each ant follows the trail and makes it stronger, so the loop pulls in more ants.

The naturalist William Beebe described one in Guyana in his 1921 book *Edge of the Jungle*. As I remember it, the circle was about 1,200 feet around, each ant took about two and a half hours per lap, and the mill went on for about two days until most of the ants were dead. T.C. Schneirla studied these "circular mills" more closely in 1944. Earlier, Jean-Henri Fabre had shown the same thing with pine processionary caterpillars: he lined them up nose to tail around the rim of a large vase, and they circled it for about a week, with food a few inches away.

**The twist:** no ant is broken. The rule "follow the strongest trail" has no step that asks "am I getting anywhere?" Once the loop forms, the loop is what keeps itself going. Distributed systems researchers call this a **metastable failure** (Bronson et al., 2021). It starts with a trigger like a brief slowdown. Then retries, timeouts, and cache misses create enough load to keep the system down after the trigger is gone. Every client is behaving correctly, and the system stays down.

For your cutover: when connections move to PG17, every app reconnecting and retrying at the same moment is exactly this kind of loop. It's worth knowing whether your pools back off with jitter, so a short blip doesn't turn into a mill.

1. **Deeper:** metastable failures in detail, including why "just add capacity" often fails to break the loop and what does work (load shedding, retry budgets)
2. **Sideways:** why mapmakers put fake streets and towns on their maps, and how the fake town of Agloe, New York ended up with a real store
3. **Wildcard:** why a hard drive's "1 TB" and your OS's "931 GB" are both honest numbers

**Sharpest idea of the session:** when copies agree, that proves they copied faithfully, not that they're correct. Ninety years of Kong atlases, a GitHub failover that did exactly what it was built to do, and ants circling a perfect trail all show the same thing: the system did what it was told, and the mistake was in what it was told.

**How it applies right now:** matching row counts are one check on the copy, but logical replication in PG14–17 doesn't copy sequence values. Unless your agent has already synced them, the sequences on PG17 may still be at their starting values, and the first inserts will fail with duplicate-key errors. Run a quick check on each table: compare `max(id)` with `last_value` from `pg_sequences`.
