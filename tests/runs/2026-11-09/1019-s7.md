The friction law in every physics textbook says contact area doesn't matter. Your climbing shoes break it.

Guillaume Amontons published the rule in 1699, and Leonardo had sketched it about two centuries earlier: friction depends on how hard you press, not on how big the contact patch is. Bowden and Tabor at Cambridge explained why in their 1950 book *The Friction and Lubrication of Solids*. Two hard surfaces only touch at the tips of microscopic bumps. That real contact area is a tiny fraction of the apparent area, and it grows with load as the bumps squash. So pressing harder and having a bigger patch come to the same thing, and area drops out of the equation.

Rubber doesn't work that way. Soft rubber drapes down into the rock's texture, and much of its grip is energy lost as the rubber deforms over each grain. Physicists call this hysteresis. K.A. Grosch showed this in 1963 (Proc. Royal Society A): rubber friction rises and falls with sliding speed and temperature, following the same curve as the rubber's own stretchiness. So on a smear, how much rubber is touching, how warm it is, and how fast it creeps all matter in ways Amontons said they shouldn't.

The twist: hysteresis grip needs bumps for the rubber to flow over. Thousands of ascents polish off the fine-scale grit and leave nothing for the rubber to deform around. That is why classic routes get "polished" and slick, even though the rock looks the same and the friction law in the textbook says nothing should have changed.

Rubber doesn't have a friction coefficient. It has a friction curve, and where you sit on it depends on speed and temperature traded off against each other.

Grosch measured rubber sliding on glass and on silicon-carbide paper over a range of speeds and temperatures. When he plotted the results, curves taken at different temperatures fell onto one "master curve" once he shifted them by the Williams–Landel–Ferry (WLF) factor. That is the same shift polymer chemists use for rubber's bounciness. The mechanism: each bump in the surface flexes the rubber as it slides past, at a frequency of roughly sliding speed divided by bump spacing. Friction peaks when that frequency matches the frequency where the rubber loses the most energy per flex. On smooth glass the peak came at a much lower speed than on the rough paper. Grosch read that as two mechanisms: molecular adhesion on glass and bump-flexing hysteresis on the abrasive. I'm confident about that two-peak result, but I don't trust my memory of his exact peak speeds, so I won't quote numbers.

Bo Persson extended this in 2001 (J. Chem. Phys.): real rock and asphalt are rough at every scale, from millimetres down to nanometres. Each scale flexes the rubber at its own frequency, so grip is a sum over the whole roughness spectrum.

The twist: by WLF, cooling rubber has the same effect as sliding it faster. A smear is close to static, which puts the rubber at very low flex frequencies, far from the lossy peak. Cold conditions push it back toward that peak. That suggests "send temps" may help the rubber as well as reducing sweat on your skin. That last part is my inference from the physics, not a measured climbing result. Skin and sweat are the well-documented reason cold days feel grippier.
