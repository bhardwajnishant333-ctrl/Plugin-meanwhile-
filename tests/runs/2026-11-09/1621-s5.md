**Meanwhile:** Nobody has a settled answer for how 13th-century sailors drew the Mediterranean as accurately as they did.

The mechanism: the Carta Pisana, dated to about 1275–1300 and held at the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris, is the oldest portolan chart that survives. It has no latitude or longitude. Instead it is covered in rhumb lines, straight compass bearings that fan out from wind roses. Even so, its coastlines line up closely with modern maps. The standard explanation is that sailors stitched them together from thousands of compass bearings and estimated distances. Roel Nicolai's 2014 PhD at Utrecht argued that the geometry is too consistent for medieval methods and suggested an older, unknown source. Most historians of cartography reject that view, and the debate is still open. One detail almost everyone agrees on is that the whole Mediterranean is rotated counterclockwise, usually given as about 9–11 degrees. The common explanation is magnetic declination: the sailors' bearings were taken from magnetic north, not true north. That explanation is widely accepted but still argued over.

The twist: the charts are accurate in each local stretch and wrong in a consistent way overall. Combine enough correct local measurements that all share one small bias, and you get a map that looks right but is turned as a whole. A generator that places rooms relative to their neighbours behaves the same way. If each placement has a small bias, the dungeon drifts overall, and checking adjacent rooms won't show it. A check on the global bounds will.

1) Deeper: the declination argument, and why the rotation is different in the eastern and western Mediterranean

**Meanwhile:** Wave function collapse has nothing to do with quantum physics. It's a classic constraint solver with an evocative name.

How it works: Maxim Gumin released it on GitHub in 2016, building on Paul Merrell's "model synthesis" work from around 2007. Every cell in the grid starts out allowing every tile. You pick the cell with the fewest remaining options (the "lowest entropy" one), fix it to one tile, and then propagate. Each neighbour drops any tile that can't sit next to the new one, those neighbours' neighbours do the same, and so on. That propagation step is arc consistency, the same idea as AC-3 from a constraint-satisfaction course. Sometimes a cell ends up with no valid options left. Then you restart or backtrack. Shipped games using WFC or close variants include Caves of Qud, and Oskar Stålberg's Bad North and Townscaper.

The twist: WFC only guarantees that every pair of neighbouring tiles is legal. It promises nothing about the whole map. You can get a dungeon where every corridor joins up properly and the exit still can't be reached. That's the portolan problem in algorithm form: accurate locally, with nothing checking the whole. In practice people add global structure first, such as a pre-seeded critical path or a reachability check after generation, and then let WFC fill in the rest. If the agent is rewriting your generator, ask it what guarantees the exit can be reached.

1) Deeper: why choosing lowest entropy first cuts contradictions, and how backtracking compares with restarting
2) Sideways: Stålberg's trick of running WFC on an irregular quad grid so Townscaper's towns don't look tiled
3) Wildcard: the 2023 "hat" tile, a single shape that tiles the plane but never repeats, found by an amateur, David Smith

**Meanwhile:** Throwing the whole map away and starting again is often faster than carefully undoing the last mistake.

How it works: choosing the lowest-entropy cell first is an old idea under a new name. Constraint-satisfaction researchers call it "fail-first". Haralick and Elliott formalised it in 1980, in Artificial Intelligence ("Increasing tree search efficiency for constraint satisfaction problems"). I believe Golomb and Baumert suggested something similar in their 1965 "Backtrack programming" paper, but I'm less sure of that. The logic is that the cell with the fewest options is the one most likely to fail. If it's going to fail, you want that to happen before you've built the rest of the map on top of it. Fixing it also removes the most options from its neighbours. Gumin's version weights the options by how often each tile appears and adds a little random noise to break ties. As far as I remember, his original code doesn't backtrack at all: if it hits a contradiction, it gives up and you run it again.

The twist: that isn't laziness. In 1998 Gomes, Selman and Kautz showed that randomized search often has heavy-tailed runtimes. Most runs finish fast, and a few get stuck exploring a hopeless branch for a very long time. Restarting with fresh randomness cuts off those long runs, and backtracking can't do that. For your Godot generator: cap the number of retries, take each retry's seed from your master RNG so a given dungeon seed always reproduces, and log how many retries each map needed. If that number starts climbing, your tile rules have become too tight. That warning shows up well before the generator starts failing outright.

1) Deeper: Luby restarts, a provably near-optimal schedule for when to give up
2) Sideways: why DOOM's RNG was a fixed 256-entry table, and what that meant for demo replays
