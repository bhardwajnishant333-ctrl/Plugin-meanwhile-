## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**The mold that makes your miso is a tamed version of one of the most dangerous food molds on Earth.**

*Aspergillus oryzae* (koji) is generally treated as a domesticated form of *Aspergillus flavus*. *A. flavus* is the mold that makes aflatoxin, one of the strongest natural liver carcinogens known, and it contaminates peanut and corn crops around the world. Genetically the two are very close. The difference is that in koji strains the aflatoxin genes are broken: in many strains parts of the gene cluster are deleted, and in others the genes carry mutations or are switched off.

No one engineered that. For centuries, Japanese brewers selected koji the only way they could, by keeping cultures that made good sake and miso and throwing out bad batches. By the Muromachi period, Kyoto had a licensed guild that sold koji spores. That was a commercial starter-culture business hundreds of years before anyone knew what a fungus was. In 2006 the Brewing Society of Japan named *A. oryzae* the "national fungus."

**The twist:** the brewers were running a very long selection experiment that judged the output only, not the mechanism. It worked because a toxic or bad batch tended to be noticed and dropped, so the safe strains were the ones that kept getting passed on. This is the dog-and-wolf story again, done by people who never saw a cell.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**Chili heat doesn't work on birds, and that may be the point.**

Capsaicin acts on TRPV1, the pain-and-heat receptor in your nerves. Normally TRPV1 fires above about 43°C. Capsaicin makes it fire at body temperature, so your brain reports a burn when nothing is hot. David Julius's lab cloned this receptor in 1997, and it earned him a share of the 2021 Nobel Prize. In 2002 his group (Jordt & Julius) showed that the bird version of TRPV1 still responds to heat but barely responds to capsaicin.

In 2001, Tewksbury and Nabhan (in *Nature*) worked with wild chiltepin peppers in Arizona. Curve-billed thrashers ate the fruit readily. Packrats and cactus mice avoided it. The useful detail was in the guts: seeds that passed through rodents mostly didn't germinate, and seeds that passed through birds did. The birds also carry seeds much farther. The authors called this "directed deterrence": the fruit is spicy for the animals that destroy its seeds and mild for the ones that spread them.

**The twist:** dispersal may not be the main reason capsaicin evolved. In 2008, Tewksbury's team found that across wild Bolivian *Capsicum chacoense* populations, pungency rose where a *Fusarium* fungus attacked seeds more often (it gets in through insect feeding wounds). Capsaicin held back that fungus. So the heat in your hot sauce may have started as a fungicide, and keeping mammals away came as a bonus. Later work reported that pungency has a cost in water-stressed sites, which may be why some wild populations stay mild. Bird-seed makers use the same blind spot: capsaicin-coated seed is sold to keep squirrels off feeders.
