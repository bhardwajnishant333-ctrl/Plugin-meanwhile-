**Meanwhile:** **In 1972 an airline bumped Ralph Nader from his flight. The Supreme Court case that followed is a big reason airline waitlists work the way they do today.**

In April 1972 Nader had a confirmed seat on an Allegheny Airlines flight from Washington to Hartford. He was bumped because Allegheny had sold more tickets than the plane had seats. Airlines did this routinely and didn't tell customers. He missed a rally he was due to speak at, and he sued. In *Nader v. Allegheny Airlines* (1976), the Supreme Court let his claim go forward on the grounds that the airline hadn't disclosed its overbooking.

The fix had been on paper since 1968. The economist Julian Simon proposed that when a flight is oversold, the airline should auction off the problem: offer money, raise the offer until enough people volunteer to give up their seats, and pay them. Airlines laughed at it. Then Alfred Kahn's Civil Aeronautics Board made voluntary bumping the rule in the late 1970s. I'm fairly sure it was 1978, but I'm not certain of the exact year. Today's "we're offering $400 to anyone willing to take a later flight" is Simon's auction.

**The twist:** overbooking and waitlists are the same bet. The difference is who carries the risk. Airlines overbook because across thousands of passengers, the no-show rate is very predictable. A waitlist makes the same bet but puts the risk on the person waiting instead of the company. What Simon did was put a price on that uncertainty, so nobody had to lose out against their will.

**Meanwhile:** **The language your agent is writing in is named after a Danish engineer who showed that phone calls arrive at random, the same way radioactive atoms decay.**

Agner Krarup Erlang (1878–1929) worked for the Copenhagen Telephone Company. In 1909 he published "The Theory of Probabilities and Telephone Conversations." It showed that incoming calls follow a Poisson distribution. Each caller decides on their own, yet the total traffic is predictable. In 1917 he published two formulas that every telephone company used for most of the next century:

- **Erlang B:** if every line is busy, the call is dropped. It tells you how many circuits you need to keep the drop rate at, say, 1%.
- **Erlang C:** if every line is busy, the caller waits in line. It tells you how long they'll wait.

The unit of traffic is still called the erlang: one line in use for a full hour. In 1986–87 Joe Armstrong, Robert Virding and Mike Williams at Ericsson named their language after him. Armstrong said they were happy the name could also be read as "Ericsson Language."

**The twist:** in a model where callers wait, wait times don't rise steadily as the system gets busier. They spike. In the simplest single-server queue, the average wait at 90% busy is 9 times the service time, and at 95% it's 19 times. Nearly all the pain sits in the last few percent. Your waitlist feature turns "slot full, booking rejected" (Erlang B) into "slot full, you wait" (closer to Erlang C). The fit isn't exact, because gym slots are fixed blocks of time and climbers don't arrive like a stream of calls. The lesson still holds: on peak evenings, when the gym is almost full, waitlists will get long out of proportion to how busy it is.
