**Your bees are electrically charged. To the next bee, a flower they just visited reads as "already taken."**

Flying bees pick up a positive charge from air friction, often tens to hundreds of picocoulombs. Flowers rooted in the ground carry a slight negative charge. When a bee lands, some charge moves across and changes the flower's electric field for a short while, roughly a minute or two (I'm not sure of the exact figure). In 2013 Daniel Robert's group at Bristol published in *Science* that bumblebees can detect those fields and learn from them. They tell a recently visited, already emptied flower apart from a fresh one, and they do it before landing. A 2016 paper in *PNAS* from the same group found the sensor is the bee's body hair, which bends in the field. Honeybees seem to sense fields through their antennae as well.

**The twist:** a whole swarm adds up to a measurable charge. In 2022 (*iScience*), the same group measured honeybee swarms passing a field monitor and saw the atmospheric potential gradient rise by about 100 V/m. They estimated a dense swarm could reach around 1000 V/m. So a swarm is an electrical event in the air, not only a weight drop on your scale. Whether a cheap electric-field sensor by a hive could spot a swarm before your load cells do is an open question, as far as I know. Nobody has shown it on real hives.

The research measures smaller things. Researchers film a sample of dances, decode the distance and direction of each one, and work backwards from there. The best-known case is Visscher and Seeley's 1982 study of a colony in a New York forest. Going from memory, most of its foraging was within a few kilometres, but some bees flew much further, possibly beyond 10 km. Check the paper before you quote any figure from it.
