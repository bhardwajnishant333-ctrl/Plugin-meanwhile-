**In 2005, two amateur chess players beat grandmasters at a tournament where everyone was allowed to use computers.**

The event was the PAL/CSS Freestyle tournament on Playchess.com. Any mix of humans and engines could enter, and grandmasters came with strong hardware. The winner was "ZackS": Steven Cramton and Zackary Stephen from New Hampshire. Kasparov later gave their ratings as about 1685 and 1398, which is club level. They ran several ordinary PCs with different engines at once. Their edge was method: they knew where each engine was weak, checked candidate moves against one another, and spent their own thinking only where the engines disagreed. Kasparov wrote it up in 2010 ("The Chess Master and the Computer", *NYRB*) as: *weak human + machine + better process* beat a strong computer alone, and also beat *strong human + machine + inferior process*.

**The twist:** once everyone has the same strong tool, skill at the task matters less than skill at managing the tool. Your correspondence games already work this way. Those 15 minutes of Playwright plus a prod build are part of your process too: what you check, when you step in, and what you leave alone are where your edge comes from.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**The shipping container wasn't the invention. The corner fitting was.**

On 26 April 1956, Malcom McLean's *Ideal X* sailed from Newark to Houston carrying 58 truck-trailer bodies on its deck. In the commonly cited figures from Marc Levinson's *The Box*, loading cost about $5.86 per ton by hand and about $0.16 per ton by container. Steel boxes had existed before that. What they lacked was a shared standard. Each line used its own sizes and its own fittings, so a box from one line couldn't ride another line's ship, crane or chassis. The real change came between the mid-1960s and 1970, when ISO standards fixed the 20 and 40 ft lengths and the corner castings with their twist-lock holes. As I recall, McLean released his corner-fitting patents royalty-free to help that happen, though I'm less sure of that detail. Once that small piece of hardware matched everywhere, any crane could lift any box onto any ship, train or truck.

**The twist:** the winners weren't the companies with the best boxes. They were the ports that rebuilt around the standard. Manhattan's and Brooklyn's piers, crowded with longshoremen, faded. Port Newark–Elizabeth, built for containers, took the trade. A shared interface makes whatever sits on both sides of it interchangeable. Your freight dashboard's API contract is the same kind of corner fitting.

1) **Deeper**: why freight rates fell so far that shipping stopped mattering in where factories were built
2) **Sideways**: the 1904 Baltimore fire. Fire companies from other cities arrived to help, but their hose couplings didn't fit Baltimore's hydrants.
3) **Wildcard**: the Bullwhip effect. A small change in shop sales becomes huge swings in factory orders, and it's how a beer game breaks MBA students.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**Customer demand changes once, by one step, and then holds steady. The players still crash the supply chain.**

That's MIT's *Beer Distribution Game*, which came out of Jay Forrester's system dynamics group in the 1960s. Four players form a chain: retailer, wholesaler, distributor, factory. Orders and shipments both take a few weeks to arrive. In the standard setup, customer demand goes from 4 cases a week to 8, early in the game, and stays at 8. John Sterman studied hundreds of teams (*Management Science*, 1989), and the result is nearly always the same. Stock runs out, panicked players order far more, then a flood of beer arrives and backlogs turn into huge piles of extra stock. The swings get bigger at every step up the chain, and the factory gets hit hardest. His explanation: players **ignore the supply line**. They forget the orders they've already placed that haven't arrived yet, so they order again.

The real world does the same thing. In 1997, Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang described how Procter & Gamble saw this with Pampers. Babies use diapers at a steady rate, yet distributors' orders to P&G swung wildly.

**The twist:** no single player acts irrationally. Each decision makes sense given what that person can see. The damage comes from the delay plus a pipeline they can't see. Make what's in transit visible and the swings shrink, even with the same people playing.

**For your dashboard:** if it shows freight on hand and freight ordered but hides **in transit / committed but not yet arrived**, it encourages exactly this over-ordering. Showing the supply line is a real feature idea, not a metaphor.

1) **Deeper**: Sterman's ordering formula, and why players "anchor" on how much stock they have
2) **Sideways**: Little's Law (L = λW). One equation connects queue length, throughput and wait time, for warehouses and for your test runner alike.
3) **Wildcard**: why the longest correspondence chess games outlast their players
