**Meanwhile:** **Your matching engine is solving a problem that a German mathematician solved before 1851, and most reconciliation code still gets it wrong.**

Matching invoices to bank lines is the *assignment problem*: pair two sets, one-to-one, so the total mismatch is as small as possible. Harold Kuhn published the standard method in 1955 and named it the "Hungarian method" after Kőnig and Egerváry, whose work it built on. Later it came out that Carl Jacobi had already solved the same problem in a paper published after his death in 1890. Jacobi died in 1851, so the method is well over 150 years old.

Most matchers are greedy instead: take each invoice in turn and grab the closest bank line within tolerance. Greedy fails in a quiet way:

- Invoices: **A = 500.00**, **B = 500.20**
- Bank lines: **X = 500.20**, **Y = 500.00**, tolerance ±1.00
- If greedy handles A first and checks X first, A takes X (off by 0.20). B is left with Y (off by 0.20).
- The right answer is A↔Y and B↔X, both exact.

Nothing fails. Both pairs pass the tolerance check, so the wrong match never shows up as an exception. It just gives you wrong totals per supplier.

**Why it matters:** order-dependent matching is a bug your tests won't catch unless they shuffle the input. When the agent finishes, run the matcher twice on the same data in reversed order. If the results differ, the engine is greedy. The fix is `scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment` on a cost matrix. It runs in O(n³), so split the data first, by supplier and date window, and keep each matrix small.

1) **Deeper:** how to build the cost matrix (amount difference, date gap, reference-text similarity), and how to handle one invoice paid in two bank transfers, which is no longer one-to-one
2) **Sideways:** Hungary 1998. Ross Brawn switched Schumacher to three stops mid-race and told him he had 19 laps to find 25 seconds
3) **Wildcard:** Galton, 1907. Almost 800 fairground guesses at an ox's weight, and the median landed within 1% of the true figure

**Meanwhile:** **About 30% of real-world invoice totals start with the digit 1. Only about 4.6% start with 9. If your suppliers' data doesn't look like that, someone may be gaming it.**

This is Benford's law. In 1881 the astronomer Simon Newcomb noticed that the early pages of shared logarithm books (the numbers starting with 1) were more worn than the later ones. Frank Benford, a physicist at GE, tested the pattern on about 20,000 numbers and published it in 1938. The share of numbers starting with digit *d* is log₁₀(1 + 1/*d*): 30.1% for 1, 17.6% for 2, down to 4.6% for 9. It holds for data that spans several orders of magnitude and grows by multiplying, which is what invoice amounts usually do. In the 1990s Mark Nigrini made it a standard forensic-accounting test, and auditors still run it.

**The useful part:** fraud rarely breaks the pattern at random. It piles up just below approval limits. If invoices over 10,000 need a second sign-off, splitting one large invoice into several leaves a bump in amounts like 9,4xx and 9,8xx. A first-two-digits test finds that bump where a check on single invoices never would.

**For your app:** you already have every supplier invoice in one table. One GROUP BY on the first two digits for each supplier, compared against the expected curve, gives you a "this supplier looks odd" flag almost for free. Two caveats: fixed-price items and subscriptions don't follow Benford, and you need a few hundred invoices per supplier before the test means anything.

1) **Deeper:** the actual test (Nigrini's mean-absolute-deviation thresholds) and the Django ORM query for it
2) **Sideways:** why the same law shows up in river lengths, populations and physical constants (scale invariance)

**Meanwhile:** **If you cut a string that holds a weight up, the weight can go *up*.**

Hang a weight from two springs connected by a short tight string, and add two loose "safety" strings on either side. Cut the short string and the weight rises. Joel Cohen and Paul Horowitz described this in *Nature* in 1991. It's the physical version of **Braess's paradox** (Dietrich Braess, 1968): adding a road to a network can make everyone's trip slower.

How it works: each driver picks the fastest route for themselves. A new shortcut pulls everyone onto the same congested links, and once everyone is there, nobody gains by switching back. That's a Nash equilibrium, and it can be worse for everyone than the network without the shortcut. Cutting the string is like closing the road: the load spreads out again.

Cities have seen it happen. Seoul tore down the Cheonggyecheon elevated highway between 2003 and 2005 and turned the site into a stream and park. Downtown traffic was widely reported to get no worse, and in places it improved.

**Why it matters:** when everyone acts in their own interest, extra capacity can hurt the whole system. This applies to anything where users choose their own route, such as roads, network packets, and load balancers.

1) **Deeper:** the "price of anarchy". Roughgarden and Tardos proved that with simple linear congestion, selfish routing is never more than 4/3 worse than the best possible routing
2) **Sideways:** does hot water really freeze faster than cold? (the Mpemba effect, still argued about 60 years later)
3) **Wildcard:** F1. When a slower tyre wins the race, and the one-stop vs two-stop maths

**Meanwhile:** **The worst case is the simplest possible network: two roads.**

This is **Pigou's example**, from 1920. One unit of traffic goes from A to B.
- Road 1: always takes 1 hour, however busy it is.
- Road 2: takes *x* hours, where *x* is the share of traffic using it.

Each driver thinks: "Road 2 is never slower than 1 hour, so I'll take it." Everyone ends up on road 2, and the average trip is **1 hour**.

A planner would send half the drivers each way. The road-2 half takes 0.5 hours, the road-1 half takes 1 hour, so the average is **0.75 hours**.

1 ÷ 0.75 = **4/3**. That ratio between the selfish result and the best possible result is the **price of anarchy**. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou introduced the idea in 1999, and Papadimitriou gave it the name.

What Roughgarden and Tardos proved ("How Bad Is Selfish Routing?", 2002), and what Roughgarden extended in 2003:
- With linear delays, selfish routing is **never worse than 4/3** of the best routing.
- The worst-case ratio **doesn't depend on the network's shape**. A network with a million links can't do worse than Pigou's two roads. Only the shape of the delay curve matters.
- Steeper delay curves cost more. Traffic engineers often model congestion with a fourth-power curve, and there the worst case is roughly **2.15×**.

**The twist:** Roughgarden and Tardos also showed that, for *any* delay curves, selfish routing costs no more than the best routing would with **twice the traffic**. So doubling capacity always cancels the cost of selfishness. Getting everyone to cooperate does no better than that. Pigou's own fix was a **toll**: charge each driver the delay they add for everyone else, and the selfish choice becomes the best choice for the whole system.

1) **Deeper:** congestion pricing in real cities. London (2003) and Stockholm (2006), where the toll ran as a trial, a referendum followed, and support rose once people had lived with it
2) **Sideways:** the Mpemba effect. Does hot water really freeze faster?
3) **Wildcard:** F1. Is a team's two-car strategy a small game of selfish routing on the same track?
