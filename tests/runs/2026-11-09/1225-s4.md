**Meanwhile:**
**A gas peaker with poor efficiency can be worth more than its expected cash flows say. The reason is volatility, not better operations.**

The mechanism is simple. In each hour the plant can choose to run, and it runs only if power pays more than the fuel and running costs. So each hour's payoff is:

`max(P_power − HR × P_gas − VOM, 0)`

That is a call option on the spark spread. HR is the heat rate (MMBtu of gas per MWh). It is roughly 6.5–7 for a modern combined-cycle plant and around 10+ for an older peaker. VOM is the variable operating cost. The plant as a whole is a strip of thousands of these calls, one for every hour of its life. Deng, Johnson and Sogomonian made this formal in 2001 (*Decision Support Systems*). Tseng and Barz (2002, *Operations Research*) then added real-world constraints: start-up costs, minimum run times and ramp limits. Those constraints mean the pure strip of calls overstates the value.

**The twist:** a high-heat-rate peaker is a deep out-of-the-money option. Out-of-the-money options gain the most, in percentage terms, when volatility rises. So a discounted cash flow model built on *expected* spreads undervalues the plant, because of Jensen's inequality. What the plant earns comes from a small number of extreme hours. Two more points follow:
- The value also depends on the correlation between power and gas prices. If power simply tracks gas, the spread barely moves and the option is worth less.
- This ties directly to your training run. A transformer trained on MSE learns the conditional mean, and the conditional mean smooths out peaks. Your MAPE can look excellent while the model systematically shrinks the hours that hold the plant's value. If the forecast feeds dispatch or trading, quantile or tail-weighted losses matter more than the headline error.

I'm confident about the payoff structure and the two papers. The heat-rate figures are typical ranges, not exact specs.

**Meanwhile:**
**Texas designed its power market so that generators earn their keep in a few dozen terrible hours a year. Then in February 2021 those hours arrived, and many of the generators couldn't run.**

**How it works:** ERCOT is mostly islanded, with limited connections to other grids. That keeps its wholesale market largely outside FERC, under the Texas regulator (PUCT). Most US markets also pay generators for promised availability through capacity markets, like PJM's. ERCOT doesn't. Generators are paid for the energy they deliver plus ancillary services, and nothing else. Scarcity prices are supposed to fund new plants. The PUCT raised the offer cap step by step from $3,000 to $9,000/MWh, reaching $9,000 in 2015. It also added the ORDC in 2014, which adds a price markup automatically as reserves run low. The goal is to fix "missing money": any cap below the value of lost load leaves peakers unable to recover their fixed costs.

During Winter Storm Uri, the PUCT ordered prices set at the $9,000 cap. The independent market monitor, Potomac Economics, later estimated that keeping prices at the cap for about 32 hours *after* rotating outages had ended overcharged the market by roughly $16B. Brazos Electric filed for bankruptcy over an ERCOT bill of about $1.8B. Afterwards the cap was cut to $5,000.

**The twist:** in an energy-only market, a generator is effectively long the call options from last round, and payment depends on actually delivering power. Uri was a textbook case of wrong-way risk, where an option fails exactly when it pays most. Freeze-offs at gas wellheads cut fuel supply in the same hours the spark spread exploded. Generators with no fuel couldn't produce, and many had sold power ahead that they still had to supply at $9,000. A price model that treats spikes in power price and the plant's ability to run as independent will misprice the tail it's built on.

For your forecasting work: at the extremes, load and supply are correlated through the same weather. A load model that's right about the freeze still tells you nothing about how much generation will be available.
