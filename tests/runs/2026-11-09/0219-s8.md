**A group of sea nomads has evolved a bigger oxygen tank, and it's their spleen.**

**How it works:** The Bajau of Southeast Asia have lived on boats for generations and hunt by breath-hold diving. Accounts say they spend hours a day underwater, though the numbers quoted vary a lot. In 2018 Melissa Ilardo and colleagues published a study in *Cell*. They used portable ultrasound and found Bajau spleens were about 50% larger than those of the Saluan, a neighbouring people who live on land. The spleen holds a reserve of red blood cells. During the dive reflex it contracts and pushes those cells into the bloodstream, which acts like a small transfusion of oxygen mid-dive. A larger spleen means a larger transfusion. The genome scan pointed to a variant near the gene *PDE10A*. That gene is linked to thyroid hormone levels, which in mice affect spleen size.

**The twist:** Bajau who did not dive also had large spleens. So this isn't a training effect. It's natural selection, one of the clearest cases of a human adaptation to diving. The part that applies to you is that spleen contraction also happens in ordinary trained freedivers, just to a smaller degree. Erika Schagatay's research found it is one reason a series of dives gets easier: the spleen releases its stored cells over the first few dives, and the effect fades over minutes once you stop. Your warm-up dives may be partly a spleen warm-up.
