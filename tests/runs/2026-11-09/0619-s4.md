**Meanwhile:** **Whisper sometimes writes whole sentences that nobody said, and it does it most when nobody is talking.**

In "Careless Whisper" (Koenecke et al., FAccT 2024), the researchers ran the OpenAI Whisper API over thousands of clips. In about 1% of transcriptions, Whisper added phrases or whole sentences that weren't in the audio. Around 38% of those additions were harmful in some way: invented violence, made-up names and relationships, fake authority like "thanks for watching." The rate was higher for speakers with aphasia. The authors linked this to their longer silent stretches.

Why it happens: Whisper's decoder is a language model that listens to the audio. When the audio gives it little to go on, the language model's own guesses take over, and those guesses were learned from web subtitles. That's the usual explanation for the famous made-up subtitle credits, like the "Amara.org community" line, appearing over silence. **Meanwhile:** **A sharp bettor can lose money for a whole season and still be sure they're good. The proof is in the price they got, not the result.**

Here's the variance problem. At standard -110 odds you need 52.4% to break even. Say your true win rate is 55%, which is an excellent bettor. Over 500 bets the standard deviation of your win rate is about 2.2 points, so 55% sits only ~1.2 SD above break-even. That gives you roughly a 12% chance of losing money over those 500 bets, even with a real edge. Results alone take thousands of bets to prove anything.

Closing line value (CLV) gets around that. Compare the odds you got with the final odds just before the game starts, ideally at a sharp book like Pinnacle. By then the market has absorbed injury news, sharp money and everything else, so the closing line is about the best estimate of the true probability there is. If you bet a team at +150 and it closes at +130, you bought something for less than the market ended up valuing it, whatever happens in the game. Every bet gives you a continuous, low-noise measurement instead of a noisy win or loss. A few hundred bets of consistent positive CLV says more than a season of P&L.

**The twist:** this is why books limit winners. It's widely reported by bettors and people in the industry that books flag accounts that keep beating the close, often while those accounts are still down money. The book is trusting the low-variance metric over the noisy one. You do the same thing every time you watch validation loss instead of a handful of sample transcripts. Win/loss is accuracy, and CLV is log-loss against a strong reference model. It also points to a check on your ASR run: per-utterance loss against a strong baseline on the *same* clips tells you more, sooner, than WER on a small eval set.

1) **Deeper:** why the closing line is efficient, and the documented cases where it isn't (low-limit markets, player props)
2) **Sideways:** paired evaluation in ML. Why comparing two models on the same examples needs far fewer samples than comparing their averages
3) **Wildcard:** the Kelly criterion's dark side. Why betting full Kelly on a slightly overestimated edge can wipe you out
