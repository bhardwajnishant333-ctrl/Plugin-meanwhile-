## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
**CAN's speed limit comes from the speed of light. CAN FD only gets around it because it stops needing agreement partway through each frame.**

How it works: CAN arbitration is a bitwise vote. Every node drives its ID, and a dominant 0 overwrites a recessive 1. Each transmitter must see the bus settle to the same value as every other node within a single bit time, so a bit has to reach the far end and the result come back before the sample point. Signal travels at about 5 ns per metre in twisted pair, plus transceiver and controller delays at both ends. Hence the rules of thumb: about 40 m at 1 Mbit/s, about 500 m at 125 kbit/s. Classic CAN carries the whole frame at the arbitration rate.

CAN FD (Bosch, 2012) noticed that once arbitration is over, only one node is transmitting, so the round trip stops limiting the speed. After the BRS bit the controller switches to a faster data-phase rate (2, 5, often 8 Mbit/s), dropping back for the ACK, the one place where everyone talks at once again.

The twist: coordination is limited by latency and transfer by bandwidth, so you separate the two and give each its own clock. The catch: at high data-phase rates the transmitter's own transceiver loop delay can exceed a bit, so FD adds a second, delayed sample point just for the transmitter. On a bus a couple of metres long, like an e-bike's, cable length isn't your limit — transceiver loop delay and sample-point settings are.

1) **Deeper**: transmitter delay compensation, and why a bad TDC value fails only on the transmitting node
2) **Sideways**: the 2013 Toyota unintended-acceleration trial, where the expert testimony was about firmware (stack use, task death, watchdog design)
3) **Wildcard**: fall factor, and why a short fall close to the anchor can load a climber harder than a long fall higher up

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**A 2 m fall can hit harder than a 20 m fall. Fall height doesn't set the force, a ratio does.**

How it works: fall factor = fall distance ÷ rope paid out. A rope is a spring whose stiffness falls as length rises (k = EA/L), so lengths cancel: F = mg + √((mg)² + 2·mg·EA·(h/L)). A 20 m fall on 40 m of rope is FF 0.5. Fall 2 m past the belay with no gear clipped on 1 m of rope: FF 2, the worst case in normal climbing.

The UIAA 101 test is built on this: an 80 kg mass dropped at about FF 1.77; a single rope must keep first-fall impact force under 12 kN and survive at least 5 drops.

The twist: take the stretch away and everything changes. Dyneema slings barely stretch; DMM's widely shared drop tests broke short Dyneema slings at FF 1–2 with 80 kg. On via ferratas fall factors go well past 2, hence tear-webbing absorbers. What matters is how much give the thing catching you has. For bouldering, a crash pad does the same job: it adds stopping distance.

1) **Deeper**: rope drag — friction through quickdraws means only part of the rope stretches, so the real fall factor is higher than on paper
2) **Sideways**: why climbing ropes are nylon and not Kevlar or Dyneema
3) **Wildcard**: the Gimli Glider (1983), a 767 that ran out of fuel at altitude because of a unit conversion

**The sharpest idea from this session:** CAN arbitration speed is capped by round-trip delay, because every node has to agree on every bit. CAN FD only goes faster once a single node holds the bus, so it splits "agree" from "send" and gives each its own clock.
