**If you win an auction, you probably overpaid. And the more people bid, the worse it gets.**

In 1971, three engineers at Atlantic Richfield (Capen, Clapp and Campbell) published "Competitive Bidding in High-Risk Situations." Oil companies had been winning offshore drilling leases in the Gulf of Mexico and then earning poor returns on them, over and over. Their explanation: nobody knows how much oil is under the seabed, so every bidder is guessing. The company with the highest guess wins. So the winner is, almost by definition, the company that overestimated the most. They called it the **winner's curse**.

Bazerman and Samuelson later showed it in a classroom. MBA students bid on jars of coins worth $8. The average bid was *under* $8, so the group as a whole guessed well. But the winning bids averaged about $10, a loss of roughly $2 per auction. (Those figures are from memory. The pattern is well established, the exact cents less so.)

**The twist, for your GenServer:** how the auction *ends* changes how people bid. Roth and Ockenfels (2002) compared eBay, which had a fixed end time, with Amazon Auctions, which extended the deadline whenever someone bid near the end. eBay had much more last-second bidding ("sniping"). Amazon had much less, because a late bid just restarted the clock. Whichever end-of-auction rule your agent is writing right now is a design choice that shapes how your users behave.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**Tulip mania probably never ruined anyone.**

The famous version, with Dutch families selling their homes for one bulb and then losing everything in 1637, comes mostly from Charles Mackay's 1841 book *Extraordinary Popular Delusions*. Mackay was working from moralizing pamphlets written right after the crash. In 2007 the historian Anne Goldgar published *Tulipmania*, based on the actual notary and court records. She couldn't find a single person she could confirm had gone bankrupt from tulips. Trading was mostly among fairly well-off merchants and craftsmen, done in tavern back rooms over drinks.

The prices really did spike and crash in early February 1637. The mechanism is the strange part: most of these trades were contracts for bulbs still in the ground, to be delivered in summer. When prices fell, buyers simply didn't pay. Towns then let them walk away for a small fraction of the agreed price. Haarlem's figure is often given as 3.5% (I'm fairly confident, not certain). Economist Earl Thompson argued that something similar was expected even before the crash: if buyers thought they could always back out cheaply, they weren't really buying bulbs. They were buying **options**. That makes the "insane" prices close to rational.

**The twist:** the best-known bubble in history may be partly a story people tell about bubbles. It was told by moralists, and it was repeated because it was a good story.

1) **Deeper:** how Thompson's options argument holds up, and who disagrees with it
2) **Sideways:** a famous Go game with a doctor watching who said he could tell which player was losing by the color of his ears

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**Can hot water freeze faster than cold? Physicists are still arguing, and the answer turned out to be "not in water, but yes elsewhere."**

In 1963 a Tanzanian schoolboy, Erasto Mpemba, noticed his hot ice-cream mix froze before his classmates' cooled mix. His teacher laughed at him. A visiting physicist, Denis Osborne, didn't, and they published together in 1969. Since then it's been called the Mpemba effect.

With water it's a mess. In 2016 Burridge and Linden redid it carefully and found the effect mostly disappears. Where you put the thermometer can create it or erase it.

The interesting part came later. In 2017 Lu and Raz showed mathematically that a system that starts hotter *can* reach equilibrium faster. In 2020 Kumar and Bechhoefer demonstrated it in the lab with a tiny bead held by a laser (*Nature*). In 2024 groups reported a quantum version using trapped ions.

**The twist:** temperature is one number, but a system's real state has many dimensions. A hotter start can sit on a shorter path to equilibrium than a cooler one. "How far from done" isn't the number you're watching.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
**Cooling isn't one process. It's several processes running at once, and only the slowest one decides when you're done.**

As a system relaxes toward equilibrium, its departure from equilibrium splits into **modes**, independent patterns that each fade away at their own exponential rate. The fast modes disappear almost immediately. After that, the time to finish depends almost entirely on **how much of the slowest mode you started with**.

Lu and Raz's insight (2017): that amount doesn't have to grow as the starting temperature rises. A hotter starting state can contain *less* of the slow mode than a warmer one. It looks farther away, but nearly all of its distance sits in modes that vanish quickly. At one special starting temperature, the slow mode's share drops to exactly zero. They called that the **strong Mpemba effect**: the system skips the slow process completely and finishes exponentially faster.

Kumar and Bechhoefer (2020) built this. They held a glass bead in water with a laser and shaped an uneven, two-sided energy landscape ("double well") using feedback. They prepared the bead at different effective "temperatures" and dropped it into a cold bath. The hotter starts did reach equilibrium first, and near the predicted special temperature they saw the strong version. It also works the other way round (the "inverse Mpemba effect"): a colder object can *heat up* faster than a warmer one.

**The twist:** "how far away am I?" is the wrong question. The right one is "how much of me is stuck in the slow mode?" Two starting points equally far away can have very different finishing times.

1) **Deeper:** why water specifically is so hard to settle (evaporation, dissolved gas, supercooling)
2) **Sideways:** the same slow-mode math is why some Markov chains "mix" suddenly instead of gradually (the cutoff phenomenon, shown for shuffling a deck of cards)
3) **Wildcard:** how Go players judge a position by "temperature", a concept from combinatorial game theory
