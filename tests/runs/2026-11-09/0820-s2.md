**Meanwhile:** **"Dynamic programming" was partly picked as a name to hide the maths from a government official who hated the word "research."**

In his 1984 autobiography, Richard Bellman says that in the early 1950s at RAND, Secretary of Defense Charles Wilson hated the word "research." So Bellman chose "programming" (in the planning sense) and "dynamic" (because, he said, nobody could use it as an insult). Historians have pointed out problems with the dates, so take the Wilson detail loosely. The idea under the name holds up fine: **solve each subproblem once, store the answer, and build bigger answers from stored ones.**

**Three places the same idea shows up:**

- **Chess tablebases** run it backward from checkmates. Every position is labelled once and never searched again.
- **Reverse-mode autodiff** (described by Seppo Linnainmaa in his 1970 master's thesis; neural nets call it backprop) starts at the single loss value and walks backward through the computation. It gets the gradient for *every* input at a cost of a small constant times one forward pass, whether there are 10 inputs or a billion.
- **TanStack Table v8**, the thing being written in your repo right now, runs it forward. The row model is a pipeline: core, filtered, sorted, grouped, expanded, paginated. Each stage is wrapped in an internal `memo` helper that recomputes only when its inputs change. Change the page, and filtering and sorting aren't redone.

**The twist:** which direction you go is the actual design decision. Go backward when there are many starting points and one goal: lots of inputs and one loss, lots of positions and one mate. Go forward when there's one source and many things depend on it: one dataset feeding many derived views. Your table refactor is the forward kind. That's why ordering the stages matters. Put filtering before sorting, and every sort runs on fewer rows.

3. **Wildcard:** the Kalah and Connect Four solutions. Games solved completely, and what "solved" actually means

**Meanwhile:** **It's been mathematically proven that the first player in Hex can always win, and nobody can tell you how to do it on a full-size board.**

**Three levels of "solved."** Victor Allis defined these in his 1994 PhD thesis:

- **Ultra-weak:** you know the result with perfect play, but not the strategy.
- **Weak:** you have a strategy that guarantees that result from the starting position.
- **Strong:** you know the right move from *every* position, the way a chess tablebase does for 7 pieces.

**Real cases:**

- **Connect Four** was weakly solved twice in October 1988, by James D. Allen and by Victor Allis, working separately. The first player wins by starting in the centre column.
- **Checkers** was weakly solved by Jonathan Schaeffer's team in 2007 (published in *Science*), after computations that started in 1989. Perfect play is a draw.
- **Kalah** variants were solved by computer around 2000, with the first player winning most board sizes. I'm less sure of which variants and who did it, so treat that as approximate.
- **Hex** is the strange one. John Nash used a "strategy-stealing" argument around 1949. [...] That's an ultra-weak solution: proof that a winning strategy exists, with no strategy. Computers have found actual strategies only for small boards (around 9x9 as of the 2010s, as far as I know).

**The twist, for your corner of chess:** strategy-stealing fails for chess, because chess has zugzwang: positions where having to move makes things worse. The trick assumes an extra move is never harmful, and chess breaks that. So nobody can even prove that White isn't losing with perfect play. Most people would bet on a draw, but there's no proof.
