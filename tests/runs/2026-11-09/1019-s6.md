**A migration that finishes in 1 ms can take your whole app down.**

`ALTER TABLE` needs Postgres's strongest lock, ACCESS EXCLUSIVE. While it waits for that lock, it sits in the lock queue. Postgres won't let newer requests that conflict with a waiting one jump ahead of it, and even a plain `SELECT` conflicts with ACCESS EXCLUSIVE. So say a 40-second report query is reading `bookings` when your migration starts. The `ALTER` waits for that query, and every new read and write on `bookings` waits behind the `ALTER`. The change itself takes a millisecond, but your API stalls for 40 seconds, and your connection pool fills up while it does.

That's why the "safe" metadata-only changes are the dangerous ones, like adding a nullable column or a column with a constant default (metadata-only since Postgres 11). Nobody watches them run. The usual fix is to give up fast and retry rather than wait. Put this at the top of the migration's `up`:

```elixir
execute "SET LOCAL lock_timeout = '3s'"
```

Ecto runs each migration in a transaction by default, so `SET LOCAL` only lasts for that migration.

**The twist:** how long a migration runs isn't the real risk. The risk is how long it waits for its lock, and that depends on whatever else is touching the table at that moment. A deploy is safe or unsafe depending on when it runs as well as what's in it. For a ferry app, that could mean the same migration that's harmless at 3 a.m. stalls bookings on a busy sailing morning.

**The "hot hand" fallacy was itself a fallacy.**

In 1985, Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky looked at basketball shooting and found players hit at about the same rate after a streak of makes as at any other time. "Hot hand" became the textbook example of people seeing patterns in noise. It stayed that way for 30 years.

Then in 2018, Miller and Sanjurjo pointed out a hidden bias in the method (*Econometrica*). Flip a fair coin 4 times. Find every flip that comes right after a heads, work out what share of those were heads, and average that share across many 4-flip runs. You don't get 50%. You get about **40.5%**. In a short run, a streak uses up heads, so fewer are left to follow it. The original study's measuring method was tilted against streaks. Correct for that, and the old shooting data does show a modest hot hand.

**The twist:** this bias shows up any time you work out "what happened after X" inside lots of short sequences and then average the percentages. Per-user conversion rates averaged across users do it. So does "rebooking rate after a cancellation" averaged per customer. The fix is to add up all the counts first and take one ratio at the end, rather than averaging many small ratios.

1) **Deeper:** how much of a hot hand survives the correction, and why that's still disputed
2) **Sideways:** the same small-sample trap in the 1990s "small schools do best" push, which a Gates Foundation program acted on

**On 1 January 1995, a wave that wave models put at once in 10,000 years hit an oil platform in the North Sea.**

The Draupner platform had a laser pointed down at the sea surface. It measured a single wave 25.6 m from trough to crest, while the waves around it were roughly 12 m. Standard ocean models at the time treated the sea as a sum of independent wave trains. In those models, a wave more than twice the height of its neighbours was effectively impossible.

For centuries, sailors had reported "walls of water", and naval architects had mostly put those reports down to panic and exaggeration. Draupner was the first time an instrument caught one. In 2004, the European Space Agency's MaxWave project scanned the oceans by satellite for three weeks and found more than ten waves over 25 m. The explanation turned out to be nonlinear. In some conditions a wave can pull energy from the waves next to it and suddenly grow, and seas crossing at an angle make that more likely.

**The twist:** the people who were right were the ones being doubted, and the model was trusted only because nothing had measured the sea well enough to prove it wrong. For your work: when a user reports something "impossible", like a double booking or a seat that vanished, treat it as your first Draupner reading. It's evidence your model doesn't cover, not a mistake on their part.

**The hardest point for a planing dinghy isn't top speed. It's the hump just before planing, and the racing rules are written around it.**

"Hull speed" is a wave-speed limit. A deep-water wave travels at about 1.25 × √(wavelength in metres) m/s. In knots and feet, that's the familiar 1.34 × √LWL. When the boat reaches the speed of a bow wave as long as its own waterline, the bow sits on one crest and the stern drops into the trough behind it. To go faster, the boat has to climb its own bow wave. A Laser has a waterline of about 3.8 m, which gives a hull speed of about 4.7 knots. Yet it planes in double figures.

What makes the difference is where the lift comes from. Below the hump, the hull is held up by buoyancy, so pushing harder mostly builds a bigger wave. Over the hump, it's held up by dynamic lift: water pressure on the flat aft sections raises the hull. The wetted area shrinks, the wave-making drag fades, and the drag curve flattens. On a light boat it can even dip. So the most force per knot you'll ever need comes right at the transition, and once you're over it, holding speed takes less force than getting there did.

**The twist:** the rule-writers wrote that hump into the rules. Racing Rules of Sailing 42 bans pumping. But 42.3(c) allows it off a beat when surfing or planing is possible, and only once per sail per wave or gust, "to initiate surfing or planing". Extra energy is allowed at exactly one point, the threshold, and nowhere else. That's the drag curve turned into a rule.

1) **Deeper:** how William Froude's 1870s tank tests on small models predicted full-size ships, and the part of drag that doesn't scale
2) **Sideways:** foiling Moths, which skip the hump by lifting the hull clean out of the water
