**The idea at the heart of your migration is older than React. It comes from a 2010 HTTP spec.**

`stale-while-revalidate` comes from RFC 5861, written by Mark Nottingham in 2010. It's a `Cache-Control` extension: `max-age=600, stale-while-revalidate=30` tells a cache to serve the stored response for 600 seconds. For 30 more seconds after that, it may keep serving the old copy right away while it fetches a fresh one in the background. Vercel's `swr` library (2019) is named after it. React Query, also from 2019 and renamed TanStack Query in v4, built the same pattern into the client.

The twist: the HTTP version limits how stale data can get. TanStack's defaults go the other way:

- `staleTime: 0` means data is stale the moment it arrives.
- `refetchOnWindowFocus: true` and refetch-on-mount are on.

Requests for the same query key that are in flight at the same time get merged. But every remount and every switch back to the tab refetches each stale query. On an analytics dashboard with a dozen widgets, a straight swap from `useEffect` can quietly multiply your requests. A per-query `staleTime` that matches how often the data really changes is usually the one-line fix. Worth checking in your agent's diff.

**Meanwhile:**
**Computers proved that some endgames are wins, and FIDE ended up ruling them draws anyway.**

In the 1980s Ken Thompson used his chess machine Belle to work backwards from every checkmate. This is retrograde analysis, which is how endgame tablebases are built. It showed that some endings can be won by force, but only in more than 50 moves. The first exceptions came before that: FIDE's 1965 rules let the move limit be raised for certain positions, such as two knights against a pawn. In 1984 FIDE wrote a list of positions into the rule and gave them 100 moves. In 1989 it changed this to 75 moves for six named endings. Then in 1992 it scrapped every exception and went back to a strict 50. The engine wins often consisted of moves no human could understand or carry out, and more exceptions kept turning up. So the rule stopped trying to describe the truth about chess and became a limit on what a human can do over the board.

The twist: today's engines bake the rule into their tables. Syzygy tablebases, which Stockfish uses, label some positions "cursed wins": wins in perfect play that the 50-move rule turns into draws. The 7-piece Lomonosov tables (2012) found a mate in 549 moves, more than ten times the limit. So in a few positions "the engine says it's winning" and "you can win this" are different claims. Since 2014, 75 moves without a capture or pawn move ends the game automatically, even if nobody claims the draw.

**User:** 1 — DTZ vs DTM, always wondered why the tablebase moves look so weird

**Meanwhile:**
**Tablebase moves look weird because most tablebases aren't trying to mate you fastest. They're racing to the next capture or pawn move.**

There are two ways to measure "how close to winning":

- **DTM (distance to mate):** the number of moves to checkmate with perfect play on both sides. Follow it and you get the fastest mate. Lomonosov's 7-piece tables store this, and they're huge, roughly 140 TB.
- **DTZ (distance to zeroing):** the number of moves until the next capture or pawn move, the moves that reset the 50-move counter to zero. Syzygy, the format Stockfish and Lichess use, stores this alongside a win/draw/loss flag. It's much smaller to store, roughly 18 TB for 7 pieces.

A DTZ-optimal engine thinks in checkpoints, not in mates. It heads for the nearest move that resets the counter, even if that means entering a different won ending, swapping pieces at odd moments, or pushing a pawn while a clean mate sits right there. Every move is correct, since the win is never lost. But the path makes no sense as a plan, because there is no plan: just "reach the next reset in time, repeat."

**The twist:** DTM-perfect play can lose a win under the rules. The fastest mate might run more than 50 moves without a capture or pawn move, and then the opponent claims a draw. DTZ (strictly DTZ50, which accounts for the rule) is the measure that guarantees a win under the rules. The tables that look stranger are the ones that are more correct for real games. The "natural" fastest mate is the one that can get a winning position ruled a draw.

That's also where "cursed wins" come from. When even the fastest route to a reset takes more than 50 moves, the position counts as a draw under the rules, even though it's a win with perfect play.
