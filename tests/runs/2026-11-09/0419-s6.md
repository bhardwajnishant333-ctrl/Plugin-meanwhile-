A courier crew that's busy 95% of the time keeps orders waiting about 5x longer than the same crew at 80%. Only 15 points more work, but five times the queue.

That comes from Kingman's formula (J.F.C. Kingman, 1961). In a queue, the average wait grows with ρ/(1−ρ), where ρ is utilisation, the share of time your servers are busy:
- 50% busy → 1
- 80% busy → 4
- 90% busy → 9
- 95% busy → 19

So the curve is mild for a long while and then turns into a wall. A second term multiplies it: how irregular arrivals and job times are. Random arrivals and uneven job lengths roughly double the wait compared with a steady, predictable flow.

The twist: idle capacity is what keeps waits short. A dispatcher that keeps every rider busy is close to that wall, and one lunch rush pushes it over. Your Oban queues work the same way. If a queue's concurrency only just covers the average load, its latency will spike as soon as job times vary. What matters isn't the average job time; it's how much job times vary and how close to full the workers run.

Gyroscopic force isn't what keeps a rolling bike upright. We know because in 2011 someone built a bike with the gyroscopic effect cancelled out, and it still balanced itself.

The paper is Kooijman, Schwab, Meijaard, Papadopoulos and Ruina, *Science*, April 2011. They put a small wheel spinning backwards next to each real wheel, which cancels the gyroscopic effect. They also took away trail, the caster-like offset that usually gets the rest of the credit. Pushed with no rider, it still caught itself when it started to tip, within a certain speed range.

What actually does it is how the weight is arranged. When the frame leans, the front end (fork, bars, wheel) is weighted so that it turns into the fall. That brings the wheels back under the bike. The bike steers itself upright, the way you move your feet under you when you trip.

The twist: people rode bikes for about 130 years before anyone could explain this. Most textbooks had it wrong. You can build something that works perfectly well long before anyone understands why it works.
