**People trust a website more when it makes them wait, as long as it shows them the work.**

In 2011, Harvard researchers Ryan Buell and Michael Norton tested fake travel-search sites. One returned flight results instantly. **The typeface on your Selectric ball spread everywhere because IBM never owned it.**

Howard "Bud" Kettler drew it for IBM in 1955. He first called it "Messenger" and then renamed it. His reasoning, roughly: a letter can be an ordinary messenger, or it can be the *courier*, which radiates dignity, prestige and stability. IBM never secured exclusive rights to the design. Other typewriter makers copied it freely, and within a few years it was the default look of typed paper. The Selectric (1961) made it even easier to use: you could swap the whole typeface in seconds by popping in a different ball.

**The twist:** because every Courier character takes up the same width, a typed page holds a predictable amount of text. Hollywood still requires screenplays in 12-point Courier for that reason. One page comes out to about one minute of screen time. A typewriter limitation became a unit for budgeting and scheduling. The US State Department also kept Courier New as its standard font until 2004.

**The usual story is that QWERTY was designed to slow typists down so the keys wouldn't jam. The evidence for that is thin.**

Christopher Latham Sholes and his partners kept reworking the layout through the late 1860s and sold the result to Remington, which shipped it in 1873. The jam story has an obvious problem: some of the most common letter pairs in English, like E and R, sit right next to each other. In 2011, Kyoto researchers Koichi and Motoko Yasuoka traced the layout's early versions and argued that it was shaped by its first serious customers, telegraph operators taking down Morse code. In American Morse, Z sounds almost exactly like "SE", so putting Z next to S and E helps a typist fix a mix-up quickly.

**The twist:** QWERTY is the classic economics example of a worse design that got locked in anyway (Paul David, 1985). Then came the "Fable of the Keys" (Liebowitz and Margolis, 1990). They found that the big claims about Dvorak, the supposedly faster layout, came mostly from Dvorak's own studies. A 1956 US government trial found that retraining typists on it wasn't worth it. So the most famous example of a bad standard winning may not show a bad standard at all. Tidy origin stories about a design are often written after the fact, and they stick because they're tidy.
