**Meanwhile:** **A Redux selector that only wasted renders can crash the app once it runs on Zustand.**

In Redux, `useSelector(s => ({ a: s.a, b: s.b }))` returns a new object every time. The cost is extra re-renders, plus a dev-mode warning in newer react-redux versions. Zustand v5 runs on React 18's `useSyncExternalStore`, and that hook is stricter. It calls your selector, compares the result to the previous one with `Object.is`, and treats a new reference as "the store changed during render." So it renders again, gets another new object, and keeps going until you hit "Maximum update depth exceeded." Selectors that build arrays with `.filter()` or `.map()` break the same way. The fix in v5 is `useShallow` from `zustand/react/shallow`, or picking one value per hook call.

**The twist:** React made this strict on purpose. With concurrent rendering, React 18 can pause a render partway through. If an outside store changes during that pause, one component can show the old shipment status while its neighbour shows the new one. This is called "tearing." The strict snapshot check is how React prevents it. Your selectors have to return stable values because React needs a consistent snapshot across the whole render.

Worth one grep when the agent finishes: look for selectors that return `({`, `[`, `.filter(` or `.map(`.

1) **Deeper**: how `useSyncExternalStore` forces a synchronous re-render to prevent tearing, and what that costs you
2) **Sideways**: why the Caro-Kann's "solid but passive" reputation is a myth built from 1900s games

**Meanwhile:** **First, a correction: "a myth built from 1900s games" was too strong. The real story is better: the most aggressive attacker of his era lost a world-championship rematch to the Caro-Kann partly *because* it's quiet.**

The opening is named after Horatio Caro and Marcus Kann, who published analysis of 1.e4 c6 in the 1880s. Kann gave it a strong start with a short win over Jacques Mieses at Hamburg in 1885. Its "solid but passive" reputation comes from how it's built. It's a French Defence where the light-squared bishop gets out to f5 or g4 *before* ...e6 locks it in. The cost is slower counterplay, because Black gives up the early ...c5 break the French relies on.

In 1960 Mikhail Tal took the world title from Mikhail Botvinnik with sacrificial chaos. Botvinnik had a rematch clause, and in 1961 he came back prepared to deny Tal exactly that. Against 1.e4 he relied largely on the Caro-Kann (I'm sure about the strategy, less sure of the exact game count). He won the rematch 13–8.

**The twist:** Botvinnik didn't choose the Caro-Kann because it was objectively best. He chose it because it was worst *for Tal*: positions with few targets, where speculative sacrifices don't work. The "passivity" was the weapon. In blitz it works the same way. The best opening is often the one that makes your opponent spend clock time on positions they don't enjoy, even if it isn't the most correct.

## Turn 3
**User:** 3

**Meanwhile:** **Take away a grandmaster's view of the board and their play barely gets worse. Take away their thinking time and it gets clearly worse.**

The source is Chabris and Hearst (Cognitive Science, 2003). They ran computer analysis on grandmaster games from the Monaco Amber tournaments, where the same elite players played both rapid and blindfold rapid, and compared those with classical games. Blunders were clearly more frequent in rapid than in classical. Blindfold rapid, with no board at all, was only slightly worse than ordinary rapid. I'm confident about that pattern but not the exact percentages, so I won't give numbers.

Their reading: a strong player's skill is mostly two things. Pattern recognition is fast, roughly a glance, and it holds up without a board. Forward search means calculating concrete lines, and that's what extra time buys. Taking away sight hurts the first a little. Taking away time cuts into the second, and that's where blunders come from.

**The twist for blitz:** your clock isn't for thinking in general. Spending it evenly wastes it, because most moves are pattern-level and more time doesn't improve them. Moves that need calculation are different: checks, captures, threats, and any position where a pattern feels off. Those gain the most from time. Strong blitz players move instantly on familiar Caro-Kann structures and save their seconds for the tactical moments. They aren't thinking faster. They're choosing *when* to calculate.

1) **Deeper**: the de Groot and Chase–Simon "chunking" experiments, where masters rebuild real positions from a 5-second look but not random ones
2) **Sideways**: "System 1 vs System 2" and why its strongest real-world evidence comes from fields like chess and firefighting
3) **Wildcard**: why a computer's evaluation of a position has almost nothing to do with how hard that position is for a human to play
