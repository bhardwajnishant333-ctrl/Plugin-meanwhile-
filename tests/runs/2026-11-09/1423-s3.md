## Turn 2
**User:** 1 — ooh yes, the usury dodge

**Meanwhile:** The Church banned interest on loans. It did not ban currency exchange. Medieval bankers built a whole credit market in the gap between those two rules.

**How it worked.** A merchant in Florence handed a banker florins. In return he got a bill of exchange, payable later in Bruges in Flemish money. "Later" meant a customary delay called *usance*, roughly the time a courier needed to make the trip. Exchange rates were quoted differently in each city, and the gap almost always favoured whoever put up the money. So if you sent funds out on a bill and back again on a second bill (*cambio* and *recambio*), you usually ended up with more than you started with. That surplus was interest in all but name. Raymond de Roover's study of the Medici Bank argues that this exchange business, not open lending, was the core of how the bank earned money on credit.

**Why theologians allowed it.** Because of risk. Rates could move while the bill was travelling, so the profit wasn't guaranteed, and an uncertain gain didn't count as usury. In practice the gap between cities was built into the system, so the return was fairly predictable. The bills just had to be real ones.

**Where it broke down.** The *dry exchange* (*cambio secco*): bills written out and "returned" without any money ever really moving between cities, so the bill was nothing more than a loan. Moralists attacked it for centuries, and in 1571 Pope Pius V's bull *In eam* condemned it outright. At the Genoese fairs, bankers could also roll a debt forward from one quarterly fair to the next at an agreed rate. That rollover rate worked very much like an interest rate, though I'm less sure of the details there.

**The twist:** the rule never stopped lending. What it did was decide what shape lending had to take. Because credit had to look like moving money between places, medieval finance ended up building Europe's payment network: bills, correspondent banks and clearing fairs. It's an early case of a compliance rule creating infrastructure. Bankers didn't argue about whether interest was moral. They argued about whether there was enough real risk in the deal.

**Meanwhile:** The 1571 ban on "dry exchange" has a close modern relative. A lot of Islamic banking money passes through metal that may only ever change hands on paper.

**The mechanism.** Islamic law forbids *riba* (interest), so banks structure finance as trade or leasing:
- **Murabaha:** the bank buys the thing you want and sells it to you at cost plus a disclosed markup, which you pay in instalments. That markup is often priced off conventional benchmarks such as LIBOR in the past and SOFR now. Critics point to this constantly.
- **Tawarruq:** you need cash, not a thing. The bank buys a commodity, often metal held on the London Metal Exchange, and sells it to you on deferred payment. You sell it straight away for cash on the spot market. The metal can go through several owners in minutes without moving off the warehouse shelf.

**The *cambio secco* rerun.** Just as medieval moralists asked whether money really changed places, modern scholars ask whether the metal is really changing owners. In 2009 the OIC's International Islamic Fiqh Academy ruled that *organised* tawarruq, where the bank arranges the whole round trip, is not permissible. It is still widely used. In 2007 Sheikh Taqi Usmani, chair of AAOIFI's Sharia board (AAOIFI sets the main standards for Islamic finance), said around 85% of sukuk (Islamic bonds) weren't truly compliant. His reason: the issuer promised to buy back the assets at face value, which guaranteed the investor's principal, and a guaranteed principal is debt, not shared risk. The market changed its standards after that.

**The twist:** both systems end up deciding legitimacy by testing whether risk is real, not whether there is profit. That gives everyone a clear target to engineer toward: add just enough risk, or something that looks like risk, to pass. Seven centuries apart, the argument is the same one about how much risk counts as enough.

1. **Deeper:** the Dana Gas case (2017). The issuer argued its own $700M sukuk were no longer Sharia-compliant and so it didn't have to pay. An English court disagreed.
2. **Sideways:** Hanseatic merchants handled credit in a completely different way from the Italians, with very little banking at all
