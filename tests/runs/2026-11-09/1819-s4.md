## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
Your model can get an Urdu word exactly right and still be scored wrong, because the reference was typed on a different keyboard.

**How it happens:** Several Urdu letters can be stored as two different Unicode characters that look the same on screen:
- yeh: Arabic ي (U+064A) or Farsi/Urdu ی (U+06CC)
- kaf: Arabic ك (U+0643) or keheh ک (U+06A9)
- heh: ه (U+0647) or ہ (U+06C1)

Urdu text scraped from the web, subtitles and news sites mixes both forms, depending on the keyboard layout or font the writer used. To WER, ی and ي are different characters, so a correct word counts as a substitution. Unicode normalization (NFC/NFKC) does not fix this, because these pairs are separate letters, not encoding variants of one letter.

Spacing makes it worse. Urdu script joins letters by fixed rules, and the space bar is often used just to stop letters joining. So writers leave out spaces, add extra ones, or use an invisible ZWNJ (U+200C). One spacing difference can turn one word into two, which costs two errors.

**The twist:** The normalizer you run before scoring can quietly decide your WER. My understanding of Whisper's `BasicTextNormalizer` is that it replaces Unicode marks, symbols and punctuation with spaces. If that's right, a short-vowel diacritic (zer, zabar) in the middle of a word could split it in two. Treat that as something to check in the source, not a fact. In practice, map the variant letters to one standard form in both references and output, strip ZWNJ, and report CER next to WER. If WER drops a lot after cleanup while CER barely moves, the gap was spelling noise, not your model.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
The dry-bulk shipping crash of the 2010s came from ships ordered at the 2008 peak, which arrived after the market had already turned.

**How it worked:** The Baltic Dry Index hit its record of 11,793 in May 2008. With rates that high, shipowners ordered new vessels heavily. Around then the global orderbook reached an unusually large share of the existing fleet — I remember it as well over half, but treat the exact figure as approximate. A bulk carrier takes roughly 2–3 years from order to delivery, so boom-ordered ships were delivered across 2010–2013 into a market that had collapsed in late 2008. A ship already built keeps sailing as long as the charter covers running costs, so the extra supply didn't leave quickly. The BDI reached its all-time low of 290 in February 2016.

**The twist:** This is the cobweb model, which Nicholas Kaldor named in 1934 studying farm cycles like the pig cycle. When supply reacts to today's price but arrives years later, a market overshoots instead of settling. Your training run has a mild version: update on stale feedback with a large step size and you oscillate instead of converging. Asynchronous SGD with stale gradients is the textbook case.
