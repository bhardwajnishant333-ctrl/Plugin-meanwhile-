**One door key and a metal file can be enough to work out the building's master key.**

In 2003, Matt Blaze (then at AT&T Labs) published "Rights Amplification in Master-Keyed Mechanical Locks". In a master-keyed pin tumbler lock, each pin stack has an extra wafer, which gives it two shear lines: one for your key's cut and one for the master's. So the lock accepts both keys.

The attack takes one pin position at a time. You cut a blank to match your own key everywhere except that position, then try each depth there. If one opens the lock and isn't your cut, it's the master's cut for that position. Repeat for every pin. That's at most (pins × depths) tries, and fewer in practice because you can file one blank deeper step by step instead of cutting a new one each time. You never pick anything and leave no marks. Blaze also pointed out that some locksmiths had already known this informally. They were mostly upset that he published it.

**The twist:** you don't break the master key, you query for it. The lock is an oracle that answers yes or no, and accepting a second key makes it leak that key one pin at a time. Software repeats this pattern. Any check that accepts more than one credential and gives a different response for partial matches, like timing differences or a "wrong password" vs "no such user" message, can be read the same way, one bit per query.

1. **Deeper**: how real master-key systems defend against this, and why most deployed ones still don't.
2. **Sideways**: GPS week rollover. GPS counts weeks with a 10-bit number, so it wrapped to zero on 6 April 2019, and some older receivers jumped back about 19.6 years. That's relevant if your trail logs trust the device clock during sync.
3. **Wildcard**: why the Ancient Egyptian wooden pin lock, about 4,000 years old, already used the same principle as your pins.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**The lock you pick for sport is a sideways version of a wooden lock that's about 4,000 years old.**

The Egyptian pin lock was a wooden bolt with holes drilled in its top. Loose wooden pins dropped from the fixed housing above into those holes and pinned the bolt in place. The key was a long wooden bar with pegs set in the same pattern. You pushed it into a hollow in the bolt and lifted, the pegs raised each pin out of its hole, and you pulled the bolt open, often using the key itself. The usual "4,000 years" date is approximate. Similar locks show up across the ancient Near East, and the dating of individual finds is fuzzy.

Linus Yale Sr. made a pin tumbler lock in the 1840s, and his son Linus Yale Jr. turned it into the small cylinder lock with a flat key in the 1860s. The big change was splitting each pin in two and rotating a plug instead of sliding a bolt. The lock now opens only when every split lines up exactly at the shear line.

**The twist:** in the old design, lifting a pin higher than needed was fine because it only had to clear the bolt. The Yale split pin made the height requirement exact in both directions. That's why the key could shrink to pocket size, and it's what lets you set pins one at a time. But that same split is where a master wafer slides in. So the 1860s change that made modern keys possible also made the master-key leak from the last topic possible.

1. **Deeper**: why manufacturing tolerances are what make pins bind one at a time (and why picking works at all).
2. **Sideways**: Alfred Hobbs picked the "unpickable" Bramah lock at the 1851 Great Exhibition, which took him about 51 hours spread over 16 days, and started a fight about whether publishing lock flaws is ethical. It's the same argument as responsible disclosure today.
3. **Wildcard**: a hike-relevant one: why your phone can still find you in a deep canyon with no signal (it's the GPS receiver, not the cell network).
