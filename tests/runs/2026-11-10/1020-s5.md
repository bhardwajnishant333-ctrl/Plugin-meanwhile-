Rafa, the most famous sourdough culture works because its two microbes don't compete for the same food.

In a classic San Francisco starter, the bacterium *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis* (formerly *Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, named in 1971 by USDA researchers) eats maltose, the main sugar released as flour starch breaks down. It splits the maltose and pushes some glucose back out into the dough. Its partner yeast, *Kazachstania humilis* (old name *Candida milleri*), can't use maltose at all, so it lives on that leftover glucose. The bacterium also tolerates the acid it makes, and the yeast does fine in it. They split the food and the conditions between them, which is why such a stable pair can survive decades of feedings.

Your starter and your new queue scale for the same reason: whoever is involved never fights over the same item.

The RAF found that radar operators started missing U-boats after about 30 minutes on watch, even though they were trained, motivated and knew lives depended on it.

In the 1940s the psychologist Norman Mackworth set out to find out why. He built the "Mackworth Clock": a single pointer that ticked forward one step at a time. Every so often it jumped a double step, and the subjects' only task was to report those jumps. His 1948 paper ("The breakdown of vigilance during prolonged visual search") showed detection falling noticeably within the first half hour and continuing to slide after that. I'm not confident of the exact percentage, so I'll leave it out. The decline came from the task being monotonous, not from tiredness. Short breaks, phone calls and even stimulants partly restored performance.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
Watching a screen where almost nothing happens feels effortless, but people who do it rate it as hard mental work.

For decades the textbook explanation for Mackworth's results was underload: the task is too dull, arousal drops, and the brain coasts. Joel Warm, Raja Parasuraman and Gerald Matthews tested that idea. They had people rate vigilance tasks on NASA's workload scale (NASA-TLX), the same instrument used for cockpit work. The ratings came out high, with mental demand and frustration at the top. Their 2008 paper in *Human Factors* has a title that gives the result: "Vigilance Requires Hard Mental Work and Is Stressful." An earlier study from the same group (Grier et al., 2003) made the same point: the decline in detection reflects spent effortful attention, not mindlessness.

There is still a rival explanation. The mind-wandering researchers argue that attention drifts off the task and so performance drops. The two camps haven't fully settled it, and the best current models probably include both: you spend effort holding your attention in place, and when that effort runs low, your mind wanders.

The twist is about why it's hard to fix. Making a dull task more interesting doesn't remove the cost, because the cost comes from holding attention, not from the dullness. What helps is cutting how long a stretch you hold it. That means breaks, cues that warn you when a signal is likely, or giving the watching to a machine. So 30 minutes of half-watching your agent isn't rest. It drains the attention you'll need later to review the Oban diff, and a real break works better.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
The light that misled the operators at Three Mile Island was working correctly. It reported what the control system had been told to do, not what the valve had actually done.

On 28 March 1979, Unit 2 at Three Mile Island had a feedwater problem. A relief valve on top of the pressurizer (the PORV) opened as designed to release pressure, and was then commanded to close. It stuck open. The control-room light for that valve showed whether its solenoid had been sent the "close" signal, not where the valve itself was. The light went out, so the operators believed the valve was shut. Reactor coolant kept escaping for over two hours.

Meanwhile the board was flooding. The figure usually cited is more than 100 alarms in the first few minutes, and the alarm printer fell far behind. Water readings made it look as though the system was overfilling, so the crew throttled back the emergency cooling, which was the exact opposite of what was needed. About half the core melted. The Kemeny Commission (October 1979) blamed operator training and control-room design far more than equipment failure.

The twist is that you're porting this design question right now. A queue that reports "enqueued" or "executing" is that light: it shows the command, not the outcome. Oban helps a little here, since jobs are rows in your own database, so you can check a job's state transactionally next to the data it touched. But the real lesson is the TMI one: for anything that matters, check the outcome itself (did the row change, did the email actually send). Don't trust the job's status as proof the work happened. That's worth thinking about when you review the agent's diff.
