**Under strict perfect-play math, some chess positions are wins. Under the rules of chess, they're draws.**

In 2012, Moscow State University's Lomonosov supercomputer finished a database of every position with 7 pieces or fewer: about 140 TB of solved chess. The longest forced win it found takes 549 moves. No human could find that line or explain it. Most of the moves look aimless.

The 50-move rule stops it anyway. If there's no capture or pawn move in 50 moves, either side can claim a draw.

FIDE has run into this before. Starting in the 1970s, Ken Thompson's early endgame databases kept finding wins that needed more than 50 moves, such as some rook-and-bishop vs rook positions. FIDE responded by giving certain endings longer limits, up to 100 moves. Then the databases found even longer wins, and every exception needed another one. Around 1992, FIDE dropped all the special cases and went back to a flat 50 moves for everything.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**A tablebase never looks ahead. It works backwards from every possible checkmate.**

1. **List every checkmate.** Generate every legal position with those pieces and mark each checkmate as "mated in 0."
2. **Step back one move.** If the side to move can reach a mated-in-0 position, it has mate in 1. If every move the defender has lands in one of those, the defender is lost in 1.
3. **Repeat until nothing changes.** Each pass pushes the solved region one move further back. When a full pass marks nothing new, every unmarked position is a draw.

There's no search tree, no evaluation and no guessing. It's dynamic programming over every state in the game. That's how a mate in 549 can be found at all: nobody searched 549 moves deep. The answer spread backwards from checkmate, one layer per pass.

Ken Thompson's early databases tested this against a grandmaster. In the late 1970s, Walter Browne played queen vs rook against the database, an ending that's known to be won. As I remember it, he failed to win within 50 moves on his first try and only managed it after studying. I'm fairly sure of the outline but not the details.

**The twist ties back to the 50-move rule.** The Syzygy tablebases (Ronald de Man, 2013), the ones engines use today, track two things for each position: whether it's a win, loss or draw, and how many moves until the next capture or pawn move resets the 50-move count. So they can label positions as **"cursed wins"** (won with perfect play but a draw under the rule) and **"blessed losses"** (the mirror case). Someone built the rulebook into the solved math.

For your port: a form is a small state machine (pristine → dirty → touched → invalid → submitting). React-hook-form only re-runs validation when one of those states changes, instead of re-rendering the whole form on every keystroke like Formik. That's the main reason for the speedup you're porting for.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**Less engine-like, and that's why strong players hold up. Under time pressure they stop calculating and lean on recognising patterns.**

The research is fairly consistent:

- **Calderwood, Klein & Crandall (1988).** They compared blitz and slow games and had the moves graded. The masters' move quality dropped only a little in blitz. The weaker club players' quality fell much more. Time pressure widened the gap between them.
- **Gobet & Simon (1996).** They studied Kasparov playing simuls against entire national teams, where he had a fraction of the usual thinking time per move. He still played at roughly elite grandmaster strength. My memory is that he lost something like 100 rating points, not several hundred. Treat that number as approximate.
- **Chabris & Hearst (2003).** They checked grandmaster games with an engine to find blunders. Rapid games had clearly more errors than classical ones. But blindfold rapid was barely worse than normal rapid, so seeing the board added little once the clock was short.

Put together: deep calculation is what goes first when the clock runs down. What stays is the stored library of "this kind of position wants this kind of move," which researchers estimate at tens of thousands of patterns. Engines work the other way. Take away their search depth and they collapse. Take away a grandmaster's and they lose a bit of polish.

**The twist:** skill is mostly what you can still do once you no longer have time to think it through. Blitz players don't have quicker calculation. They need less of it. The same goes for review. The person who spots in five seconds that a diff's `onSubmit` is missing `handleSubmit` isn't reading faster. They've seen that mistake before.
