## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
Rubber friction isn't really a property of the rubber. It works more like a frequency response, and you tune a frequency response all day.

In 1963, K.A. Grosch at the Natural Rubber Producers' Research Association measured rubber sliding over glass and silicon-carbide surfaces. He covered a huge range of speeds and temperatures, from about −58 °C to 100 °C. Every friction-vs-speed curve had a peak. Then he slid each curve along the speed axis using the Williams–Landel–Ferry temperature shift (the standard rule for how temperature speeds up or slows down a polymer's response). The curves stacked into one master curve. Friction peaks when the rock's bumps flex the rubber at the frequency where it loses the most energy, which is the peak of its loss modulus. On a rough surface, that frequency is roughly sliding speed divided by the spacing between bumps (asperity wavelength).

So the rock texture is your input signal, foot speed sets the frequency, and the rubber's loss curve is the transfer function. Temperature and speed can be traded for each other: making the rubber warmer has the same effect as slowing the excitation. For a slow, static smear, a warm afternoon can push you off the friction peak even with the same shoes on the same rock. One caveat: I can't say how much of climbers' "cold is better" comes from the rubber and how much from drier skin. Skin is probably a big part of it.

1) Deeper: why a rough surface moves the friction peak to a different speed than smooth glass does.
2) Sideways: Robert Park's 1929 power-grid paper, where the dq frame in your FOC loop comes from.
3) Wildcard: how a physics anomaly that lasted 30 years turned out to be heat leaving a spacecraft unevenly.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
The PI controllers in your FOC loop only work because of a 1929 trick for skipping hard math by hand.

Robert H. Park at GE had a problem with synchronous generators. Their inductances change as the rotor turns, so the equations had coefficients that varied with time. Nobody could solve those with a slide rule. His paper "Two-Reaction Theory of Synchronous Machines" (AIEE Transactions, 1929) moved the equations into a frame that rotates with the rotor, where those inductances become constants. That's your d and q axes. Edith Clarke, also at GE, later laid out the stationary αβ step in *Circuit Analysis of A-C Power Systems* (1943). I recall a 2000 IEEE survey ranking Park's paper among the most influential power papers of the century, but I'm not sure of its exact rank.

Here's what that buys you. In the dq frame, phase currents at hundreds of hertz become DC. An integrator only gets zero steady-state error on DC, so a plain PI loop tracks it perfectly. Without the rotating frame, you'd need a resonant (PR) controller tuned to the electrical frequency, and that frequency moves with every change in pedal speed. Field orientation itself (Blaschke at Siemens, around 1971) added only one idea: point d at the rotor flux. Your agent is refactoring a hand-calculation shortcut from almost 100 years ago, and that shortcut is what turns an awkward sinusoidal tracking problem into a DC one.

1) Deeper: what PR controllers do, and why grid inverters often use them anyway.
2) Sideways: Edith Clarke's path from computing by hand at AT&T to GE's first woman electrical engineer.
3) Wildcard: how engineers stopped the Leaning Tower of Pisa by pulling soil out from under it.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
Tulip mania probably didn't ruin anyone you can name.

Most people know the story from Charles Mackay's *Extraordinary Popular Delusions* (1841): bulbs traded for houses, then a crash in February 1637 that wrecked the Dutch economy. Mackay mostly reworked moralizing pamphlets from 1637. When historian Anne Goldgar went through the Dutch archives for *Tulipmania* (2007), she couldn't find the wave of bankruptcies or the economic damage. The trade was a niche of merchants and artisans, and the Republic carried on fine.

The prices have an explanation too. Economist Earl Thompson argued (Public Choice, 2007) that late-1636 contracts were in effect turned into options. The buyer could walk away for a small fee. In 1638 Haarlem settled the outstanding contracts at roughly 3.5% of the agreed price; I'm fairly sure of that figure but not certain. If walking away costs about 3.5%, paying a "crazy" headline price isn't crazy at all.

Here's what follows from that. The textbook case of crowd irrationality is partly a moral sermon that got copied without anyone checking it. The prices look mad only if you misread what the contract actually committed the buyer to.

1) Deeper: how a cheap exit option inflates a headline price.
2) Sideways: other famous bubbles that look different in the archives (the South Sea Bubble, for one).
