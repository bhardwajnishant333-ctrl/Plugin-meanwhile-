**Onions are banned from US futures trading, and they're the only physical commodity that is.** It's been that way since 1958. Ending onion futures was meant to calm onion prices, and it seems to have done the opposite.

What happened: in 1955 two traders, Vincent Kosuga and Sam Siegel, built up a huge position in Chicago onion futures and physical onions, roughly 30 million pounds. Then they flooded the market. Over the following months a 50 lb bag dropped from about $2.75 to about 10 cents, which was less than the empty sack was worth. Farmers were furious, Congress stepped in, and Eisenhower signed the Onion Futures Act in 1958. Since then, the only other thing Congress has banned from futures trading is movie box-office receipts (Dodd-Frank, 2010).

The twist: this gave economists a natural experiment, with the same crop traded with futures and then without. Holbrook Working and Roger Gray studied it in the early 1960s, and later work has generally backed them up: onion prices swung more after the ban than when futures traded. **The Hunt brothers didn't lose to the market. They lost to the rulebook.**

What happened: Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt, Texas oil heirs, spent 1979 buying physical silver and futures, much of it on margin and alongside Saudi partners. Silver went from about $6/oz at the start of 1979 to a peak near $50 on 18 January 1980. Ordinary people melted down family silverware to sell it. Tiffany ran a full-page newspaper ad calling the Hunts' hoarding "unconscionable."

The exchanges then changed the rules twice. On 7 January COMEX adopted "Silver Rule 7," which sharply limited how much silver anyone could hold on margin. On 21 January it moved silver to **liquidation-only trading**: no new positions, only closing existing ones. In a squeeze that rule only cuts one way. New buyers were locked out while sellers could still leave. The price fell, and on 27 March, "Silver Thursday," it dropped to about $10.80. The Hunts missed a margin call from their broker, Bache, of roughly $100M. Banks put together a bailout loan of about $1.1B so the failure wouldn't spread. The brothers later lost a civil manipulation case and went bankrupt in 1988.

The twist: it later came out in congressional scrutiny that some COMEX board members held short silver positions while they voted for those rules. The microstructure lesson isn't "squeezes fail." It's that in a squeeze, the venue is a player whose payoff you haven't modelled. The same thing happened in March 2022. Nickel on the LME spiked above $100,000/t as Tsingshan's huge short got squeezed. The LME halted trading and cancelled about $3.9B of trades already done that morning, which mostly saved the shorts. Two squeezes 42 years apart, and both ended with the exchange intervening.

The panic got worse at the end of the year: the US embassy hostage crisis began in November and the Soviets invaded Afghanistan in December. Gold peaked at $850 on 21 January 1980, three days after silver's peak near $50.

The way it ended goes back to the same macro story. Volcker, Fed chair since August 1979, had already tightened sharply in October. In March 1980 Carter's credit-control program pushed banks away from lending for commodity speculation, which cut off the Hunts' borrowing just as the margin calls arrived. So you could argue about half of silver's rise was the macro move everyone was trading, and the rest was the Hunt premium. That's the part that disappeared when the rules changed.
