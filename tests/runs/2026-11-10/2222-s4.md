**What your fine-tuned model writes when nobody is speaking matters more than what it writes when they are.**

**Meanwhile:**
**One bank was shut down at the wrong time of day, and a whole global system for settling currency trades was built to handle that one time gap.**

On 26 June 1974, German regulators withdrew the banking licence of Bankhaus I.D. Herstatt in Cologne. Its currency bets had gone badly wrong. They closed it at the end of the German business day, around 15:30 local time, which was mid-morning in New York. Banks that had sold Deutsche Marks to Herstatt that day had already paid their marks in Germany. The dollars Herstatt owed them were due in New York hours later and were never paid. Herstatt's US correspondent bank froze its outgoing payments, and dollar settlement in New York seized up for days. Losing your half of a currency trade because the two halves settle at different times is now called "Herstatt risk". Before the end of 1974, the G10 central bank governors set up the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, mainly because of this.

**The twist:** this is the atomic-commit problem, with central banks as the databases. A currency trade is two writes in two systems, in two time zones, with no shared transaction. The industry's fix was CLS Bank, launched in 2002. It uses payment-versus-payment: each side's payment goes through only if the other side's does too. That only works during a daily window of about five hours, when the European and Asian central-bank payment systems are open at the same time. It was a scheduling fix, not a protocol fix. And it still doesn't cover everything. A BIS Quarterly Review in December 2022 estimated that around $2.2 trillion a day of FX trading still settles without that protection. I'm confident about the order of magnitude, less sure about the exact figure.

**Meanwhile:**
**There's a proof that no commit protocol can be safe and also never get stuck, and the currency-settlement world built CLS around that limit without ever calling it a protocol.**

Two-phase commit (Jim Gray's 1978 notes; Lampson and Sturgis around the same time) works like this. A coordinator asks every participant "can you commit?". Each one votes yes or no. If all say yes, the coordinator sends "commit". The weak point is the gap after a participant has voted yes but before it hears the decision. In that gap the participant is *in doubt*. It can't commit on its own and it can't abort on its own, so it holds its locks and waits. If the coordinator crashes then, everyone stays stuck. In 1981 Skeen proposed three-phase commit, which avoids getting stuck, but only if messages arrive within bounded delays and the network never splits. Then in 1985, Fischer, Lynch and Paterson (the "FLP" result) proved that in a fully asynchronous system, no algorithm can guarantee agreement if even one process can crash. You can't have safety and guaranteed progress for free.

**The twist:** Herstatt was a participant that voted yes in Frankfurt and then vanished before its New York write. Its counterparties were in exactly that in-doubt state, except the stuck resource was real money, not database locks. CLS doesn't get around FLP. It cuts the problem down in three ways. The coordinator is a heavily regulated bank that is very unlikely to "crash". Settlement happens only in a fixed window when all the payment systems are known to be open, which is effectively making the system synchronous by schedule. And it backs that up with committed liquidity lines. Gray and Lamport's 2006 Paxos Commit reaches the same idea from the computer-science side: you make the coordinator survive failures instead of hoping it won't fail.

**Your run, right now:** saving a checkpoint across several GPUs is the same commit problem. If a node dies partway through, you can end up with some shards written and others not, and that half-written checkpoint can load without any error. The standard fix is a commit marker: write every shard first, then write a small metadata or marker file last, and only treat a checkpoint as valid if that file exists. PyTorch's distributed checkpointing writes its `.metadata` file in the final step, though check this against your version. If you have your own checkpoint-saving code, make sure it does the same.
