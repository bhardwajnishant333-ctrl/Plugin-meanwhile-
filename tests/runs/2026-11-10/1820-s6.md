## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
**In 2009, Ubuntu users who crashed their machines started booting up to find their config files empty: still there, but zero bytes long.**

The cause was ext4's new "delayed allocation." To batch disk work, the filesystem held new file data in memory for a while (often tens of seconds) before deciding where it went on disk. Renames, though, were recorded almost immediately. Apps like KDE and GNOME saved settings the "safe" way: write a temp file, then rename it over the old one. With no `fsync` between those steps, a crash could leave the rename on disk while the data it pointed to was never written. You ended up with the new name, no contents, and the old file already gone.

Ted Ts'o, the ext4 maintainer, said the apps were wrong for skipping `fsync`. He also shipped a workaround in Linux 2.6.30: the `auto_da_alloc` heuristic, which forces the data out when a file is renamed over an existing one. Behaviour that POSIX never promised became something the kernel now guarantees, because so much software relied on it.

**The twist:** a save is only correct if it reaches the disk in the right order. The order of lines in your code doesn't guarantee that. Async adds a second layer that can reorder things. If two `SaveAsync()` calls are in flight and the older one finishes last, it silently overwrites the newer save. Nothing crashes, so no exception tells you. When the agent is done, check two things: what happens when a save starts while another is still writing, and whether there's a flush (`FileStream.Flush(true)` in .NET) before the temp-file swap.

1) **Deeper:** how to make an async save crash-proof (serialize, write temp, flush, swap, keep one backup)
2) **Sideways:** your sourdough starter is a two-species deal where the yeast and bacteria avoid competing because one can't eat the other's sugar
3) **Wildcard:** why `async void` in a MonoBehaviour can keep running after you exit Play Mode

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**Cut a string holding up a weight, and the weight goes *up*.**

In 1991, Joel Cohen and Paul Horowitz published a setup in *Nature* using two springs and three strings. A weight hangs from it. One short string links the springs in series, and two slack "safety" strings run in parallel. Cut the short middle string and the springs switch from series to parallel. Each spring now carries only half the load, so the weight rises.

The setup is a physical copy of **Braess's paradox** (Dietrich Braess, 1968): adding a road to a network can make everyone's trip slower. When each driver takes the route that is fastest for them, the shortcut pulls in so much traffic that it ends up slowing everyone down. It shows up outside the lab too. Seoul tore down the Cheonggyecheon elevated highway in 2003, and downtown traffic got no worse; by some measures it got better.

**The twist:** Tim Roughgarden and Éva Tardos proved there's a ceiling on how bad this gets. With linear congestion costs, everyone acting selfishly is never more than **4/3** as slow as a perfectly coordinated plan. This is the "price of anarchy." Selfishness costs something, but there's a limit. Engineers lean on that limit whenever they let each part optimise for itself instead of controlling the whole system centrally.

1) **Deeper:** where the 4/3 comes from, and why it gets much worse when congestion costs are steep instead of linear
2) **Sideways:** a starter and its microbes as an economy where no species competes for the same sugar
3) **Wildcard:** the 1990 Earth Day closure of 42nd Street in New York, when traffic didn't jam the way everyone predicted

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**Myth: San Francisco sourdough tastes the way it does because of San Francisco.**

In 1971, Leo Kline and T.F. Sugihara at a USDA lab in Albany, California, isolated the bacterium behind the city's famous sour bread and named it after the city: *Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, now *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*. Researchers later found the same species in starters across Europe and Asia. It isn't local to anywhere.

Rob Dunn's Global Sourdough Project then sequenced about 500 starters from home bakers (Landis et al., *eLife*, 2021). Geography explained surprisingly little of the differences between starters. Bigger factors were how the baker kept the starter: how often they fed it, where they stored it, and how old it was. A related study from the same group found bakers' hands carry more sourdough-type yeast and bacteria than other people's hands. I remember that finding well, but not exactly which journal it appeared in.

**The twist:** your starter doesn't really come from where you live. It's a record of your routine. Two bakers in the same kitchen who feed on different schedules can end up with measurably different cultures.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
**The smell isn't acetone, even though that's what most people's brains say.**

It's mostly **ethyl acetate**, the solvent in "non-acetone" nail polish remover. It forms when ethanol and acetic acid combine. A neglected starter produces plenty of both: the yeast keeps making alcohol (the grey "hooch" layer on top is mostly ethanol and water), and the bacteria keep making acid. Some sourdough yeasts make ethyl acetate directly, especially *Wickerhamomyces anomalus*, which brewers and winemakers know as a source of that solvent smell.

There's a common claim that cold fridge storage shifts a starter toward acetic (vinegary) acid and away from lactic (milder) acid. That's widely repeated by bakers and plausible, but I'm less sure how strong the effect is than I am about the chemistry above.

**The twist:** the smell means your starter is hungry, not dead. The microbes are still working; they've just run out of fresh flour and are giving off solvent and acid. Two or three feeds at room temperature usually bring back the yogurty smell. The real warning signs are pink or orange streaks or fuzzy mould. Sour, boozy or solvent smells are not.

1) **Deeper:** how many feeds it takes to rebalance a starter, and why discarding part of it before each feed matters
2) **Sideways:** "off-flavours" in beer brewing are the same molecules, deliberately dialled up or down
3) **Wildcard:** the Gold Rush prospectors who carried their starters and got nicknamed "sourdoughs"
