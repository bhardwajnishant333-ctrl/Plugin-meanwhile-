**Meanwhile:**
**The Hanseatic League dominated northern European trade for about 300 years, and it never published a definitive list of its members. As I understand it, the vagueness was deliberate.**

How it worked: the Hansa had no founding charter, no standing army, no common treasury and no permanent officials for most of its life. The Hansetag, its general assembly, is usually dated from 1356 in Lübeck and met for the last time in 1669. Attendance was patchy, and decisions only took effect once each town adopted them locally. Historians still argue over who counted as a member, with estimates from about 70 core towns to around 200 loosely tied ones. The real network was the four big foreign trading posts (Kontore): the Steelyard in London, Bruges, Bergen and the Peterhof in Novgorod. Its main tool for enforcing anything was exclusion. Cologne was expelled ("verhanst") in 1471 for siding with England during the Anglo-Hanseatic war, and taken back in 1476.

**The twist:** when foreign rulers, England in particular, demanded a member list, the Hansa avoided giving one. I'm fairly but not fully sure of the reason usually given: a list would have made every town liable for the debts and piracy of every other. Leaving the boundary unclear limited how far one town's trouble could spread. The cost was that nobody, insiders included, could say exactly what the Hansa covered.

**Meanwhile:**
**The canal that fed Lübeck was probably slower than the road it replaced, and it still won.**

The Stecknitz Canal was built roughly 1391–1398. It linked the Trave at Lübeck to the Elbe at Lauenburg, using the Stecknitz and Delvenau rivers plus a dug cut over the low ridge between them. That makes it a summit-level canal: boats climbed up one side and down the other, which needed locks and a steady water supply at the top. The route was about 95 km. Most locks were simple flash locks or weirs, so boats waited while water built up behind each one. The Palmschleuse at Lauenburg is often cited as one of Europe's oldest surviving chamber locks, though the structure you can see today is an 18th-century rebuild. Sources I trust give a trip of one to several weeks, depending on water levels. I won't give a firmer figure than that.

What it carried was salt from Lüneburg, mined from brine springs that had been worked since the Middle Ages. From Lübeck the salt went north to the Scania herring markets in what is now southern Sweden. There the herring was gutted and packed in salt barrels, and those barrels fed Catholic Europe through about 150 fast days a year. Lübeck's wealth depended on this salt-and-fish chain.

**The twist:** the overland Old Salt Road already existed and could be faster. The canal won on cost per ton, not on speed. One flat-bottomed boat could replace many carts, and tolls and bulk prices mattered more than delivery time. It was a choice for throughput over latency, and it worked for about 500 years until the Elbe–Lübeck Canal replaced it in 1900.

**Meanwhile:**
**In medieval Lüneburg, most of the salt pans were owned by clergy who never came to see them. In the end, that "passive income" set off a war that went all the way up to the Pope and the Emperor.**

How it worked: brine rose from a salt dome under the town. It was boiled down in a fixed number of works. The figure usually given is 54 boiling houses with 4 lead pans each, so about 216 pans. That number was capped, which made each pan right scarce and easy to trade. The pans were run by the Sülfmeister, local patrician families who did the work and sat on the town council. The underlying rights to a share of each pan's output belonged to owners spread across northern Germany, mostly monasteries, cathedral chapters and churches. They had received them as gifts or bought them as investments. Their income arrived every year without anyone leaving the cloister. In effect this was an early, illiquid market in income-producing shares, with ownership split from operation.

**The twist:** in the mid-1400s the town was deep in debt. The council demanded that the clerical owners give up a large part of their salt income to help pay it off. The clergy refused, and the dispute became the Lüneburg Prelates' War (Prälatenkrieg), which ran from about the 1440s into the 1460s. It brought in excommunications, appeals to Rome and imperial intervention, and at one point the town council was overthrown. I'm confident about the outline and less certain about the exact dates and the size of the levy. The underlying flaw was that ownership looked passive, but the operators controlled the cash flow. Once the operators were in trouble, nothing in the arrangement settled whose claim came first.

1) **Deeper:** Lüneburg is still sinking. Centuries of pumping brine dissolved the salt dome under the old town, and some streets have dropped by metres.
2) **Sideways:** the Fuggers of Augsburg, who went from textiles to financing Habsburg emperors and paid for the bribes behind Charles V's election in 1519
3) **Wildcard:** the claim that Lüneburg's salt boiling stripped the forests and created the Lüneburg Heath. It's widely repeated, but is it true?
