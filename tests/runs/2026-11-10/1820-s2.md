## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
That "1900" is really two numbers, and Lichess usually only shows you one of them.

**How it works:** Lichess uses Glicko-2, which Mark Glickman designed as a successor to Elo. Elo stores one number per player. Glicko stores your rating plus a **rating deviation (RD)**, which measures how sure the system is about that rating. Glicko-2 adds a third value, **volatility**, which tracks how erratic your results have been. Your true strength is roughly your rating ± 2×RD, with about 95% confidence. A typical RD for an active blitz player is around 45–60. At RD 50, "1900" really means somewhere between about 1800 and 2000. The "?" appears when your RD goes above a threshold (I think it's 110 on Lichess, but I'm not certain), which means the rating is provisional. You can see your RD on your Lichess rating history page.

**Three things that follow from this:**
- **Inactivity raises RD.** Take a break from blitz and the system becomes less sure about you, so your first few games back move your rating more, in either direction.
- **Your opponent's RD matters.** Beating someone with a "?" rating moves your rating less, because the system doesn't fully trust their number either.
- **Volatility follows your tilt.** A streak of upsets in both directions raises it, and then each game moves your rating more. Tilt is literally built into the math.

**The twist:** Elo gives everyone the same K-factor, so it's equally confident about a new player and a veteran. Glicko's real improvement is that it stores uncertainty as data instead of discarding it. Your booking app has the same choice. "Patient X no-shows 20% of the time" means one thing after 50 appointments and something very different after 2. If a no-show score or an overbooking rule ever goes in, storing the RD-like part next to the score matters more than the formula you pick.

1. **Deeper:** why Glickman had to invent volatility, and what went wrong without it
2. **Sideways:** "Elo hell": why matchmaking in games like League of Legends can make players feel trapped, and whether it's real
3. **Wildcard:** the Elo formula came out of chess and now ranks AI models on LMArena, and it was never built for that

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
The leaderboard that ranks AI models with "Elo" mostly stopped using Elo.

**What happened:** Chatbot Arena (now LMArena) launched in 2023 with online Elo. People vote on pairs of answers, and the ratings update after every vote. That causes a problem. Elo assumes players change over time, so recent games count more and the order of games matters. A fixed model checkpoint doesn't change at all. Shuffle the same votes into a different order and Elo gives you a different ranking. So Arena switched to fitting a **Bradley–Terry** model over all votes at once, the same math underneath Elo but without the time ordering, and added bootstrapped confidence intervals. They kept the "Elo" name because everyone already knew it.

**The twist:** The bigger distortion came from outside the formula. A 2025 paper, *The Leaderboard Illusion* (Cohere and coauthors), argued that big labs privately tested many variants and published only the best score. I believe Meta tested about 27 variants before Llama 4, though I'm less sure of that exact number. Test enough variants and the best score is partly luck. Your Playwright suite has the same problem: if you retry flaky tests until they pass, a green run is the best of several attempts and tells you less than a clean pass would.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
On 3 September 1967, all of Sweden switched from driving on the left to driving on the right in a single morning, and road deaths went down afterwards.

**What happened:** Sweden drove on the left, but most Swedish cars had the steering wheel on the left too, which is the setup for right-hand traffic. Overtaking on two-lane roads was dangerous because drivers sat on the wrong side to see oncoming cars. Every neighbouring country drove on the right. In a 1955 referendum about 83% voted no to switching. Parliament did it anyway in 1963 and spent four years preparing. On "Dagen H" (H for *högertrafik*, right-hand traffic), non-essential traffic was banned overnight. At 04:50 every vehicle stopped, moved to the other side of the road, and waited until 05:00. Crews had already put up the new signs, which were kept covered until the switch.

**The twist:** Accidents fell after the switch, probably because everyone was scared and drove carefully, and they drifted back to normal within about two years (that timeline is from memory, so treat it as approximate). The deeper lesson is that some migrations can't be done gradually. Half the country driving on each side isn't a gentle transition, it's a disaster. Sweden had no safe in-between state, so the only safe option was a short, total, well-rehearsed cutover. Your migration has a smaller version of this: a whole form on Formik or a whole form on react-hook-form is fine, but one form that mixes the two (Formik context wrapping RHF fields, or two sources of truth for validation) is where bugs come from. Migrate one whole form at a time, never half of one.

1. **Deeper:** Iceland made the same switch in 1968, and Samoa went the other way in 2009, for a surprising reason
2. **Sideways:** expand/contract (parallel change), the database-migration pattern that does give you a safe in-between state
