**The most famous "root cause" of the fall of Constantinople may never have happened.**

On 29 May 1453 the Theodosian Land Walls fell after holding for about a thousand years. Theodosius II's prefect Anthemius built them around 413. When the 447 earthquake knocked them down with Attila nearby, they were rebuilt in about 60 days, with the circus factions, the Blues and Greens, supplying the labour. Even the Fourth Crusade in 1204 got in over the sea walls on the Golden Horn, not through the land walls. In 1453, after weeks of bombardment by Orban's cannon, the historian Doukas reports that about 50 Ottoman soldiers slipped through the Kerkoporta, a small postern gate left unbarred after a sortie, and raised a banner on the walls. It's the perfect single-point-of-failure story. Many historians treat it as uncertain or as a later embellishment. The better-attested event happened at about the same time: Giovanni Giustiniani, the Genoese commander holding the most-hit section, was badly wounded and carried off. His men followed him, and the defence there fell apart.

**The twist:** Doukas's version survived for the same reason postmortems lean toward "one bad config line." An unlocked door is tidy and easy to retell. "Exhausted system under long load, and the one person holding the critical section walks away" is the real failure mode, and it's harder to write up. Walls rarely fail at the door. They fail at the person everyone was quietly depending on.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**After cutover your new primary will hand out IDs your old one already used, and the first sign is a wave of duplicate key errors.**

Logical replication in Postgres 16 and earlier copies row changes, but not sequence state. The subscriber's sequences stay at whatever value your schema load gave them, usually 1. Replicated rows still arrive with their real IDs, so the tables look fine. Then on cutover day the app calls `nextval()`, gets 1, 2, 3 and so on, and runs into existing primary keys. `GENERATED ... AS IDENTITY` columns have the same problem because sequences sit behind them. Upstream work on sequence sync is in progress, but none of it is in 16.

The fix belongs in the cutover window. Stop writes on PG13, let the subscriber catch up, then run this on the old side:

```sql
SELECT format('SELECT setval(%L, %s, true);',
              quote_ident(schemaname)||'.'||quote_ident(sequencename),
              last_value)
FROM pg_sequences
WHERE last_value IS NOT NULL;
```

Run the statements it prints on PG16 before you let writes in. Some teams add a buffer, for example `last_value + 10000`, in case a writer was missed.

**The twist:** the bigger trap is on the old primary, and it can hit you before cutover. As soon as a publication that publishes updates and deletes covers a table with no primary key and no replica identity, every `UPDATE` or `DELETE` on that table fails on production PG13 with `cannot update table ... because it does not have a replica identity`. `FOR ALL TABLES` publications pick these tables up without warning. Check before your agent runs `CREATE PUBLICATION`:

```sql
SELECT c.oid::regclass
FROM pg_class c JOIN pg_namespace n ON n.oid = c.relnamespace
WHERE c.relkind = 'r'
  AND n.nspname NOT IN ('pg_catalog','information_schema')
  AND (c.relreplident = 'n'
       OR (c.relreplident = 'd' AND NOT EXISTS (
             SELECT 1 FROM pg_index i
             WHERE i.indrelid = c.oid AND i.indisprimary)));
```

Anything this lists needs a primary key, `REPLICA IDENTITY USING INDEX` on a unique non-null index, or `REPLICA IDENTITY FULL`. FULL is slower, but it works.

Logical replication also skips DDL and large objects. Freeze schema changes during the run, and if you use `lo_*`, plan to copy those separately.

**"24 karat" is very likely an echo of a Byzantine coin's weight.**

Constantine I introduced the solidus around 309 at 72 to the Roman pound, about 4.5 g of nearly pure gold. It was divided into 24 *siliquae*, in Greek *keratia*, which means carob seeds. That's where "carat" comes from, and the usual explanation for why 24 means pure gold. The weight and fineness then stayed roughly the same for about 700 years, through plague, the Arab conquests and Iconoclasm. People called it the dollar of the Middle Ages, and it was accepted far outside the empire. The 6th-century merchant Cosmas Indicopleustes tells of a king of Sri Lanka comparing a Roman solidus with a Persian silver drachm and judging Rome the greater power, purely on the strength of the coin. Treat that story as a parable as much as a report.

Then it went wrong. Debasement started quietly under Michael IV in the 1030s and sped up as military and fiscal pressure grew, with Manzikert in 1071 as the low point. By about 1080 some issues held only around a third of their old gold, and the worst issues of the next decade were mostly base metal. Historians still argue over how much of this was panic and how much was a deliberate response to a growing economy that needed more coins.

**The twist:** Alexios I didn't restore the solidus. In 1092 he launched a new coin, the hyperpyron ("highly refined"), at about 20.5 carats. That's below the old standard, but it was honestly labelled and stable again. In effect he dropped a contract he could no longer keep and shipped a breaking change as a new major version under a new name, instead of quietly patching the old one. Your 13 to 16 cutover works the same way: a clean break with an explicit contract, rather than pretending nothing changed.

2) **Sideways:** the Byzantine fire-beacon chain across Anatolia and how it was switched off
3) **Wildcard:** why Venice's ducat, launched in 1284, took over the solidus's job and kept its gold standard for about 500 years

**The sharpest idea of the session:** systems rarely fail at the obvious door. They fail where trust was implicit, whether that's a wounded commander everyone depended on, a coin's unwritten 24-carat promise, or a sequence nobody thought to copy.
