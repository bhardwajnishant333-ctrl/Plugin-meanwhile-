## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
Until May 2000, the US military made your GPS lie on purpose.

The feature was called **Selective Availability**. The satellites added deliberate errors to the civilian signal, so a consumer receiver's position was only good to roughly 100 m (95% of the time). Military receivers could decrypt the correction. Clinton announced the end of it on 1 May 2000, and it was switched off overnight. Civilian accuracy jumped to about 10–20 m almost at once.

Two details make it stranger:
- **It was already turned off once, during the 1990–91 Gulf War.** There weren't enough military receivers, so troops were buying civilian units. The policy meant to protect the military was hurting it.
- **Civilians had already worked around it.** Differential GPS used ground stations at known positions to broadcast the error, and the Coast Guard ran DGPS beacons along US coasts. Anyone with a reference station could largely cancel the effect.

The twist: **geocaching started on 3 May 2000**, two days after the announcement. Dave Ulmer hid a container near Beavercreek, Oregon, to test whether a consumer receiver could now find something hidden in the woods. Your trail-runner market only exists because people found out within 48 hours that a cheap GPS could lead them to a specific tree.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
GPS is worst at measuring height, the one number trail runners argue about most.

**The mechanism is geometry.** Your receiver works out its position from the satellites it can see, and they are all above you, none below. Horizontally, satellites in every direction partly cancel each other's errors. Vertically, nothing pulls from underneath, so the error doesn't cancel. That shows up as VDOP being worse than HDOP. A common rule of thumb is that vertical error is about 1.5–2× horizontal, and worse in a canyon or under trees, where you lose the low satellites. There's a second problem: the receiver's clock error and your height affect the overhead satellites' ranges in similar ways, so the solver has trouble telling "I'm 10 m higher" from "my clock is slightly off."

**The datum layer:** GPS natively gives your height above the WGS84 ellipsoid, a smooth mathematical shape, not sea level. The gap between the two (the geoid) varies from about −106 m to +85 m around the world. Devices normally apply a geoid model such as EGM96 to give you "real" elevation. I'm not certain every GPX exporter states which height it writes, so treat `<ele>` from unknown sources as suspect.

**The twist:** total ascent has the same problem as distance. Add up every positive change in a noisy altitude signal and the jitter becomes fake climbing, because noise only ever adds. A flat run can log hundreds of metres. That's why watches use barometric altimeters, which are good for relative changes but drift when the weather changes, and why apps either use a dead-band or hysteresis threshold or replace your elevations with a terrain model. For your CLI, the ascent number depends on the threshold you choose, and different reasonable thresholds give different totals.
