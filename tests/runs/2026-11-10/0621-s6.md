## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
**Your agent is about to put one Go service into two containers. Unless your go.mod says 1.25 or later, each one may think it has every core on the node.**

How it works: before Go 1.25 (Aug 2025), `GOMAXPROCS` was set to the host's CPU count and ignored cgroup limits. Say a pod has a 2-CPU limit on a 64-core node. The Linux CPU scheduler hands out quota in 100ms periods, so that pod gets 200ms of CPU time per period. With up to 64 threads running Go code, it can spend that budget in about 3ms. Then it's throttled for the other ~97ms. That shows up as p99 latency spikes that look like GC trouble or network trouble but aren't. That's why `uber-go/automaxprocs` became a near-standard import. Go 1.25 builds this in on Linux: GOMAXPROCS now defaults to the lower of the core count and the container's CPU *limit*, and it re-checks over time if the limit changes. The new default depends on the `go` version line in go.mod, so a 1.25 toolchain building a module that declares an older version keeps the old behavior (I'm fairly sure, worth checking).

The twist: it reads the CPU *limit*, not the CPU *request*. A lot of Kubernetes teams have dropped CPU limits on purpose to avoid throttling. For those pods Go 1.25 changes nothing, and GOMAXPROCS goes back to the full node core count. So "upgrade Go" and "remove limits" are two fixes that each assume the other one isn't in place. With the split, you're about to write two fresh sets of resource specs. That's a good moment to pick one approach on purpose.

1) **Deeper**: how to check what your pods actually get (and why the throttling counter is the metric that gives it away)
2) **Sideways**: a European robin's compass may run on quantum entanglement, through a protein in its eye
3) **Wildcard**: why UPS routes its trucks to avoid left turns, and what that says about routing cost functions

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**A murmuration of 10,000 starlings turns as one, with no leader, and each bird is watching only about seven others.**

How it works: in 2008, Ballerini and colleagues (STARFLAG project, Rome, published in PNAS) reconstructed starling flocks in 3D from stereo photos. They found that each bird adjusts to a fixed *number* of neighbors, roughly 6 to 7, not to every bird within some distance. When the flock spreads out or bunches up, each bird still tracks its nearest seven, however far away they are.

The twist: a distance rule would break the flock apart the moment it thins out. Tracking a fixed number of neighbors keeps it together at any density. A 2010 follow-up (Cavagna et al., PNAS) found that the correlations are "scale-free": how far a change in direction carries grows with the size of the flock, so a turn starting at one edge can cross the whole flock. Every bird talks only to its neighbors, yet the information carries across the whole flock. That is the property people want from gossip protocols, and usually don't get.

1) **Deeper**: how they actually measured it (thousands of birds tracked in 3D, from just a few cameras)
2) **Sideways**: Braess's paradox. Adding a road can slow everyone down, and cities have improved traffic by removing highways.
3) **Wildcard**: in 1902 the French colonial government in Hanoi paid a bounty per rat tail. Hanoi ended up with tailless rats, alive and breeding.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**January 1919, Boston: a wave of molasses about 25 feet high, moving around 35 mph, killed 21 people.**

What happened: a storage tank in Boston's North End burst. It belonged to Purity Distilling, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and it held about 2.3 million gallons, which turned into a flood that wrecked buildings and an elevated railway girder. The tank had leaked since it was built in 1915. It was never properly tested by filling it with water. According to Stephen Puleo's history *Dark Tide*, the company painted it brown so the leaks wouldn't show. The day before, the temperature jumped from about 2°F to over 40°F.

The twist: the cold is what made it deadly. In 2016 a Harvard team, Nicole Sharp and colleagues, modeled it. The wave moved fast while the molasses was warm from the tank. As it spread into the freezing air its viscosity rose steeply and it went almost solid around people, so rescuers couldn't reach them. The company blamed an anarchist bomb. After about six years in court, a court-appointed auditor found the tank was structurally unfit, and Massachusetts went on to require that engineers sign and file the calculations for construction like this. The leaks had been the test result all along, and the company painted over them.

1) **Deeper**: why cold molasses traps you, and why swimming in syrup is a real physics question (and an Ig Nobel winner)
2) **Sideways**: tulip mania mostly didn't happen the way the legend says. Historian Anne Goldgar found almost no bankruptcies in the records.
3) **Wildcard**: "The Day of Two Noons", 1883. US railroads invented the time zones, and Congress didn't make them law until 1918.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
**The best-known bubble in history may have ruined almost nobody.**

What happened: prices for tulip bulbs really did spike in the Dutch Republic over the winter of 1636–37, then collapsed in early February 1637, starting at an auction in Haarlem. But in 2007 the historian Anne Goldgar published *Tulipmania*, built on notarial records, court papers and the archives of the guilds that ran the trading. She went looking for the ruined families, the bankrupt merchants and the suicides. She found essentially none she could tie to tulips. The buyers were a fairly small circle of well-off merchants and craftsmen. Much of the trade was contracts for bulbs still in the ground, agreed in taverns, which the Dutch mocked as *windhandel*, "trading in wind." After the crash, many contracts were simply never paid. A 1638 ruling in Haarlem reportedly let buyers walk away for a small fraction of the price (about 3.5% is the figure usually cited).

Where the legend came from: Charles Mackay's 1841 *Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds*. That gave us the sailor who ate a priceless bulb thinking it was an onion. Mackay took much of it from moralizing Dutch pamphlets printed right after the crash, which were sermons about greed, not financial reporting.

The twist: what tulip mania actually damaged was *trust*, not wealth. Goldgar's subtitle is *Money, Honor, and Knowledge*. The crisis was about whether your neighbor's word still meant anything, and whether anyone could know what a bulb was worth. The legend survives because every later bubble needs a cautionary tale, and a story that's too useful rarely gets checked.

1) **Deeper**: the economists' fight over it. Garber argued rare-bulb prices were rational, and Thompson argued the contracts quietly turned into options.
2) **Sideways**: Isaac Newton supposedly lost a fortune in the South Sea Bubble and said he could "calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people." The loss is real. The quote is shakier.
3) **Wildcard**: "The Day of Two Noons", 1883. US railroads invented the time zones, and Congress didn't make them law until 1918.
