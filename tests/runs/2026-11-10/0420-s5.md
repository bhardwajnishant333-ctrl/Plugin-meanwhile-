## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
**In 2009, people upgrading to Ubuntu's new ext4 filesystem found their config files wiped to zero bytes after a crash. The filesystem wasn't broken. It was doing exactly what POSIX allows.**

How it happened: apps like KDE saved settings the "safe" way. They wrote a temp file, then `rename()`d it over the original. ext4 had added **delayed allocation**, which holds data in memory for up to ~30–60 seconds before writing it to disk. The rename (a metadata change) could be journaled to disk *before* the file's contents were. If the power went out in that window, the new name pointed at an empty file. ext3 had mostly hidden the problem because its default mode flushed data within about 5 seconds. The bug report was Launchpad #317781. Ted Ts'o's reply, roughly, was that the apps were wrong: without an `fsync()`, POSIX never promised the data would survive. He still added the `auto_da_alloc` heuristic in kernel 2.6.30, which forces the data out on a rename-over or truncate, because he couldn't fix every app.

**The twist for you:** Go's `os.WriteFile` doesn't fsync, and neither does `os.Rename`. A crash-safe dotfile write needs four steps: write the temp file in the same directory, `f.Sync()`, rename, then fsync the *directory* so the rename itself is saved. And your agent's integration tests will pass whichever way you do it, because tests never pull the power cord. A green suite says nothing about crash safety.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**A robin's compass seems to run on electron spins in its eye. And it can be knocked out by radio noise millions of times weaker than anything known to affect living tissue.**

How it works: in 1972 Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko showed that European robins use an *inclination* compass. They don't sense north versus south. They sense the angle the field lines make with the ground, which tells them "toward the pole" or "toward the equator". The compass also needs blue-green light to work. In 1978 Klaus Schulten proposed a mechanism: light creates a **radical pair**, two molecules each holding one unpaired electron with linked spins. Earth's weak field (~50 µT) slightly changes how often the pair flips between spin states, and that changes the chemical result. In 2000 Thorsten Ritz pointed to cryptochrome proteins in the retina, and in 2021 Xu et al. (*Nature*) showed that robin Cry4a is magnetically sensitive in the lab. One caveat: no one has yet proven it is *the* sensor inside a living bird. The strongest evidence is still indirect.

**The twist, from Henrik Mouritsen's lab in Oldenburg (Engels et al., *Nature* 2014):** for years their robins couldn't orient on campus at all. It turned out that background electromagnetic noise from the city (roughly 50 kHz–5 MHz, from ordinary electronics) was scrambling the compass. They lined the huts with grounded aluminium and the birds oriented. When they removed the grounding, the birds lost their bearings again. They ran it double-blind because no one believed it.

That bears on your 25 minutes: when your agent's integration tests start flaking, the likeliest cause may be the environment the tests run in, not the code. A shared `$HOME`, leftover dotfiles from a previous run, a real `~/.gitconfig` leaking in. Give each test its own `t.TempDir()` as `HOME`, the code equivalent of the aluminium hut.

**The first evidence that the universe had a beginning was mistaken for pigeon droppings.**

How it happened: in 1964 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were trying to use Bell Labs' 20-foot Holmdel Horn Antenna in New Jersey for radio astronomy. They kept finding a faint, steady hiss of about 3.5 K of extra noise. It came from every direction, day and night, all year. They ruled out New York City, the military, and their own electronics. A pair of pigeons was nesting in the horn, so they removed the birds and scraped out what their paper politely called "white dielectric material". The hiss didn't change. Thirty miles away at Princeton, Robert Dicke's group was building an instrument to look for exactly this: leftover radiation from a hot early universe. When Penzias phoned him, Dicke reportedly hung up and told his team, "Boys, we've been scooped." The 1965 paper was titled, with great restraint, "A Measurement of Excess Antenna Temperature at 4080 Mc/s". They won the 1978 Nobel Prize in Physics.

**The twist:** what found it wasn't brilliance. They refused to label a reading "noise" until they had eliminated every local cause, pigeons included. Most labs would have subtracted the hiss and moved on. A commonly repeated figure is that about 1% of the static on an old analog TV was this same radiation. That claim is widely cited but I can't vouch for the exact number.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
**The universe's ingredient list was worked out by measuring the pitch of sound waves that stopped ringing 13.8 billion years ago.**

How it works: for the first ~380,000 years the universe was a hot plasma of photons and charged particles. Gravity pulled matter into clumps and radiation pressure pushed it back out, so the plasma rang with sound waves. When it cooled to about 3,000 K, electrons joined nuclei, light escaped, and the pattern was frozen into the CMB as ripples of about 1 part in 100,000. COBE first detected them in 1992 (a Nobel for Mather and Smoot in 2006). Measuring the *harmonics* took WMAP and then Planck:

- **Where the first peak sits** (about 1° across the sky): this tells you the geometry of space. The BOOMERanG balloon flight over Antarctica (results published in 2000) found it at the angle expected for a spatially flat universe.
- **How the 2nd peak compares to the 1st:** ordinary matter weighs the oscillation down, which makes the odd-numbered peaks louder than the even ones. That gives the amount of ordinary matter.
- **The 3rd peak:** dark matter doesn't feel radiation pressure, so it deepens the gravity wells without pushing back. That gives the amount of dark matter.

Planck's 2018 result: about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter and 68% dark energy, from fitting a six-parameter model to one sky map.

**The twist:** that same fit predicts how fast the universe expands today, about 67 km/s/Mpc. Measuring nearby galaxies directly with Cepheids and supernovae (the SH0ES team) gives about 73. Both camps have tried hard to break their own results. JWST re-checked the Cepheids and the gap didn't close. This is the "Hubble tension". Some of the recent tip-of-the-red-giant-branch measurements land in between, so it's an open question whether this is new physics or a hidden systematic error.

The lesson for an agent writing integration tests: two methods that each pass every internal check and still disagree are the most useful thing you can find. Either your model of the system is wrong or one of them has a bug that no single suite would catch. Your unit tests and integration tests should be able to disagree with each other.

2. **Sideways:** how "standard candle" supernovae revealed dark energy in 1998, the result two rival teams reached at the same time
3. **Wildcard:** why GPS needs both special and general relativity, or it drifts by ~10 km a day
