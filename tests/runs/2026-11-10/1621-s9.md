## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
Straight answer: I don't know of a reliable total. Teams don't publish spoke-failure counts, and any number I gave you would be made up. Modern pro wheels also carry fewer spokes than older ones (often 18–24 carbon or steel spokes per wheel), so a broken spoke matters less than a crash or a puncture.

Here's a cycling number that does hold up:

**Deep inside a big peloton, a rider can face only about 5–10% of the air drag of a rider riding alone.**

In 2018 Bert Blocken's group (TU Eindhoven / KU Leuven) ran computer airflow simulations of a 121-rider peloton and checked them in a wind tunnel. Riders in the middle and back of the bunch saw drag fall to roughly 5–10% of a lone rider's. That's like riding at 50–60 km/h in the drag you'd feel at about 12–19 km/h on your own. Older rules of thumb said a peloton saves 50–70%, which understated the effect by a lot.

**The twist:** the bunch becomes better shelter as it gets bigger, so being near the front costs much more than it looks. In a crit, every lap you spend in the top five puts you in a far harsher aero situation than a few places further back. Riders who look like they're "saving it" at position 15 really are. It's a lot like a fan-out in a pipeline: put the heavy load on the one task at the front, and everything behind it gets nearly free throughput.

1. **Deeper:** why the drag saving stops growing with peloton size, and where the best spot in the bunch is
2. **Sideways:** why "idempotent" models in dbt fail the same way a bad crit paceline does
3. **Wildcard:** why a spinning wheel's top half moves at twice the bike's speed while the bottom is momentarily stopped

No one publishes an exact figure for that. Companies don't report which Airflow version they run, and the yearly community surveys (Astronomer's "State of Airflow") report version share across everyone who answers, not by Fortune 500 status. Any percentage I gave you would be made up.

Here's what is verifiable, and it's odder:

**Airflow 1.x reached end of life on June 17, 2021, and yet "how many still run it" is a question people keep asking years later.**

The dates: Airflow 2.0 came out in December 2020. The 1.10 line stopped getting fixes, security patches included, in June 2021. Airflow 3.0 shipped in April 2025. A big company still on 1.x would be two major versions behind on unpatched software, and that keeps happening because the pipelines work. The scheduler keeps running, nobody touches it, and a full-day migration never makes it into a sprint.

**The twist:** what keeps old software around is usually a workflow nobody understands well enough to rewrite, not a missing upgrade path. Your current refactor is exactly that kind of job. Turning DAG logic into dbt models forces every unstated assumption into plain SQL, so the slow part is relearning what the old pipeline actually did, not porting the code.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**For decades, the US system that sends nuclear launch orders depended on 8-inch floppy disks, and the people running it said that made it safer.**

The system is SACCS (Strategic Automated Command and Control System), which passes emergency action messages to the forces holding nuclear weapons. A GAO report in May 2016 found it still ran on an IBM Series/1 computer from the 1970s and stored data on 8-inch floppies. The disk format dates from the early 1970s, and replacement disks were hard to get by then. The Air Force reported in October 2019 that it had replaced the floppies with what it called a "highly secure solid state digital storage solution" earlier that year. Details of the new storage weren't made public. I'm confident about those dates. I don't know how much of the rest of the system has since been modernized.

Operators defended the old setup on security grounds. It wasn't connected to any outside network, so attackers had no way in over the internet. Its hardware was also so old that very few people alive knew how to exploit it.

**The twist:** age protected the system in two ways: it was air-gapped, and almost nobody understood the hardware. Modernizing removes both protections, so a newer stack can leave you more exposed. Your refactor works the same way at a smaller scale. A pipeline nobody understands is fragile, but nobody depends on its internals either. Once the logic is clear, testable dbt models, people will start building on it. That's the payoff, and the new risk is that it's now visible and easy to change.

1. **Deeper:** how emergency action messages are authenticated (the codes system and the two-person rule)
2. **Sideways:** why the Voyager probes, launched in 1977 with about 69 KB of memory, are still being patched from Earth
3. **Wildcard:** a dead-hand-style automatic retaliation system really existed in the USSR, called "Perimeter"

Here's something real from your own discipline:

**Crits have a rule that removes the penalty for crashing for most of the race, then brings it back near the end.**

It's the free-lap rule. If you crash or have a mechanical in a crit, you can go to the pit, sit out a lap, and rejoin in the same position. Officials stop allowing free laps near the end, often around 5 laps to go. I'm sure the rule exists, but the cutoff depends on the race and the officials. After that point, a crash or a puncture ends your race.

**The twist:** this is an incentive designed by the rules. The free lap separates bad luck from your result until the finish matters, so the final laps carry very different stakes. If you're racing on a pipeline-builder's instincts, treat it like a retry policy that switches off at the end. Before the cutoff, failures cost almost nothing. After it, every risk you take counts for real.
