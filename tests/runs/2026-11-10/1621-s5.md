**Golden-winged and Blue-winged Warblers look nothing alike, but their genomes are about 99.97% the same.**

In 2016 Toews and colleagues sequenced both species. The paper's title says it all: *"Plumage genes and little else distinguish the genomes of hybridizing warblers"* (Current Biology). ## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**A 12-gram bird flies across the open Atlantic without stopping, and we only know for sure because of a tiny clock with a light sensor attached.**

The bird is the Blackpoll Warbler. In 2015 DeLuca and colleagues published "Transoceanic migration by a 12 g songbird" in *Biology Letters*. They fitted about 0.5 g geolocators to Blackpolls breeding in Vermont and Nova Scotia, then caught the same birds again a year later to download the data. The birds left the northeastern coast and flew roughly **2,270–2,770 km nonstop** over the ocean to the Caribbean. The flights took up to about **3 days**, averaging around 62 hours. Before leaving, Blackpolls put on so much fat that their body mass nearly doubles. Radar studies had pointed to this route since the 1970s, but the trackers showed it bird by bird.

**Here's the twist:** the tracker has no GPS and no radio. It only logs light levels with timestamps. The time of local noon gives longitude, and day length gives latitude. Near an equinox, day length is about the same everywhere on Earth, so latitude can't be worked out. Fall migration falls right around the September equinox, so researchers had to reconstruct the most important stretch of the trip through what was effectively a broken sensor. They did it by leaning on longitude and on known start and end points. It's offline-first logging with one known blind spot, and it still produced one of the best migration datasets we have.

**On a phone, the cheapest way to find out where you are is to ask which Wi-Fi routers are nearby, not to use GPS. Those routers' positions were learned from other people's phones.**

The Fused Location Provider is part of Google Play services, not AOSP, and it blends GPS, Wi-Fi, cell towers and sensors based on the priority you request. With `PRIORITY_HIGH_ACCURACY` it uses GPS, which is accurate to a few meters but power-hungry. A cold start can also take tens of seconds to get a first fix. `PRIORITY_BALANCED_POWER_ACCURACY` targets about 100 m ("block level"). It mostly works by scanning the MAC addresses (BSSIDs) and signal strengths of nearby access points and matching them against a crowdsourced database of router locations. That database is built from phones that had a GPS fix while they could see those routers. `PRIORITY_LOW_POWER` drops to about 10 km ("city level"), mostly from cell towers. `PRIORITY_PASSIVE` costs you nothing because it just reuses fixes other apps already asked for.

**Here's the twist:** this works like the warbler tracker. The `setMaxUpdateDelayMillis` setting on `LocationRequest.Builder` lets the system hold on to location fixes and deliver them to your app in batches, so the device doesn't wake up for each one. You trade real-time updates for battery, the same deal the geolocator makes. It also fails in a similar way. If someone moves house and takes their router, the database still places that router at the old address, and phones near it get confidently wrong locations until the crowd corrects it. The equinox problem had the same shape: a cheap signal that is usually right, with one predictable way of being badly wrong.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
**GPS satellites broadcast their orbit data at 50 bits per second. That's slower than a 1962 acoustic-coupler modem (300 baud), and it's the main reason a phone without help needs so long to get a first fix.**

A receiver needs two things. First it has to *find* each satellite's signal: for each one it searches 1,023 possible code offsets across a range of Doppler shifts, because the satellites move fast and your phone's oscillator is a little off. Then it has to *decode* that satellite's ephemeris, its precise orbit, which takes about 18–30 seconds of clean signal at 50 bps. The full almanac takes **12.5 minutes** to download. With no prior knowledge, a true cold start can take from 30 seconds to several minutes.

Assisted GNSS sidesteps both steps. Over the internet, often using the SUPL protocol, the phone gets the ephemeris, an approximate time and a rough position, which can come from the Wi-Fi and cell lookup we just covered. Now it knows which satellites are overhead and roughly what Doppler to expect, so the search shrinks from a big grid to a small window and nothing has to be decoded from space. The time to first fix drops to a few seconds. Chip vendors go further and ship *predicted* orbit data that stays valid for days.

**Here's the twist:** your GPS fix speed depends on your **data connection**. Abroad with roaming off, or with a stale offline cache, the same phone is noticeably slower outdoors under open sky. GPS is still "free" and works without internet, but in practice it runs on a network-supplied hint: the slow pipe from space gets bypassed by a fast pipe from the ground.
