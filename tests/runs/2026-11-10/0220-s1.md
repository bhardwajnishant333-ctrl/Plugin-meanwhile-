**A vehicle with no motor, powered only by wind, has gone straight downwind at 2.8 times the speed of that wind.**

The vehicle was Blackbird, a land cart built by Rick Cavallaro and John Borton. In 2010 at El Mirage dry lake, NALSA certified it going directly downwind at about 2.8 times wind speed. Lots of people with physics degrees called it a perpetual-motion scam. In 2021 Veritasium's Derek Muller made a $10,000 bet on it with UCLA physicist Alexander Kusenko. Kusenko conceded and paid.

How it works: the wheels drive a big propeller, and the propeller pushes air backward. At exactly wind speed the cart feels zero apparent wind, so a sail would have nothing to work with. But the ground is still moving past the cart at wind speed. So the cart isn't using "wind pushing on it". It's taking energy from the speed difference between the air and the ground: the wheels take power from the ground, and the prop uses it to push on the air. As long as the air and the ground move at different speeds, there's energy to collect.

**The twist:** you already do a version of this in your Laser. Going dead downwind you can never beat the wind, because your apparent wind drops toward zero. That's why fast boats (iceboats, foilers, AC75s) don't sail dead downwind. They reach across at an angle, make their own apparent wind, and gybe. That gets their downwind progress above wind speed. Blackbird does the same thing, except its "angled sail" is a propeller blade spinning in place.

1) **Deeper**: Blackbird also beat the wind going straight *upwind* (about 2.1x, in 2012), and the explanation is stranger
2) **Sideways**: a pgx v5 trap your agent might hit right now. pgx caches prepared statements by default, and that breaks behind PgBouncer in transaction mode unless you're on 1.21+ with prepared-statement support turned on (or you switch exec mode)
3) **Wildcard**: why UPS route optimisation famously avoids left turns, and what that says about "shortest" vs "cheapest" in route batching

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**UPS drivers deliberately take longer routes, and the company saves millions of gallons of fuel a year by doing it.**

UPS has planned routes to avoid left turns (in right-hand-traffic countries) since around the 1970s. A left turn usually means sitting idle waiting for a gap in oncoming traffic, and it puts the truck across that traffic, where the bad crashes happen. Around 2012 UPS was publicly crediting the policy with roughly 10 million gallons of fuel saved a year, plus a big drop in emissions and more packages delivered per day. Treat the exact figures as their claims. MythBusters tested it on a small scale and found the right-turn-heavy route was longer in miles but used less fuel. It isn't absolute: drivers still make left turns when the alternative is clearly worse. Its successor is ORION, UPS's route optimiser, which was rolled out through the mid-2010s. UPS says it saves on the order of 100 million miles a year.

**The twist, for your route-batching worker:** "shortest route" is the wrong goal, and the fix isn't a new weight on each edge. A turn's cost depends on *which road you came in on*. So the cost lives in the transition between edges, not in the edges themselves. A plain node graph can't represent that. Routing engines like OSRM deal with it by switching to an *edge-based graph*: every road segment becomes a node, and every allowed turn becomes an edge with its own penalty. That also makes costs asymmetric (A→B ≠ B→A), which quietly breaks any heuristic or solver that assumes a symmetric distance matrix. If your batching code clusters stops by straight-line or road distance and then sequences them, it may be optimising a cost your drivers don't actually pay.
