**Meanwhile:**
**Three retries sounds safe. Put three retries at each of three layers and one user click can become 64 calls to your bottom service, all of them during an outage.**

How it happens: each layer makes up to 4 attempts (1 call + 3 retries). Stack three layers that each retry on their own (edge, then pricing, then rates or carrier lookup) and the worst case is 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 attempts. Those attempts all arrive when the bottom service is already struggling, because failure is what triggers retries. Retries that each look sensible add up to a pile-on.

gRPC has a guard built into its retry policy, and most people never turn it on: `retryThrottling` in the service config. It's a token bucket with two settings, `maxTokens` and `tokenRatio`. Each failure removes 1 token, each success adds `tokenRatio`, and once the bucket drops to half or lower, retries stop. During a real outage the client stops piling on and only sends first attempts.

**The twist for your agent's current change:** deadlines and retries interact. In gRPC the deadline covers the whole call, retries included. In Go, the deadline only carries across hops if every outgoing call derives its context from the incoming `ctx`. One `context.WithTimeout(context.Background(), 2*time.Second)` in a client stub silently resets the clock, so the downstream service keeps working (and retrying) for a caller that has already given up. When the agent finishes, grep the diff for `context.Background()` in request paths. That one line is where this usually goes wrong.

**Meanwhile:**
**In a position battle, the car behind often holds the strategic option. The leader can only react.**

**The undercut.** You're stuck behind a rival. You can't pass on track, so you pit first. Your out-lap on new tyres runs against their in-lap on worn ones, and fresh rubber is often worth a second or more per lap at that point (a rough figure; it varies by track and compound). If that gain over the laps before they pit is bigger than the gap between you, you come out ahead when they make their stop.

**The overcut.** It's the reverse: stay out while your rival pits. It works when new tyres are slow to come up to temperature (cold track, hard compound), when the rival rejoins in traffic, or when your old tyres are still holding up in clean air.

**Why the leader is stuck reacting:** the standard defence is to "cover", pitting the lap after the attacker. That means the leader never picks the moment. And the deciding factor often isn't tyre pace at all but where you rejoin.

A bigger version of this is the offset strategy. At Barcelona 2021, Mercedes pitted Hamilton a second time, gave up the lead, and he chased Verstappen down on fresher tyres and passed him with a few laps to go. I'm confident about the outline, less sure of the exact lap numbers.

**How it connects to your service:** the overcut and a fresh failover replica have the same problem. Both look like the faster option on paper and both need warm-up first: cold tyres there, a cold cache and new connections for your service. If your pricing service caches carrier rates, a retry that lands on a freshly started instance can be slower than the original attempt it replaced.
