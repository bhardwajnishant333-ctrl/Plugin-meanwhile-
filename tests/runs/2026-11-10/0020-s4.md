**Before October 1987, the market didn't price crash risk at all.**

Black-Scholes, published in 1973, assumes log-normal returns. So for any one expiry, it gives the same implied vol at every strike. Up to 1987, S&P index options were in fact priced close to that, with a nearly flat smile. Mark Rubinstein documented this in his 1994 paper "Implied Binomial Trees" in the Journal of Finance.

Then came Black Monday, 19 Oct 1987. The Dow fell 22.6% and the S&P about 20% in one session. At normal daily vol of around 1%, that's roughly a 20-sigma move. Under a log-normal model, it essentially should never happen. Since then the index skew has never gone away: out-of-the-money puts have been priced at higher implied vol than calls for nearly four decades.

**The twist:** a model helped cause the crash it said was impossible. "Portfolio insurance" was dynamic hedging that copied a put by selling index futures as prices fell, and it was sold heavily by firms like Leland O'Brien Rubinstein. (Yes, the same Rubinstein.) In the crash, that hedging became forced selling into a falling market, and the falling market triggered more of it. It was a positive feedback loop the model left out by design. The skew you trade around today is basically scar tissue: the market's prior was updated by a single sample and never decayed.

1) **Deeper**: why the skew is so persistent, and who collects the premium (variance risk premium, and the case that put sellers get paid for wearing crash risk)
2) **Sideways**: intrinsic dimensionality, and why the LoRA you're running works at all. Fine-tuning a big model turns out to need far fewer free parameters than you'd guess.
3) **Wildcard**: Benford's law. Why the first digits of real-world numbers are skewed toward 1, and how that catches faked data

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**Fine-tuning RoBERTa-large to 90% of its full performance took about 200 parameters.**

That number comes from Aghajanyan, Zettlemoyer & Gupta (2020), "Intrinsic Dimensionality Explains the Effectiveness of Language Model Fine-Tuning." Their method builds on Li et al. (2018, Uber AI). You freeze the model, pick a random low-dimensional subspace through its parameter space, and train only the d coordinates inside that subspace. Then you look for the smallest d that reaches 90% of full fine-tuning performance. For RoBERTa-large on MRPC, that d was about 200, against roughly 355M parameters.

Two more findings stood out:
- **Pretraining shrinks it:** intrinsic dimension falls as pretraining goes on.
- **Bigger models need fewer:** larger models have *lower* intrinsic dimension, not higher.

LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) is that same idea made practical. Instead of a random subspace, you learn the low-rank update ΔW = BA directly. On GPT-3 175B it cut trainable parameters by about 10,000x and GPU memory by about 3x. The paper found very low ranks, even r=1–2 on the attention projections, often matched higher ones.

**The twist:** a bigger pretrained model is *easier* to steer, because fine-tuning mostly selects behaviour the model already has rather than teaching new behaviour. That has a limit. Biderman et al. (2024), "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less," found LoRA clearly behind full fine-tuning on continued pretraining for code and math, where the model really has to absorb new knowledge. In return, LoRA kept more of the base model's other abilities. **For your run:** if you're teaching format, style or task behaviour, a rank sweep will probably come out flat, and r=8–16 is plenty. If you're pushing in new domain knowledge, rank (or full fine-tuning) starts to matter.

1) **Deeper:** where low rank breaks, meaning what "learns less, forgets less" measured and how to tell which regime your data is in
2) **Sideways:** the same low-rank idea in your options book. Most eigenvalues of a stock correlation matrix are statistically noise (random matrix theory, Laloux et al. 1999), and that matters for any hedge built on them
3) **Wildcard:** why gold is yellow. Relativistic effects on its electrons shift its absorption into blue light, and without them it would look silvery like most metals

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**In a correlation matrix of 406 S&P stocks, about 94% of the eigenvalues could not be told apart from pure noise.**

That result comes from Laloux, Cizeau, Bouchaud & Potters, "Noise Dressing of Financial Correlation Matrices," published in Physical Review Letters in 1999. Plerou et al. reported the same thing that year. The test is the Marchenko–Pastur law. Build a correlation matrix out of pure random returns for N assets over T observations, with Q = T/N. Its eigenvalues all fall inside a fixed band: [(1 − 1/√Q)², (1 + 1/√Q)²]. With N = 406 and T = 1309 days, almost every real eigenvalue landed inside that band. Only a few stood clearly outside it. The largest is the "market mode," about 25x the top of the noise band. A handful of others line up with sectors.

**The twist:** the noise isn't harmless. It sits mostly in the *smallest* eigenvalues, and those are exactly the directions an optimizer likes. A minimum-variance or "optimal" hedge loads up on combinations that look nearly riskless in the sample. Those directions are just where the estimate is worst, so the risk you actually get out of sample comes in well above what the model predicted. Laloux et al. showed this gap directly.

**Your book, in numbers.** Take the 11 sector SPDRs:
- **1 year of daily data** (Q ≈ 23): the noise band is about [0.63, 1.46]. That's narrow, so most structure above it is probably real.
- **60 days of data** (Q ≈ 5.5): the band widens to about [0.33, 2.04]. Most of what looks like sector structure is now inside the noise.

So sector ETFs are a good hedge choice: you are hedging along the directions that do stand out from the noise. The danger is fitting hedge ratios on a short window, or across many instruments at once. A spread hedge that looks unusually cheap is the usual sign of this.

1) **Deeper:** fixing it. Eigenvalue clipping and Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage (2004) for stable hedge ratios, and which one suits a small ETF set
2) **Sideways:** why correlations jump toward 1 in a selloff. The market-mode eigenvalue spikes, sector hedges turn into plain beta, and implied correlation and dispersion trades are priced on exactly this
3) **Wildcard:** the "Byzantine generals" problem behind every consensus protocol came from a real NASA-funded fly-by-wire flight-computer project (SRI's SIFT, Lamport et al., 1982)
