## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
**A text index can be quietly wrong without one corrupted byte on disk. Your logical replication cutover is one of the few migration paths that protects you from that, and it can also be the moment the damage shows up.**

**How it works:** Postgres usually leaves text sorting to the OS's C library (glibc). glibc 2.28 (August 2018) updated its locale data to a newer ISO 14651 version, which changed the sort order of many strings. Spaces, hyphens and punctuation got the biggest shifts. Debian 10 and RHEL 8 shipped that version. A B-tree index built under the old order stays in the old order. The new glibc then searches it using the new rules, and two things go wrong:
- Lookups can miss rows that really exist.
- A UNIQUE index can let in duplicates, because the check looks in the wrong spot for the existing value.

Postgres gives no error for any of this. The PostgreSQL wiki page "Locale data changes" describes the issue.

**Why it matters for your migration:**
- **pg_upgrade or a physical replica on a new OS image:** the old indexes come across as they are, still in the old order.
- **Logical replication:** the subscriber builds every index fresh under its own glibc, so the new cluster starts clean.
- **The catch:** if your PG13 hosts ever went through that glibc jump without a REINDEX, a UNIQUE index may already hold duplicates. Logical replication's first copy loads the rows into tables that already have the unique index, so it would fail with `duplicate key`. That error would be years-old damage finally coming to light, not your agent's mistake. I can't tell you whether your cluster is exposed. It depends on its OS history.

The PG16 cluster has the same weak spot: a later CNPG image with a newer glibc could shift the sort order again. Since PG 15, Postgres records collation versions and warns when they change. PG 17 adds a "builtin" C.UTF-8 locale provider that doesn't depend on glibc at all.

1) **Deeper:** checking for this with `amcheck` (`bt_index_check` with `heapallindexed`) before the cutover, not after.
2) **Sideways:** João II's 1484 *junta dos matemáticos*. Once ships sailed south of the equator, Polaris sank below the horizon, so the navigators had to find latitude from the sun instead.
3) **Wildcard:** why the Gregorian calendar reform of 1582 still makes date handling go wrong when the historical dates are Portuguese.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**In 1484 the same expert panel that worked out how to navigate without the North Star also turned down Christopher Columbus. On the science, they were right.**

**The problem.** North of the equator, Portuguese pilots found latitude from the height of Polaris above the horizon. They applied a correction (the *regimento do norte*), because in the 1400s Polaris sat about 3.5° from the true pole. As they pushed down the African coast, Polaris got lower each night and then disappeared. Without a fixed reference point, they had no latitude.

**The fix.** João II brought together a *junta dos matemáticos*. The best-documented member is José Vizinho, a Jewish physician and astronomer who had studied with Abraão Zacuto. Their switch was to measure the sun instead of a star: take its height at local noon, then correct for its declination, meaning how far north or south of the equator it sits that day. The declination comes from tables. Roughly, latitude = (90° − noon altitude) ± declination, with the sign depending on season and hemisphere. The tables were built on Zacuto's *Almanach Perpetuum*, printed in Leiria in 1496. The instruments were the quadrant and the mariner's astrolabe. The rules were later printed as the *Regimento do Astrolábio e do Quadrante* (c. 1509). Columbus wrote in a margin note that Vizinho was sent to Guinea to test the method by measuring the sun himself. I'm not certain of the details of that trip.

**The twist.** The panel rejected Columbus because his numbers were wrong. He had shrunk the Earth and stretched Asia, putting Japan a few thousand miles west of the Canaries. The real distance is more like 12,000 miles. The *junta*'s skepticism was justified: a crew going that far could not have survived. Columbus got lucky only because an unknown continent was in the way. Portugal kept its proven route around Africa, da Gama reached India in 1498, and for a few decades that bet looked like the better one.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**The man whose tables guided Vasco da Gama to India was gone from Portugal by the time those tables were doing their most important work.**

**How it happened.** Abraão Zacuto was born in Salamanca around 1452 and wrote his main astronomical work in Castile, in Hebrew. In 1492 Ferdinand and Isabella expelled the Jews from Spain, and he crossed into Portugal, where João II, and then Manuel I, made him royal astronomer. The sources say Manuel consulted him before da Gama's voyage, and the expedition's latitude method depended on his declination tables. Vizinho put them into Latin, and a Jewish printer, Abraão d'Ortas, printed them in Leiria in 1496.

The same year, Manuel needed something from Spain: marriage to Isabella of Aragon, Ferdinand and Isabella's daughter. One of her parents' conditions was that Portugal remove its Jews. In December 1496 Manuel issued an expulsion decree. Then, unwilling to lose the people and the skills, he turned it into forced mass conversion in 1497, with children taken from their families that spring. Zacuto got out, apparently in 1497 or soon after, around the time da Gama sailed in July. He went to North Africa, wrote the chronicle *Sefer Yuhasin* in Tunis around 1504, and seems to have died in the Ottoman Levant around 1515. The details of his escape and death are uncertain.

**The twist.** Two expulsions, four years apart, carried out because of the same Spanish royal couple, first gave Portugal its best astronomer and then took him away. Portugal kept the advantage anyway, because the knowledge no longer lived only in his head. It was in printed tables and a written *regimento* that any trained pilot could use. The person was the single point of failure only until the method was written down. After that, losing him cost Portugal far less.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
Nice. A clean cutover.

Before you call it finished, check two things that matching row counts won't catch:
- **Sequences.** Logical replication in PG16 doesn't copy sequence values. If nobody synced them before cutover, the first inserts into the new cluster will fail with `duplicate key` on serial or identity columns. Compare `last_value` on old and new, and `setval` past the current max on any that are behind.
- **Planner stats.** These aren't replicated either. If the agent hasn't run `ANALYZE`, run `vacuumdb --analyze-in-stages` so the first queries don't get bad plans.

**The idea worth keeping:** Zacuto left Portugal and the ships still sailed, because his knowledge was in printed tables, not just in his head. Something that only exists in one place, whether a person, an old index or an untested flag, is a risk until someone writes it down or rebuilds it and checks it.

**For your work:** this migration built every index fresh under PG16's glibc. Write down which glibc version that was, so the next CNPG image bump doesn't quietly change your sort order.
