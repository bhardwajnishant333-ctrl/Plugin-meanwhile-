**Reindeer were still too radioactive to sell decades after Chernobyl, and lichen is the reason.**

Lichens have no roots. They get their nutrients straight from rain and dust, they don't shed tissue each year, and they live for decades. So whatever lands on them, they keep. When Chernobyl blew on 26 April 1986, caesium-137 rained onto the reindeer lichens (*Cladonia*) of Scandinavia. Reindeer live on those lichens in winter. Swedish and Norwegian herds, and the Sámi who herd them, were hit hard, and thousands of carcasses had to be destroyed. Cs-137 has a half-life of about 30 years, and the lichens kept passing it on. The same chain had already been measured in the 1960s, from bomb-test fallout: lichen to caribou to Arctic people, with people at the top carrying the highest levels.

**The twist:** the trait that makes lichens look fragile also makes them a very precise record. In 1866 William Nylander saw lichens vanishing from Paris's Jardin du Luxembourg and tied it to air pollution. They've been used as air-quality sensors ever since. An organism that grows a few millimetres a year behaves like an append-only log of the sky: it rarely forgets, so it's easy to read and also easy to poison.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**Strictly speaking, no lichen has a scientific name. Every "lichen name" belongs to a fungus.**

Linnaeus put all lichens into a single genus, *Lichen*, in *Species Plantarum* (1753), and treated them as one kind of plant. His student Erik Acharius later split them into many genera (*Lichenographia Universalis*, 1810) and became known as the father of lichenology. In 1867 Simon Schwendener argued that a lichen is a fungus and an alga living together. Many lichenologists resisted that idea for decades, because it broke the rule of one organism, one name.

The compromise is still in the rulebook today. Under the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants, a lichen's name applies to its fungal partner only. So *Xanthoria parietina* formally names the fungus. The green partner (often *Trebouxia*) has its own separate name, and the thing you look at through your hand lens has no formal name of its own.

**The twist:** Linnaeus's system assumes that every name refers to one lineage, so a composite thing has nowhere to go. That got worse in 2016. Spribille and colleagues reported in *Science* that basidiomycete yeasts live in the cortex of many macrolichens, which means a third partner with no part in the name. So a field guide is really a catalogue of fungi that happen to farm algae, and the "species" count (roughly 20,000, depending on who's counting) is a count of fungi, not of the partnerships.

1) **Deeper:** whether the 2016 yeast finding has held up, and what later studies found.
2) **Sideways:** your refactor has the same naming problem. In SwiftData, a `@Model` is one class, but what your budget actually means lives in the relationships between models, such as the delete rules and inverses. Those are the parts most likely to break quietly in a Core Data migration.
3) **Wildcard:** why some biologists say *you* don't have a proper name either, given how many of your cells aren't human.
