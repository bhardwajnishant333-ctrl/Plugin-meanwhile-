## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
**A honeybee swarm picks its new home by quorum, not by everyone agreeing. Sometimes it splits in two.**

When a colony swarms, roughly 10,000 bees hang on a branch while a few hundred scouts search for cavities. A scout that finds a good site dances for it, and better sites get longer dances. Each time she flies back to check, she dances fewer waggle runs. So support for any site fades on its own unless new recruits keep it going. Scouts also head-butt bees dancing for rival sites. This "stop signal" was described by Seeley and colleagues in *Science* in 2012.

The decision isn't counted on the branch. It's made at the site itself. Once about 15–30 scouts are there at the same time, they go back and start "piping," which tells the swarm to warm its flight muscles to about 35°C. Within an hour the swarm takes off. No bee ever compares all the options. (Thomas Seeley's *Honeybee Democracy*, 2010. The thresholds are approximate.)

**The twist:** bees accept a known failure in exchange for speed. If two sites reach quorum at once, the swarm can lift off, stall, and split, or settle back on the branch and start over. That rare split is the cost of not waiting for everyone to agree. Your offline scanners face the same choice. They can't reach consensus about whether a ticket was already used, so the real design question is how you detect and resolve a double-boarding once the devices sync. Trying to prevent it entirely won't work.

1) **Deeper:** how the stop signal is basically the bees' form of cross-inhibition, the same math as neurons choosing between two options
2) **Sideways:** why Room's default WAL mode means copying just the `.db` file off a device can quietly lose your latest scans
3) **Wildcard:** how Wall Street's dark pools work, the opposite of a waggle dance

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
I can't give you an exact number for western Norway, and I don't think one exists. As far as I know, nobody has measured foraging distance per kilo of heather honey for that region. Any precise figure would be made up.

**What is real is a widely repeated estimate: about 55,000 miles of flying per pound of honey, which works out to roughly 190,000 km per kilo, close to five times around the Earth.** It's a back-of-envelope number, not a measurement. It multiplies an assumed nectar load per trip (around 40 mg), an assumed number of trips, and an assumed round-trip distance. Change any one of those and the answer moves a lot. Published versions range from about 150,000 to over 200,000 km per kilo.

**Why heather would move it, though I can't tell you which way overall:**
- Distance to forage dominates the total. Hives set right on the moorland would fly far less than the average. Hives a few kilometres from the heather would fly far more.
- Heather nectar sugar content varies a lot with weather. Thin nectar means more trips per kilo, because the bees have to evaporate more water.
- Heather honey is thixotropic, a gel that turns liquid when stirred. That's why it has to be pressed out of the comb instead of spun. It affects the beekeeper's workload, not the flight miles.

With your two hives you could actually estimate it yourself. Count returning foragers per minute at the entrance during the heather flow, weigh a hive daily on a scale, and pace out the distance to the nearest heather. That's three numbers, and you'd have a western-Norway figure nobody has published.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**About 800 km is the best-known figure, but it's an estimate, not an exact number.** It comes from Neukirch's 1982 study. It found that summer foragers seem to have a lifetime flight budget of roughly 800 km. Once they've flown about that far, their flight muscles can no longer refuel well enough, and they die soon after. Ragged wings are a visible sign, but the limit appears to be mostly metabolic. Individual bees vary a lot, so treat 800 km as an order of magnitude.

Summer workers usually live about 5–6 weeks. Only the last stretch of that is foraging, usually a week or two, sometimes up to three. That's where the 800 km gets spent.

**On "what percent die on their last trip":** I can't give you an honest percentage, and part of the reason is the question itself. Any forager who dies away from the hive has, by definition, died on her last trip. What researchers actually measure is how many foragers come back from each trip or each day, and those numbers change a lot with weather, predators, pesticides and how far away the forage is. I don't have a reliable baseline figure I'd stand behind. What is well established is that most foragers die out in the field, not inside the hive. That's partly why you rarely find a pile of old workers at the entrance.

That "flight budget" idea is a lot like a battery with a fixed number of charge cycles. The colony doesn't stop its oldest workers from flying. It just keeps producing younger ones to replace them.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
**A winter bee and a summer bee have the same genes and hatch from the same kind of egg. One lives about six weeks and the other can live about six months. What decides it isn't the season. It's whether the colony has brood that needs feeding.**

In late summer the queen lays less and brood rearing tapers off. Young workers that emerge then have no larvae to feed, so the protein they would have spent on brood food stays in their bodies. The result is the "winter bee" (sometimes called *diutinus*). It has an enlarged fat body and high levels of a yolk protein called **vitellogenin**.

Vitellogenin is the key to the long life. Gro Amdam's group (with Omholt and others, from about 2003 onward) showed that it works as a protein store and also helps protect cells from oxidative damage. Vitellogenin and juvenile hormone suppress each other. High vitellogenin keeps juvenile hormone low, and that keeps the bee from switching to foraging. When vitellogenin falls, juvenile hormone rises, she starts foraging, and she has only days to weeks left. So for a summer bee, the aging clock really starts when she begins foraging, more than when she was born.

**The twist:** the switch appears to run partly in reverse. In experiments where foragers were forced back into nursing, some regained vitellogenin. Amdam's lab also reported that age-related learning decline in some of these bees improved. I'm confident that study exists, but I wouldn't overstate how big the effect was. Bee aging seems to depend more on what job she's doing than on how old she is.

For your two hives, this is why late-summer conditions decide whether you get through the winter. Mite load and pollen in August and September decide how healthy your winter bees are. Bees damaged in that window don't live through a long western-Norway winter.

1) **Deeper:** how varroa specifically lowers vitellogenin in developing winter bees
2) **Sideways:** caloric restriction and "aging by job" in mice, ants, and naked mole-rats
3) **Wildcard:** why a queen can live years on the same genes as a worker
