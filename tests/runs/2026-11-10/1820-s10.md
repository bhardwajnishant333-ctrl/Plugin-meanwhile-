## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
Your red copper glaze isn't really coloured by copper. It's coloured by copper particles too small to see.

Copper oxide in a glaze fired with plenty of oxygen gives you greens and turquoises, because the copper stays dissolved in the glass as ions. In reduction you starve the kiln of oxygen. That strips oxygen from the copper, and it comes out of the glass as tiny specks of metallic copper, about tens of nanometres across. At that size the specks soak up green light and let red through. Gold does the same thing: tiny gold particles turn glass ruby red, which is how "gold ruby" glass got its name. The Romans used it too. The Lycurgus Cup, from the 4th century AD, looks jade green in daylight and glows red when lit from behind, because of gold and silver nanoparticles in the glass.

The twist is that the colour depends on how big the particles are, not just on what they're made of. Two pots can have the same glaze recipe and the same amount of copper. If one cools a bit faster or sits nearer a draft, its particles grow to a different size, so one pot comes out blood red and the other liver brown or nearly clear. This is why Chinese copper reds were so prized and so hard to repeat. The recipe was known, but the particle size was in the hands of the firing. So in reduction glazes, the firing decides the colour as much as the glaze does.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
The pale or greenish rim on a copper red pot is copper that got away.

There are three reasons, and they add up.

**The glaze runs.** At top temperature the glaze is a slow liquid, and it creeps down the pot. That leaves the rim with a thin coat and puts the thick coat lower down, often as a pooled roll near the foot. Pooled copper red usually turns darkest, close to oxblood.

**Copper evaporates.** Copper is slightly volatile at stoneware temperatures, so some of it boils off from the surface. A thin layer has no reserve to replace what it loses. You've probably seen the evidence: pink blushes on the white pots that sat next to a copper red in the kiln. That pink is copper that left one pot and settled on another.

**The surface re-oxidises.** As the kiln cools and air gets back in, the outermost skin of glaze can take oxygen back. The tiny metal specks there dissolve into ions again, and those ions read as clear or faintly green. Where the glaze is thick, only a thin outer skin is affected and the red underneath survives. At a thin rim, that skin is nearly the whole layer.

Chinese collectors named this edge. On Qing dynasty Lang ware (Kangxi period, early 1700s), the clear white rim is called "lamp-wick mouth" (dengcao kou), and it's considered a sign of the real thing. I'm fairly sure of the term, but check the romanisation before you quote it.

**The twist:** the red sits inside the glaze, not on its surface. It needs a thick layer of glass on top to shield it from both the air and the heat. That's why some potters do a "copper sandwich": a thin copper glaze with a clear glaze over it. The red ends up deeper inside the glaze, and deeper means safer.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
The most valuable glaze effect in the world shows up on only three complete bowls, and no one has proven exactly how it was made.

They're called **yōhen tenmoku**. They came from the Jian kilns in Fujian, China, during the Song dynasty (roughly the 12th–13th century). The bowls are thick and black because the glaze is very rich in iron. Most Jian bowls came out as "hare's fur", with fine brown streaks, or "oil spot", with silvery dots. A tiny number came out with dark spots ringed by halos of shifting blue, violet and gold, like looking into a night sky. Three complete examples survive. All three are in Japan and all are National Treasures: one at the Seikadō Bunko in Tokyo, one at the Fujita Museum in Osaka, and one at Ryūkō-in, a sub-temple of Daitoku-ji in Kyoto.

The name tells the story. "Yōhen" is usually understood to have first been written 窯変, "kiln transformation": something the fire did that the potter didn't plan. Later it was written with a character meaning "shining" or "star". The colours aren't pigment. The best explanation is that they're structural colour: a very thin film on the surface bends light the way a soap bubble or an oil slick does. So the colours shift as you move the bowl. The exact chemistry and firing conditions are still debated, and modern potters keep announcing reproductions that experts keep arguing over.

**The twist:** the bowls were made in China, but they've survived only in Japan. In Song China, black bowls mattered because of tea contests, where whisked white foam was judged against a dark bowl. Very few yōhen bowls or pieces of them have turned up on Chinese soil. A broken piece was reported from Hangzhou, the old Song capital, in 2009, though I'm not sure of the details. Japan took them in through Zen monasteries and the tea world, and treated them as treasure. One theory holds that Chinese potters saw such unpredictable kiln changes as flawed or even bad luck and threw them away, but that's a theory, not a record. Either way, the same bowl could be a reject in one culture and a National Treasure in the next. The bowl stayed the same. Only who was looking changed.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
Some of the most valuable bowls in Japan started out as cheap Korean rice bowls.

By the 1500s, Japan's warlords showed off with flawless imported Chinese ceramics: celadons, and tenmoku like the yōhen bowls. A line of tea masters changed that. It started with Murata Jukō in the 15th century and peaked with **Sen no Rikyū** (1522–1591). They built a style called *wabi-cha*: small rooms, simple tools, beauty in what is rough and imperfect. Rikyū brought in **Ido bowls**, plain everyday wares from Joseon Korea, crackled and uneven. One of them, the "Kizaemon" Ido, is now a National Treasure, kept at Kohō-an in Daitoku-ji. That's the same temple complex as one of the yōhen bowls.

Then he went further and commissioned a tile-maker, **Chōjirō**, to make tea bowls. Chōjirō shaped them by hand without a wheel, fired them low, and pulled them out of the kiln while still hot. That was the start of **Raku**. The Raku family is still making bowls today, and is now in its 16th generation.

The stakes were real. Tea bowls and caddies became political currency. Oda Nobunaga and then Toyotomi Hideyoshi gathered famous utensils and handed them out as rewards, sometimes in place of land. Rikyū served as Hideyoshi's tea master. Hideyoshi also built a portable tea room covered in gold, about as far from wabi as you can get. In 1591 he ordered Rikyū to kill himself. Historians still argue about why.

**The twist:** Rikyū didn't make luxury cheaper. He moved where the value lived. Before him, the value was in the object: perfect, imported and rare. After him, it was in the person choosing: a rough bowl became priceless because the right person had picked it and named it. That's branding in its purest form. The object barely changed, but who endorsed it and the story around it changed everything. It's the same move as the yōhen bowls, done on purpose this time.
