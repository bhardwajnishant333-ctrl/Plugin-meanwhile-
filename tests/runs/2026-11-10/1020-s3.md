## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
**The Byzantine gold coin kept the same weight and purity for about 700 years. Then it lost almost all its gold in roughly 40 years, and the fix was a hard cutover to a new version.**

- **The coin:** Constantine introduced the solidus around 309 AD, struck 72 to the Roman pound (~4.5 g, near-pure gold). The Greeks called it the nomisma. It stayed essentially unchanged into the 11th century and was the coin traders trusted from Italy to the Indian Ocean.
- **The decline:** Debasement started around the 1040s under Michael IV and Constantine IX. Under Michael VII (1071–78) it got bad enough that he was nicknamed *Parapinakes*, "minus a quarter." The story is that a nomisma bought a quarter-measure (pinakion) less grain than it used to. By the 1080s the "gold" coin was mostly alloy. Sources disagree on exact percentages, but the late issues were close to token coins.
- **The fix:** Alexios I didn't patch the old coin. In 1092 he issued a new one, the *hyperpyron* ("refined by fire"), at about 20.5 carats. It wasn't the old standard, but it held. Several debased coins stayed in circulation alongside it at official exchange rates, which looks a lot like running a compatibility shim during a migration.
- **The twist:** The 700 years of stability was the failure mode. Because everyone treated the nomisma as unchangeable, nothing was built to handle it changing. Once it slipped, the only clean option was a new version plus a published conversion table, not a gradual in-place fix. Your 13 → 17 move by logical replication works on the same principle: stand up a new primary and cut over, instead of mutating the old one.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**The best-known problem in fault tolerance was named for marketing reasons, and the first name was "Albanian."**

- **Why it needed a story:** Pease, Shostak and Lamport had already proved the core result in 1980 ("Reaching Agreement in the Presence of Faults," JACM). The work came from SRI's SIFT project, fault-tolerant flight-control computers for NASA. Lamport later wrote that he thought Dijkstra's dining philosophers got attention because of the cute story, so he wanted a story too. He has also credited Jim Gray's "Chinese Generals" framing of the two-generals problem as an influence.
- **The rename:** In his notes on his own papers, Lamport says the generals were first Albanian, since Albania was closed off and nobody would object. A colleague pointed out that Albanians exist outside Albania. Lamport switched to Byzantine because those generals were long dead. The 1982 TOPLAS paper, "The Byzantine Generals Problem," made the name stick.
- **The math:** With unsigned ("oral") messages, tolerating *f* traitors needs at least **3f+1** generals. With 3 generals and 1 traitor, the loyal ones can't tell who is lying. With unforgeable signed messages, agreement works with any number of traitors.
- **The twist:** No Byzantine called themselves Byzantine. They were *Rhomaioi*, Romans. Hieronymus Wolf, a German scholar, popularized "Byzantine" in 1557, about a century after Constantinople fell. Enlightenment writers then turned the word into a synonym for scheming. So the CS term rests on a label the empire never used, chosen for PR. Your stack doesn't assume Byzantine faults at all: the etcd under your EKS control plane runs Raft, which needs **2f+1** nodes and assumes nodes may crash but never lie. That f-node gap between 2f+1 and 3f+1 is the price of tolerating liars, and most production systems choose not to pay it.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**The proof works by showing that one loyal general sees exactly the same messages in two scenarios that require opposite actions.**

**Setup:** one commander (C), two lieutenants (L1, L2), one traitor, and only unsigned ("oral") messages. The two rules: (1) all loyal lieutenants make the same decision; (2) if the commander is loyal, they obey the commander.

**Scenario A:** C is loyal and orders *attack* to both. L2 is the traitor and tells L1 "C said *retreat*."
L1 sees: C says attack, L2 claims C said retreat. Rule 2 requires L1 to **attack**.

**Scenario B:** C is the traitor. It sends *attack* to L1 and *retreat* to L2. L2 is loyal and truthfully tells L1 "C said *retreat*."
L1 sees: C says attack, L2 claims C said retreat. These are the **same messages as in A**, so L1 must decide the same way and **attacks**.

L2's view in B is the mirror image: C says retreat, L1 claims C said attack. That's identical to a scenario where C is loyal and says retreat and L1 is the traitor, so L2 must **retreat**. Two loyal lieutenants now disagree, which breaks rule 1. QED.

**Scaling up:** Assume some protocol works for 3m generals with m traitors. Have each of 3 generals simulate m of them, so one of the 3 simulates all m traitors. That gives a working 3-general protocol with 1 traitor, which the proof above rules out. So n must be ≥ 3m+1.

**Why signatures fix it:** In Scenario A, L2 can't forge "C said retreat" with C's signature. L1 sees an unsigned claim, discards it, and the two scenarios become distinguishable.

**The twist:** The core technique here is indistinguishability. Nothing lies to the honest node about what *it* did. The problem is that two different realities produce the same view from its seat. FLP (1985) uses the same move to prove deterministic consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system with even one crash. Your cutover has the same problem: from the new primary's seat, "the old primary has stopped writing" and "the old primary is slow and still writing" can look identical. That's why cutover runbooks enforce a stop: fence the old primary or revoke its write access, instead of trusting it to go quiet. CNPG supports fencing an instance, which is worth checking against your rehearsal steps.
