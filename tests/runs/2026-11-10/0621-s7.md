## Turn 1 — Meanwhile
**In synth control voltage, adding is the same as transposing, and that only works because Bob Moog made pitch exponential.**

Moog's standard is 1 volt per octave: each extra volt doubles the oscillator's frequency. A circuit inside the oscillator called an exponential converter turns a straight-line voltage into an exponential pitch. Hearing works on ratios, so +1V is an octave and +1/12V is a semitone, at any starting pitch. That is why you can mix a sequencer and an LFO with a passive mult and it stays in tune. Not everyone did it this way. Korg (MS-20) and Yamaha (CS series) used Hz/V, where voltage is proportional to frequency. Buchla used 1.2V per octave, so each semitone is a tidy 0.1V.

**The twist:** under Hz/V, adding a fixed voltage adds the same number of Hz to every note. That shifts low notes by large intervals and high notes by tiny ones, so a chord pulls out of tune when you transpose it. Turning pitch into log space changes musical math from multiplying into adding, and adding is what you can do with a simple resistor mixer. Eurorack runs on 1V/oct because Moog's choice made the cheapest circuit also the correct one.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**Your VCO is a thermometer that happens to make sound. That is why tuning drifts more the further you play from where you tuned.**

The exponential converter is a transistor pair. Its current grows exponentially with base voltage, scaled by the "thermal voltage" V_T = kT/q, about 26 mV at room temperature. One octave needs V_T·ln2, about 18 mV at the base, so the 1V/oct input is divided down to roughly that size. But V_T is proportional to absolute temperature. Near 300 K it changes about 1/300 for every °C, roughly 0.33%/°C. So the volts-per-octave scale stretches as the circuit warms up.

There are two separate drift sources, and each has its own fix:
- **Offset:** the transistor's saturation current I_s changes sharply with temperature. A matched pair cancels it, because both transistors drift together. That's why designs use monolithic pairs (LM394, MAT02, THAT300). Some designs also hold a transistor array at a fixed temperature, using one transistor on the chip as a heater.
- **Scale:** the V_T term can't be cancelled that way. The fix is a **tempco resistor** in the scaling network, at about +3300–3500 ppm/°C, so the gain rises with temperature as fast as V_T does.

**The twist:** a scale error isn't fixed. It grows with distance from the note you tuned. 0.33% per °C is tiny at your reference note, but five octaves away it's about 20 cents per °C. That's why "let it warm up" is real advice, and why you tune in the middle of the range you'll play. Taking the tempco out doesn't make a VCO warmer or more musical. It makes it track badly, more so the further it goes from the reference.

1) **Deeper:** the CEM3340, Doug Curtis's single-chip VCO (used in the Prophet-5 Rev 3), which put the temperature compensation on the chip
2) **Sideways:** the same V_T-proportional-to-temperature physics runs the bandgap voltage reference in nearly every chip, your iPhone's SoC included. There it's used on purpose to cancel temperature drift.
3) **Wildcard:** SwiftData and Core Data's NeXT-era lineage, while your migration runs
