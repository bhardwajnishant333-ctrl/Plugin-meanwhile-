**Your copper reds are nanotechnology. Chinese potters were doing it about 600 years before the word existed.**

Copper in a glaze can fire green or red, and the kiln's atmosphere decides which. When there's plenty of oxygen, copper stays dissolved in the glass as ions, and those give you greens and turquoises. In reduction, the kiln is starved of oxygen and grabs oxygen back from the glaze. The copper then clumps into tiny metal particles, far smaller than a speck of dust, and those particles scatter light as red. Ming potters had mastered this by the early 1400s (the prized "sacrificial red" wares of the Xuande reign).

The Romans found the same effect with gold. The Lycurgus Cup in the British Museum is 4th-century glass that looks jade green when lit from the front and glows red when lit from behind. In 1990 researchers showed why: it contains gold and silver particles about 70 nanometres across. Ruby glass made from gold uses the same trick.

**The twist:** in these glazes and glasses, the colour doesn't come from what's in the pot. It comes from how big the particles grow. The same copper in the same recipe can come out green, red, muddy liver-brown or clear depending on how fast the kiln cools and how hard it reduces. That's why two pots from one bucket can come out as strangers, and why copper red has a reputation for being temperamental. You aren't mixing a colour. You're growing one.

1) **Deeper:** why copper reds often have a clear "skin" at the rim, and what that tells you about where the oxygen got in.
2) **Sideways:** why your invitations can look different on your new shop pages than on paper. Screens make colour with light and printers with ink, and some colours exist on one but not the other.
3) **Wildcard:** America's favourite dinnerware once had uranium in its orange glaze, and the US government took it away during WWII.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**In 1943 the US government took the colour out of America's favourite plates, because the colour was made from uranium.**

Fiesta launched in 1936 from the Homer Laughlin China Company in West Virginia. The idea was cheerful solid colours that you mix and match on one table. The star colour was "Fiesta Red", which is really a deep orange, and it got its glow from uranium oxide in the glaze. Uranium was then just a fairly obscure colourant. Once the Manhattan Project began, the government controlled the supply, and red disappeared from the line in 1943. It came back in 1959, made with depleted uranium. Original Fiesta was discontinued in the early 1970s and relaunched in 1986 with uranium-free glazes.

Old red pieces still make a Geiger counter click, and collectors film it. The usual advice: displaying one on a shelf is fine. Don't eat off it regularly, especially acidic food like tomatoes or citrus, which can draw material out of an old glaze.

**The twist:** the 1959 red was made from depleted uranium, the leftover after uranium is enriched for reactors and weapons. So for about a decade, a cheerful orange dinner plate was one way of using up nuclear-industry waste. A glaze colour can depend on world politics as much as on chemistry: the most famous colour in American dinnerware disappeared because of a war and came back because of the waste that war left behind.

About your thrift plate: "Fiesta-looking" covers a lot. There are many lookalikes, and anything from 1986 on is uranium-free. Check the back for a Homer Laughlin or "fiesta" mark. Note that the orange-red colour is the one that matters most, though some other early colours are also reported to contain smaller amounts. I'm not certain enough of the exact mark styles by year to date your plate from a description.

1) **Deeper:** how to tell vintage Fiesta from the 1986 relaunch and from the lookalikes, and what a cheap radiation meter would actually show.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**The best-known pricing trick in pop psychology rests on a classroom experiment, and the stricter tests behind it give mixed results.**

The famous version comes from Dan Ariely's *Predictably Irrational* (2008), which used a real Economist subscription offer:
- Web only: $59
- Print only: $125
- Print + web: $125

Nobody picks "print only", because it's obviously worse than the bundle at the same price. That's the point of it. Ariely reported that among 100 MIT students, 84% chose the $125 bundle. When he removed the decoy, only 32% did and most took the $59 option. The idea goes back to Huber, Payne and Puto (1982), who called it the "asymmetric dominance effect". The decoy is built to be beaten by your target option and only by that one, so the target looks like a clear win.

**The twist:** in 2014 Frederick, Lee and Baskin published "The Limits of Attraction" in the *Journal of Marketing Research*. They found the effect often shrank or vanished with realistic products, especially when people could see or feel what they were comparing. The decoy works best when value is abstract and hard to judge. When people can compare directly, they mostly just pick what they want.

**For your pricing page:** wedding invitations sit in between. Couples can see your designs, so the artwork does the persuading. A package, though, is a bundle of things that are hard to judge (paper weight, foil, envelope liners, number of proof rounds), and that's where a decoy has room to work. If you try one, make it clearly worse than your favourite package on one point and similar in price, for example the same price without the liners. Don't let it compete with your cheapest tier. Then watch what people actually choose instead of trusting the textbook.

1) **Deeper:** the "compromise effect", the related and better-supported reason so many people pick the middle of three options.
2) **Sideways:** "wedding tax". Do vendors really charge more once they hear the word "wedding", and what does the evidence say?
3) **Wildcard:** why a glaze can crack into a web of fine lines years after firing, long after the pot passed every check.
