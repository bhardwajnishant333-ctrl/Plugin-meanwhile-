**Some won chess endgames are legally a draw, and endgame databases have a name for them: "cursed wins."**

Here's how it works. Endgame tablebases solve chess outright for small numbers of pieces. They start from every possible checkmate and work backwards. The 7-piece Lomonosov tablebases, finished around 2012, include a forced mate that takes about 549 moves with perfect play. I'm sure of the scale, less sure of the exact number. The problem is the 50-move rule. If 50 moves pass with no capture or pawn move, either player can claim a draw. Most of those monster wins never get a capture or pawn move often enough to beat that clock, so in a real game they're draws.

FIDE did try making exceptions. For a while in the 1980s some endgames got extended limits (KRB vs KR among them). The exceptions were dropped around 1992, partly because computers kept finding new long wins. I'm confident about the overall story, but check the exact dates if they matter to you.

**The twist:** Ronald de Man's Syzygy tablebases, the ones engines use today, record what the rules actually allow, not just what's true on the board. A position can be a "cursed win" (winning, but not inside 50 moves) or a "blessed loss" (lost, but you survive the clock). They also use a different measure, DTZ (distance to the next capture or pawn move), instead of DTM (distance to mate). That means a perfect engine sometimes passes up the fastest mate, because the fastest mate isn't the one that beats the clock.

"Correct under the model" and "correct under the rules that get enforced" are different targets. Your focus migration has the same split: jsdom can pass every focus test while a real screen reader does something else.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**In a combobox, the highlighted option never actually has focus.**

Here's how it works. The ARIA Authoring Practices combobox pattern keeps real DOM focus on the `<input>` the whole time, so typing keeps working. Your listbox tracks the "current" option, and the input points to it with `aria-activedescendant="opt-42"`. The screen reader follows that id and reads the option out. The browser doesn't follow it. To the browser, that option is just a `<div>`.

That gap causes a set of bugs that are easy to miss:
- **No auto-scroll.** Real focus scrolls the element into view, and virtual focus doesn't. Arrow past the visible list and the highlight disappears off-screen unless you call `scrollIntoView({ block: 'nearest' })` yourself.
- **No `:focus-visible`.** The highlight style has to come from your own state, like `data-active` or `aria-selected`, not focus pseudo-classes.
- **Stale ids.** If the list is virtualized or filtered, the active option can unmount while `aria-activedescendant` still points at its id. The screen reader goes quiet and nothing throws an error.
- **The click/blur race.** On `mousedown` over an option, the input blurs, your blur handler closes the popup, and the `click` lands on nothing. The usual fix is `preventDefault()` on the listbox's `mousedown`.

**The twist for your migration:** a shared focus hook usually assumes roving tabindex, which moves real DOM focus from item to item. That's right for menus, toolbars and grids, and wrong for an editable Combobox, where moving focus off the input breaks typing. A select-only Select can use either approach, which is exactly why one hook can look like it works for both. jsdom won't catch this, because it has no accessibility tree. One cheap test catches most of it: after every key press, check that `document.getElementById(input.getAttribute('aria-activedescendant'))` exists and sits inside the listbox. Run it with filtering and virtualization too. A stale id is the cursed win again: your tests say it works, the real rules say it doesn't.

1. **Deeper**: why the `mousedown` fix works, and the cases where it breaks (touch, text selection)
2. **Sideways**: NVDA and JAWS "browse mode" vs "focus mode", and why screen reader users experience your component as two different UIs

**The most famous endgame study in chess exists because the first published solution was wrong.**

In 1895 Georges Barbier published a position in a Glasgow newspaper, the *Weekly Citizen*, with the answer "draw." A reader showed it was a win. He was Fernando Saavedra, a Spanish priest living in Glasgow, and the position has carried his name ever since. Here is the standard form. White has Kb6 and a pawn on c6. Black has Ka1 and Rd5.

1. c7 Rd6+ 2. Kb5 Rd5+ 3. Kb4 Rd4+ 4. Kb3 Rd3+ 5. Kc2 Rd4!

Black's trick is that if White queens with 6. c8=Q, then 6...Rc4+! 7. Qxc4 is stalemate.

6. **c8=R!** The rook can't be stalemated that way. Now 6...Ra4 is forced to stop Rc1 mate, and 7. Kb3! puts both the rook and the king under threat at once, and Black loses either way.

**Twist:** the stronger piece fails here exactly because it's stronger. The queen covers the extra squares that turn Black's position into stalemate, so taking less power is what removes Black's last resource. Barbier's mistake wasn't a miscalculation. He just didn't consider promoting to anything except a queen. That "of course it's a queen" assumption is still the main way strong players miss underpromotions.
