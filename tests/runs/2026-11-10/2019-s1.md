**Meanwhile:**
**In 1944, the date for D-Day depended partly on a brass machine of pulleys and wires that added sine waves together. Your app is doing the same calculation.**

Tides aren't predicted by simulating the ocean. They're predicted by splitting the tide into "harmonic constituents": steady sine waves, each tied to one astronomical cycle. M2 follows the moon (period about 12.42 h), S2 follows the sun (exactly 12 h), K1 and O1 are the daily ones, and so on. Each tide station has its own amplitude and phase for each wave, worked out from long tide-gauge records. To predict a tide you add the waves up.

Lord Kelvin built the first mechanical tide predictor in 1872–73. Each constituent was a crank driving a pulley, and one wire running over all the pulleys added them together, so a pen drew a year of tides in hours. In 1921 Arthur Doodson expanded the tide-generating forces into 388 terms. In 1944 he used machines like Kelvin's in Liverpool to predict the tides for the Normandy beaches. As I recall, he had to estimate those beaches' constituents from sparse nearby data, so treat that detail as approximate. Today NOAA's standard predictions use up to 37 constituents per station.

**The twist for your app:** the astronomical tide can be fully predicted years ahead from a few dozen numbers per station, with no network and no model. What it can't predict is weather. Wind and air pressure (storm surge) can move water levels a lot, and in a tidal race the current matters more than the height. So the honest output for a sea kayaker is "this is the astronomical prediction", not "this is what the water will do."

1) **Deeper:** why currents are much harder to predict than heights, and why kayakers get caught out at narrows
2) **Sideways:** iron gall ink, which goes on pale and darkens on the page through the same iron chemistry that slowly eats medieval manuscripts
3) **Wildcard:** why the Bay of Fundy's tides are so huge. It isn't the moon pulling harder there; the bay is the right length to resonate with M2.

**For about a thousand years, Europe's standard ink went on almost invisible and turned black only after it was on the page. The same reaction that makes it permanent slowly destroys the paper.**

How it works: crush oak galls (growths a wasp causes on oak trees) to get tannins and gallic acid. Add iron(II) sulfate, called "green vitriol" or "copperas", and some gum arabic to thicken it. Fresh, the iron(II)–gallate complex is pale and fairly soluble. Once it's on paper, oxygen turns it into an iron(III) complex. That one is dark, insoluble, and sits inside the fibres, so you can't wash it off. That's why it was the ink for legal documents and why it lasted from late antiquity into the 20th century. Bach's and Leonardo's manuscripts are written in it.

The catch is "ink corrosion." Leftover sulfate makes sulfuric acid, which breaks down the cellulose. Spare iron ions speed up oxidation (Fenton-type radical chemistry), which attacks it too. After a few centuries the dark strokes turn brittle and can fall out, so you're left with the letters cut out as holes. Conservators treat badly affected manuscripts, Bach's among them, with a calcium phytate bath that binds the free iron and then neutralises the acid.

**The twist:** "blue-black" ink got its name from this lag. Makers added a blue dye so the writer could see the line straight away, and the true black came in over hours. It's a placeholder for a result that arrives late, like a loading skeleton on a screen. Modern fountain-pen iron galls such as Diamine Registrar's, Rohrer & Klingner Salix and Scabiosa, and Platinum Classic still use that trick, with much less iron and acid. They're usually safe in a pen you flush regularly. Avoid leaving them sitting in a pen for months, especially one with a steel nib.

1) **Deeper:** why the ink is permanent. Iron(III) and the gallate groups form a polymer network, and some of that chemistry is now used in coatings research.
2) **Sideways:** why Prussian blue, found by accident in Berlin around 1706, is also an iron compound and is now used as an antidote for radioactive caesium
3) **Wildcard:** how historians date undated letters by iron gall corrosion and ink chemistry, and the forgeries that tests exposed
