Hi Inés. 25 minutes, Elixir and bees. Here's one to start.

**When your bees swarm, the colony picks the best new home, but almost no individual bee ever compares two sites.**

Thomas Seeley spent decades on this (his book is *Honeybee Democracy*). A swarm hangs off a branch while a few hundred scouts search. A scout that finds a cavity comes back and dances for it, and she dances longer for a better site. Each time she goes back and returns, her dance gets shorter until she stops. So support for a mediocre site dies out on its own, while a good site keeps winning new recruits. Most scouts only ever see the one site they're dancing for.

The swarm doesn't wait for every bee to agree. Scouts sense a quorum at the site itself. When enough of them (roughly 20 to 30) are there at once, they go back and signal the whole swarm to warm up and leave. In a 2012 *Science* paper, Seeley's group showed a second mechanism: scouts head-butt dancers who are promoting a *rival* site, and each butt makes the dancer more likely to stop. That's cross-inhibition, the same pattern neurons use when choosing between options.

**The twist:** the head-butt exists to prevent a split brain. A swarm that flies off toward two sites at once has a real problem, because the queen can only go one way. Bees solved netsplits with a quorum read at the destination plus active suppression of the losing side, not with a leader. That's close to the trade-off you face with distributed Erlang: be fast enough, never split, and stay correct "most of the time" by design.

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**Evolution already tuned the quorum, and it landed near the best speed/accuracy trade-off, not on maximum accuracy.**

**The experiment.** Seeley ran swarms on Appledore Island off Maine, which has almost no natural tree cavities, so the only homes were nest boxes he put out. When he offered one good box (about 40 litres) and several mediocre ones (about 15 litres), swarms picked the good one most of the time. Accurate, but not perfect.

**The knob.** The quorum is a threshold. Set it low and scouts commit to whichever okay site gets a crowd first. That's fast, but the swarm often ends up in a draughty 15-litre box. Set it high and the swarm sits on a branch for days, exposed to rain and predators and burning through the honey it carried. That's accurate, but a dead swarm doesn't benefit from accuracy. Passino and Seeley (2006) modelled the full process and found that thresholds close to what real bees use sit near the sweet spot. Take the exact number loosely: the counts I've seen are roughly 15 to 30 scouts at the site, depending on how it's measured.

**What a quorum triggers isn't a decision. It's a warm-up.** Scouts that sense the quorum go back and start "piping", a vibrating signal that tells the other bees to warm their flight muscles to about 35°C. That takes something like an hour. Then "buzz runs" set off the liftoff. So the swarm separates *deciding* from *committing*, with a deliberate delay in between. During that delay, rival dancers can still be head-butted into silence.

**The twist:** the bees are tuned for the cost of being wrong and the cost of waiting together, not for being right. You probably make the same choice in your own code whenever you pick a timeout or retry count. Most of us pick it by gut. The bees' answer: let what waiting actually costs decide how much certainty you buy.

1) **Deeper**: what happens when a swarm *does* split, and how they recover
2) **Sideways**: the "speed–accuracy trade-off" in human brains, where the same maths shows up in neurons choosing between two options
3) **Wildcard**: why the queen, despite the name, has no say in any of this

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
Bees can wait. Here's the bug.

**Cause:** the upload is now named `:avatar`, but your progress callback only matches `:photo`. LiveView calls `handle_progress/3` with the upload's name as the first argument. When no clause matches, you get a `FunctionClauseError`. The agent most likely renamed the upload in `allow_upload` or the template and missed this function (or the other way round).

**Fix:** pick one name and use it everywhere. If `:avatar` is the name you want to keep:

```elixir
# mount
|> allow_upload(:avatar, accept: ~w(.png .jpg .jpeg), max_entries: 1,
     auto_upload: true, progress: &handle_progress/3)

# line 41
def handle_progress(:avatar, entry, socket) when entry.done? do
  # consume_uploaded_entry(socket, entry, fn %{path: path} -> ... end)
  {:noreply, socket}
end

def handle_progress(:avatar, _entry, socket), do: {:noreply, socket}
```

**Add that second clause even after you fix the name.** `handle_progress` fires on every progress update, not just at 100%. With only the `when entry.done?` clause, the first partial update (say 37%) will crash with this same error. Your test probably sent one completed chunk, so it only hit the name mismatch.

**Find any other spots the rename missed:**

