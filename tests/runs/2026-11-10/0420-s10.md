Hi Mireille, happy to. 15 minutes it is.

**The cones in your kiln don't measure temperature. They measure something potters call "heatwork": temperature and time together.**

**How it works:** A pyrometric cone is a small slender pyramid made of a carefully mixed ceramic recipe. It softens and bends over once it has absorbed enough heat, and how fast you get there changes the point where it bends. Going by the Orton company's charts, cone 10 bends at roughly 1285°C if you fire slowly (about 60°C an hour) and about 20°C hotter if you fire fast (about 150°C an hour). The German ceramic chemist Hermann Seger developed the modern cone system in the 1880s. Before cones, Josiah Wedgwood (yes, the pottery Wedgwood) built a pyrometer in the 1780s from little clay cylinders that shrank in the heat, and it got him elected to the Royal Society. His scale went badly wrong at the top end and claimed temperatures for melting iron that were thousands of degrees too high. I don't trust my memory of his exact numbers, so I'll leave them out.

**The twist:** a cone gives the more honest reading. Your glaze doesn't care what the thermocouple says at one moment. It cares how much melting and reacting it has had time to do. That's why two kilns that both "hit 1285" can give you different pots, while two kilns that both bent cone 10 usually won't. The clay itself is the measuring tool.

1) **Deeper:** why a slow cool can turn a glossy glaze matte, as crystals grow while it cools
2) **Sideways:** the chemistry that makes old paperback pages turn yellow, and why some books are still white after 200 years
3) **Wildcard:** the Chinese porcelain secret that Europe spent about a century trying to copy, and the alchemist held prisoner until he cracked it

## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**Europe's first true porcelain came from a man locked up because he had promised a king gold.**

**How it happened:** Johann Friedrich Böttger was a young alchemist who boasted that he could turn base metals into gold. Augustus the Strong, ruler of Saxony, heard about it and took him into custody around 1701 to make good on the claim. When no gold came, Böttger was put to work with the scientist Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus. Tschirnhaus had been working on a different "white gold": Chinese porcelain, which European courts were spending fortunes on. Europe had been trying to copy it for well over a century. The Medici workshops in Florence made a glassy imitation in the 1570s and 80s, but it wasn't the real thing. Around 1708 the Saxon team finally made hard-paste porcelain. The key was kaolin, a pure white clay, fired far hotter than European kilns usually ran. The Meissen factory opened in 1710 inside a castle, and its workers were sworn to secrecy. Böttger died in 1719, aged 37, still more or less under watch.

**The twist:** "the secret" was mostly a material and a temperature, not some hidden trick. Chinese potters at Jingdezhen mixed kaolin with porcelain stone, and a French Jesuit, Père d'Entrecolles, described the process in letters home in 1712 and 1722. Even so, the secret wouldn't stay locked up. In 1719 a Meissen worker, Samuel Stöltzel, slipped off to Vienna and helped start a rival factory there. Augustus was so obsessed that in 1717 he traded about 600 soldiers to the King of Prussia for a set of large Chinese vases, still known as the "dragoon vases." The kaolin in the porcelain slip you use comes straight from this story.

1) **Deeper:** why porcelain turns translucent when you hold it to the light, while stoneware the same thickness doesn't
2) **Sideways:** how Meissen's crossed-swords mark became one of the first trademarks, and the history of counterfeit marks that followed
3) **Wildcard:** the "blue" in blue-and-white porcelain was cobalt imported from Persia, and a shortage of it shows up as a visible shift in Ming pottery

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**Your bowls glow because there's less inside them for light to bounce off, not because they're whiter.**

**How it works:** Light gets blocked mainly by scattering. Every time it crosses a boundary between two materials that bend light differently, it gets knocked off course. The worst boundaries are tiny air pockets, because air and clay bend light very differently. In the kiln, the feldspar in porcelain melts into glass, and that glass flows into the gaps between the clay particles. Once it's fully fired, porcelain holds very few pores and is largely glass, so light passes through with few bounces. Kaolin also contains very little iron, so it absorbs little of the light that does get through.

Your mug clay works against you on all three counts. Stoneware usually has more pores, coarser grains and sometimes grog (each a fresh boundary), and more iron and titanium, which soak up light.

**Thickness matters more than it looks.** Light fades roughly exponentially as it passes through a material. Halve the wall thickness and you can let through far more than twice as much light. A thin bowl against a chunkier mug wall can be the gap between a glow and nothing.

**The twist, which ties back to the cones:** underfired porcelain won't glow even when it's thin. The glass hasn't finished filling the pores yet. Translucency is a heatwork reading you can hold in your hand. If a bowl you expected to glow comes out dull, the kiln probably didn't give it enough heat for long enough.

1) **Deeper:** bone china, why the English put cow bone in porcelain, and why it's so translucent
2) **Sideways:** paper has the same problem. Why vellum and tracing paper are see-through, and why you can sometimes see the next page's printing through a picture book
3) **Wildcard:** some Chinese potters pierced porcelain and filled the holes with glaze ("rice-grain" ware), so the light shows through as tiny windows
