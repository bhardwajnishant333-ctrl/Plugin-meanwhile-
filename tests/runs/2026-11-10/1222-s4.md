## Turn 2 — Meanwhile
**Onions are one of the few farm goods you can't trade as futures in the US. Congress banned them in 1958 and the ban is still law.**

What happened: in 1955 two traders, Vincent Kosuga and Sam Siegel, bought a huge share of the onion futures traded on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange. They also bought a large share of the actual onions in Chicago storage, often put at about 30 million pounds. They used that position to squeeze the market. Then, the usual story goes, they switched sides: they bet on falling prices and pushed their stored onions onto a market that couldn't take them. They also had onions shipped out of Chicago and back in to make the glut look bigger. A 50-lb bag that sold for about $2.75 in August 1955 was down to about 10 cents by March 1956. That was less than the empty sack was worth. Growers were ruined and lobbied hard. A Michigan congressman named Gerald Ford sponsored the Onion Futures Act, and it was signed in 1958.

The twist: the ban didn't calm onion prices. Holbrook Working, an economist at Stanford, studied it around 1960 and found onion cash prices swung less in the years futures traded than in the years without them. Later studies found the same after the ban. Shutting the contract didn't remove the price risk. It removed the place where that risk was priced and could be hedged. Congress did it again in 2010: the Dodd-Frank Act banned futures on movie box-office takings before they launched. In both cases, lawmakers saw the visible price swing and banned the market, but the market wasn't what caused it.

## Turn 3 — Meanwhile
**What futures did for onions: they paid someone to hold them over winter.**

The mechanism comes from Holbrook Working's theory of the price of storage (1949). Onions come in at harvest in the autumn but people eat them all year. To bridge that gap, someone has to buy them in October and hold them until March. Without futures, that warehouse takes the full price risk, so it stores less. Onions flood the market at harvest, which crushes the price. By spring they're scarce, and the price spikes. With futures, the warehouse buys the physical onions and sells a March contract against them. Now its profit doesn't depend on where onion prices go. It depends only on the gap between spot and March. If that gap pays more than the cost of storage, it stores. Storage then links the autumn price to the spring price, and the seasonal swing flattens.

What Working measured (1960): onion cash prices from the 1930s to the 1950s. He compared seasonal price ranges in years with active onion futures against years without them, and the ranges were smaller in the futures years. Roger Gray came back to it after the ban in "Onions Revisited" (1963) and found the same thing. To be fair to the other side: these were before-and-after comparisons, and weather and planted acreage changed too, so it's strong evidence but not a controlled experiment.

The twist, for you in particular: Working argued that hedgers aren't really getting rid of risk. They are betting on the basis, meaning the gap between spot and futures, rather than on price. The warehouse holding onions is long the calendar spread, whether it thinks of itself that way or not. Spread traders like you fill the other side of that trade. By banning the contract, Congress took away the spread that paid someone to store onions, and the risk went back to the farmers.

## Turn 4 — Meanwhile
**A 10-minute test for whether your checkpoint writes text during silence. It runs on CPU, so your 4×A100 run isn't touched.**

The idea: give the model audio with no speech in it and see whether it writes anything. Run it on base `openai/whisper-small` and on your latest checkpoint. The difference between the two is what matters.

```python
import torch, gzip, numpy as np
from transformers import WhisperProcessor, WhisperForConditionalGeneration

SR = 16000
def probes():
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    yield "digital_silence", np.zeros(SR * 20, np.float32)
    for db in (-60, -45, -35):
        a = 10 ** (db / 20)
        yield f"white_{db}dB", (rng.standard_normal(SR * 20) * a).astype(np.float32)
    # Most important: add 20-30 real non-speech gaps cut from your court audio
    # (room tone, paper, chairs, coughs). Synthetic noise != a courtroom.

def check(ckpt, device="cpu"):
    proc = WhisperProcessor.from_pretrained(ckpt)
    model = WhisperForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained(ckpt).to(device).eval()
    tok = proc.tokenizer
    ns_id = next(i for t in ("<|nospeech|>", "<|nocaptions|>")
                 if (i := tok.convert_tokens_to_ids(t)) != tok.unk_token_id)
    sot = tok.convert_tokens_to_ids("<|startoftranscript|>")
    for name, audio in probes():
        feats = proc(audio, sampling_rate=SR, return_tensors="pt").input_features.to(device)
        with torch.no_grad():
            logits = model(feats, decoder_input_ids=torch.tensor([[sot]], device=device)).logits
            p_ns = logits[0, -1].softmax(-1)[ns_id].item()
            ids = model.generate(feats, language="pl", task="transcribe")
        text = tok.decode(ids[0], skip_special_tokens=True).strip()
        cr = len(text.encode()) / max(1, len(gzip.compress(text.encode())))
        print(f"{name:18} p_nospeech={p_ns:.2f} cr={cr:.1f} | {text[:80]!r}")

check("openai/whisper-small")
check("/path/to/your/checkpoint")
```

What to look for:
- **Any text at all on a probe** means it's making things up. Count the hits; don't just glance at them.
- **`p_nospeech`**: OpenAI's own decoder treats a segment as silent when this is above about 0.6 and the average log-prob is below −1.0. If base Whisper scores high and your checkpoint scores low, fine-tuning has damaged its sense of when nobody is talking.
- **Compression ratio above 2.4**: that's OpenAI's threshold for repetition loops, the "the the the" failure.

Why fine-tuning can cause this: if every training clip contains speech and has a non-empty transcript, the model never sees an example where the right answer is nothing. Its no-speech signal can fade without anyone noticing, and WER on speech-only eval clips won't show it.

If it looks bad, there are two fixes. First, mix non-speech clips with empty targets into the training data. A few percent is a common rule of thumb, not an established number, so tune it. Second, put a VAD such as Silero in front of Whisper at inference time, so long gaps never reach the decoder.

1) **Deeper:** timestamps and the no-speech token: what Whisper's training format teaches it about gaps, and what HF fine-tuning scripts drop by default
2) **Sideways:** TF32 on A100: your matmuls may be running at about 10-bit mantissa precision, and when that matters
