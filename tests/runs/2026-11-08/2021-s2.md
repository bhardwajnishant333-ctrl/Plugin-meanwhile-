Your React Query hooks run on a caching rule that was written down 16 years ago for proxy servers.

"Stale-while-revalidate" comes from RFC 5861, which Mark Nottingham wrote in May 2010. It let a CDN or proxy serve an expired response right away and fetch a fresh copy in the background. React Query takes that HTTP header idea and applies it to every query key. The default `staleTime` is `0`, so data is stale the moment it arrives. That's why a query refetches whenever a component using it mounts, the window regains focus, or the network reconnects. Unused data stays in the cache for 5 minutes (`gcTime`, which was called `cacheTime` before v5) and is then garbage-collected.

The twist is that "stale" never meant "wrong". It's a claim about how fast that part of your domain changes, and each query should make its own claim. The clinic's vet roster or species list can safely stay fresh for hours. Open appointment slots should probably stay at `0`. And no `staleTime` setting will stop a double-booking. Only the server can enforce that, because the cache is just a display.

The "+2" in your 3+2 is patented. Bobby Fischer holds US patent 4,884,255 for a game timer, granted in 1989.

Under the old sudden-death clocks, you could lose a completely won position only because your flag fell. Fischer's fix was to add a fixed amount of time to your clock after every move. However low you get, each move earns you a little time back. The clock got its first big public test at his 1992 rematch with Spassky in Yugoslavia. FIDE later adopted increments as standard. Before that, David Bronstein had proposed a "delay" version: the clock waits a few seconds before it starts counting down, but you can never bank that time.

Lichess has a formula for this. It sorts time controls by estimated game length: initial time plus 40 times the increment. For 3+2 that's 180 + 80 = 260 seconds, which is why it counts as blitz and 3+0 sits at the edge of bullet.

The twist is that a Fischer clock is a token-bucket rate limiter. Your initial time is the bucket's capacity, and the increment is the refill earned on each action. Bronstein delay is the version that can't burst: you get a per-request allowance that disappears if you don't use it. So when you're scrambling with 4 seconds left and premoving, you're a client trying to stay under the limit by sending lots of cheap requests.

Your Lichess rating is really two numbers, and the hidden second one decides how far each game moves the first.

Arpad Elo's system was adopted by the USCF in 1960 and by FIDE in 1970. It gives every player one number and treats it with the same confidence whether the player has 10 games or 10,000. In 1995 the statistician Mark Glickman added a rating deviation (RD) and called the result Glicko. Glicko-2, which Lichess uses, also adds volatility. Your rating's rough 95% range is rating ± 2×RD. New accounts start at 1500 with a very large RD, so early games swing the rating by a lot. Lichess shows a "?" after ratings it still treats as provisional. I believe the cutoff is an RD above about 110, but I haven't checked that figure.

The key part is that RD grows while you're not playing. Take a few weeks off and the system becomes less sure of your strength, so your first games back move your rating more. Elo has no way to express "I've lost track of you."

The twist: a number reported without its uncertainty is half a measurement. Your Playwright suite has the same problem. "The e2e passed" is like a rating with RD = ∞, because one green run says almost nothing about a test that flakes 1 time in 20. Track the pass rate over many runs and ask how sure you are about it, not just whether the last run was green.
