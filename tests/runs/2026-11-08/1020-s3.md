**The "Byzantine" in the Byzantine Generals Problem nearly said "Albanian."**

How it got the name: In 1982, Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease published the result most consensus systems still rest on: with lying nodes you need at least 3f+1 nodes to survive f traitors. Lamport has written that his first choice was "Albanian generals"; Jack Goldberg pointed out real Albanians might object, so he picked an empire that no longer existed. Byzantium got the label partly from centuries of Western historians (Gibbon in the 1700s) treating it as a byword for intrigue — an odd fit for an eastern empire that lasted ~1,000 years after Rome fell in the West, largely through diplomacy and bureaucratic redundancy.

Why it matters for your cutover: logical replication assumes every node is honest. The danger isn't a lying node, it's a loyal one that quietly leaves something out. As of Postgres 17, logical replication doesn't copy sequence values, DDL or large objects. 2 TB of rows can arrive perfectly and the first insert after cutover still fails with a duplicate key because the 17 cluster's sequences are at 1. Check the runbook has a `setval` per sequence, with headroom, before writes switch.

1) Deeper: the 3f+1 proof in two minutes, and why Raft and Postgres don't need it
2) Sideways: the Nika riots of 532, when Constantinople nearly overthrew Justinian over chariot-racing fan clubs
