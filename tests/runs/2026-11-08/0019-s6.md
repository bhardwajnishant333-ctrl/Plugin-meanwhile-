**The "21 days to form a habit" rule started as a plastic surgeon noticing his patients, not as a study.**

In his 1960 book *Psycho-Cybernetics*, Maxwell Maltz wrote that people needed "a minimum of about 21 days" to get used to a change, such as a new face after surgery. Over time, "a minimum of about" got dropped. In 2009, Phillippa Lally's team at UCL actually measured it. 96 volunteers picked one daily behaviour and tracked how automatic it felt. The median time to reach automaticity was 66 days, and the range ran from 18 to 254 days, depending on the person and the habit.

**Every chord you've ever played on guitar is out of tune, and the frets are built that way on purpose.**

Stack 12 pure perfect fifths (frequency ratio 3:2) and you should land back on your starting note, seven octaves up. You don't. You overshoot by about 23.5 cents, almost a quarter of a semitone. This gap is the Pythagorean comma. No arrangement of pure intervals closes the circle. Equal temperament fixes this by spreading the error over all 12 notes: every semitone is exactly the 12th root of 2. Fifths come out only 2 cents flat, which nobody hears. Major thirds suffer most. A pure third (5:4) is 386 cents, while the equal-tempered third is 400, about 14 cents sharp. That's why a major chord on guitar has a faint shimmer, or "beating", that a barbershop quartet singing pure thirds doesn't have.

**The twist:** tuning by the 5th–7th fret harmonics, as many players are taught, tunes some string pairs to pure intervals. That can leave chords sounding worse than tuning each string to an electronic tuner. The more carefully you tune by ear, the more out of tune your chords can sound.

1) Deeper: why jazz voicings that skip the 3rd, or put it up top, sound "cleaner" because of this
2) Sideways: how Bach's *Well-Tempered Clavier* fits in. "Well" didn't mean "equal", and historians still argue over which tuning he meant
3) Wildcard: back to code. Why `0.1 + 0.2 != 0.3` in Swift is the same problem of a circle that won't close

**The navigators saw it coming for months. The problem was that nobody had a way to say "stop".**

Mars Climate Orbiter launched in December 1998. Every so often it fired small thrusters to dump momentum built up in its reaction wheels, which is called angular momentum desaturation. Lockheed Martin's ground software (a module called SM_FORCES) reported those small pushes in pound-force seconds. JPL's navigation software read them as newton-seconds, as the interface spec required. So each push looked about 4.45 times weaker than it really was. Each one was tiny. But the craft's solar array was on one side only, so these firings happened far more often than planned, and the error grew all the way to Mars.

The data file was also unusable for the first few months of the cruise because of format problems, so the navigators worked around it. By the time real data flowed, there was less time left to notice that it disagreed. They did notice: orbit estimates kept drifting, and people raised concerns. But the concerns went through email and hallway talk, not the formal problem-reporting system. An optional fifth course correction (TCM-5) was discussed and not carried out. On 23 September 1999 the orbiter went into Mars's atmosphere at about 57 km instead of the planned 150 or so. Below roughly 80 km it was not built to survive, and it was destroyed. The investigation board called the units error the "root cause". But most of its report was about why a known oddity never turned into an escalation.

**The twist:** the board's real finding was about a communication failure, with the conversion error as its trigger. NASA was running its "Faster, Better, Cheaper" programme: small teams, few people doing reviews, lots of trust in informal channels. The units bug was found. What was missing was a way for "this feels off" to become "we stop until it's explained".
