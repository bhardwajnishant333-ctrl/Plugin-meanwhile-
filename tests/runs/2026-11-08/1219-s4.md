**The Hunt brothers more or less won their silver corner. The exchange then changed the rules.**

Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt had been buying silver since the early 1970s, both physical metal and futures, and they took delivery instead of rolling their contracts. In 1979 they brought in Saudi partners. Silver went from about $6/oz in early 1979 to about $50 in January 1980. The Hunts were thought to control a large share of the world's deliverable silver, with estimates commonly in the hundreds of millions of ounces; I'm not confident of the exact figure. Silver was flowing out of households to be melted down. Tiffany's chairman took out a newspaper ad calling the hoarding "unconscionable."

Then COMEX moved. On January 7, 1980 it adopted **Silver Rule 7**, which sharply limited leveraged silver positions. Soon after it went to **liquidation only**: traders could close positions but not open new ones. With no new buyers allowed, the price could only fall. By March 27, "Silver Thursday", silver was near $11, the Hunts missed a margin call of roughly $100M+, and fears that brokers would fail followed. Banks put together a credit line of about $1.1B to keep things stable, with the Fed aware. Years later a jury found the Hunts liable for manipulation, and Bunker Hunt went bankrupt.

**The twist:** a corner beats the market but not the rulebook, and the rulebook is written by the exchange's members. Congress later looked at the fact that some COMEX board members were reportedly short silver while voting on those rules. The lesson isn't that manipulation fails. It's that whoever can change margin and position rules has the final say in any squeeze. That's the same lever ICE pulled in cocoa in 2024, when margin hikes pushed out the hedgers.
