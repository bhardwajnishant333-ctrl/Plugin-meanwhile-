**On a Cortex-M, RTIC's scheduler is the interrupt controller itself. A `lock` there is basically one register write.**

RTIC began as RTFM ("Real-Time For the Masses") at Luleå University of Technology. It's built on the Stack Resource Policy (SRP) from T.P. Baker's 1991 paper. At compile time it looks at which tasks share each resource. Each resource then gets a "ceiling", the highest priority of any task that uses it. Taking a lock just raises BASEPRI to that ceiling. Only tasks that could contend for the resource get masked, and anything with a higher priority still preempts you. Cortex-M0 has no BASEPRI, so RTIC masks interrupts there instead.

SRP has two consequences:
- **No deadlock, by construction.**
- **Bounded priority inversion:** a task waits at most one critical section of one lower-priority task.

A task also never blocks once it starts, so task frames nest perfectly and everything runs on a single shared stack.

**The twist:** compare Mars Pathfinder in 1997. Its resets were caused by priority inversion, and JPL fixed them by turning on priority inheritance on a VxWorks mutex, which was a runtime patch to a runtime protocol. SRP removes that whole class of bug before the code ever runs. For your motor controller, this makes the jitter of your current-loop ISR something you can calculate rather than just measure: it's bounded by your longest lower-priority critical section on a shared resource. That's a concrete number to check against what your HIL run measures. (If you're on Embassy rather than RTIC, this is how the other camp does it.)

**In 2010 the proton suddenly got 4% smaller, and for almost a decade nobody knew which number was wrong.**

For decades the proton's radius was measured with electrons, either by scattering them off protons or from hydrogen spectroscopy. CODATA's accepted value was about 0.877 fm. Then Randolf Pohl's team at PSI built "muonic hydrogen", with a muon orbiting the proton instead of an electron. A muon is about 207 times heavier, so it sits roughly 200 times closer to the proton and is far more sensitive to the proton's size. Their result, published in Nature in 2010, was 0.842 fm. Both camps had tight error bars, and they disagreed by about 5 sigma. People proposed new physics to explain the gap.

The resolution came mostly from re-measuring with electrons more carefully. Jefferson Lab's PRad experiment, published in 2019, got about 0.831 fm, and CODATA 2018 moved to 0.8414. So the gap wasn't new physics. It was old measurements whose error bars were too small.

**The twist:** this is common. Henrion and Fischhoff found the same thing in 1986 in a study of how physical constants were measured over time. Reported uncertainties were consistently too small, and successive values of constants like the speed of light swung by more than their stated errors. Precision and accuracy kept being confused with each other, by very careful people. For your HIL run, a tight spread across runs on the same rig tells you about repeatability. It says nothing about the systematic error the rig itself might be adding.

**The "10,000 hours" rule was never a rule. It came from an average in a study of about 30 violinists.**

Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) found that the best violin students at a Berlin music academy had averaged around 10,000 hours of solo practice by age 20. Gladwell turned that average into a threshold in *Outliers*, and Ericsson himself later objected to the popular version. When Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (2014) pooled 88 studies, deliberate practice explained only about 12% of the differences in performance. By field it was about 26% for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education, and under 1% for professions.

**The twist:** in their 2016 follow-up on sports, among *elite* athletes practice explained about 1%. Once everyone at a level trains hard, hours stop separating anyone. The differences come from things a training log doesn't record. That's useful to know before you blame your next plateau on not enough sessions on the wall.
