**Most photos on the web keep their color at a quarter of the resolution, and almost no one can tell.**

When a photo is saved as a JPEG, the brightness and the color are split into separate layers. Brightness is kept at full resolution. Color is usually kept at half the width and half the height, so a quarter as many pixels. The technical name is "4:2:0 chroma subsampling." It works because your eyes pick up fine detail through brightness, and are much less sharp at telling where one color ends and the next begins.

The trick is older than JPEG. When American color TV standards were set in 1953, the color signal got far less bandwidth than the black-and-white picture, and viewers didn't notice.

**The twist for you:** detail in white dresses, lace and hair is mostly brightness, so it comes through fine. The weak spot is a hard edge between two strong colors: a red bouquet against white, red lipstick, a saturated dress against a blue sky. Look closely there and you may see a faint smear or blur at the edge.

If that ever bothers you, there's a quick fix: tell Claude "export portfolio images with 4:4:4 chroma" (meaning full-resolution color). The files get a little bigger.

1) **Deeper:** why your eyes work this way (the setup of your retina explains it)
2) **Sideways:** why skin tones are the hardest thing for a camera to get right, and how film companies once used a single reference photo (the "Shirley card") to set them
