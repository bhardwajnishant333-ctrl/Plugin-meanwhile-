**Meanwhile:**
Rust's safety guarantees say nothing about stack depth, and on a Cortex-M a stack overflow by default doesn't crash, it silently overwrites your `static`s.

**How it works:** the usual Cortex-M linker layout puts `.data` and `.bss` at the bottom of RAM and starts the stack at the top, growing down toward them. Nothing stands between the two. If you recurse too deep or build a big buffer in an interrupt handler, the stack walks into your globals: PWM duty limits, fault flags, controller state. No exception fires. The code keeps running on corrupted data. This is how the Toyota unintended-acceleration case went to court. In *Bookout v. Toyota* (Oklahoma, October 2013), embedded expert Michael Barr testified that the throttle firmware had more than 10,000 global variables and used recursion, so its worst-case stack use had no bound. His argument was that an overflow could quietly kill the task that ran both the throttle and the fail-safes. The jury found Toyota liable, and Toyota settled before punitive damages were decided.

**The non-obvious part:** the Rust fix is a linker trick, not a language feature. Knurling's `flip-link` flips the layout so the stack sits at the bottom of RAM and grows toward the edge of the address space. An overflow then hits unmapped memory and raises a HardFault right away, before it can corrupt anything. For a motor controller, the Toyota failure was a silent wrong state, and flip-link turns that into a loud stop. Your HIL rig can catch a loud stop. It may never catch a silent one.

**Meanwhile:**
Your HIL rig will almost never measure your real worst-case stack. Reaching it takes every interrupt priority level preempting the one below it at exactly the deepest point of each call chain, all at once.

**How it works:** `cargo-call-stack` (Jorge Aparicio) does static analysis over the whole program. It builds with fat LTO on a nightly toolchain, has LLVM emit the stack size of each function, builds the call graph, and adds the numbers up along the deepest path from each root. Each interrupt handler counts as a separate root. On Cortex-M the true total is roughly: the deepest path in `main`, plus the deepest handler at *each* preemption priority level, plus the hardware exception frame stacked for each nested level. Only one handler can be active per priority level, so it's one handler per level, not all of them. The frame is where it gets expensive: 8 words (32 bytes) normally, but 26 words (104 bytes) when the FPU context is stacked. FOC math usually runs on the FPU, so three nested levels can cost about 300 bytes before any of your own code has used a byte.

**Where the analysis breaks:**
- Recursion makes a cycle in the call graph, so the result is unbounded.
- `dyn Trait` calls and function pointers have no fixed target. The tool falls back to matching every function whose signature fits, which can be anything from very pessimistic to useless.
- Inline asm and linked C have no stack info, so you get gaps.

**The non-obvious part:** a firmware architecture that analyses well on stack is also one that's provably sound at runtime. RTIC's priority-ceiling scheduling (Stack Resource Policy) keeps the call graph static and dispatch free of `dyn`. Tasks at the same priority never preempt each other, so they share one stack slot. Getting a provable stack bound and getting a design free of deadlocks come down to the same constraint.

Race-car drivers heat their tyres to get more grip, and boulderers wait for cold days to get more grip. Both are right, because a rubber's friction isn't a single number. It's a curve, and temperature moves you along it.

**How it works:** rubber doesn't follow the school rule that friction equals μ times the normal force. Most of its grip comes from two sources. *Hysteresis*: rock asperities push the rubber in, and it springs back slower than it was pressed, so energy is lost. *Adhesion*: molecular contact at the real touching area. Both depend on how fast the rubber is being flexed compared with how fast its polymer chains can relax. In 1963 K. A. Grosch (Proc. Royal Society A) measured rubber friction across a range of speeds and temperatures. He found that all the curves fall onto one master curve once temperature is converted into an equivalent speed (the Williams–Landel–Ferry shift). Cooling rubber does the same thing as sliding it faster. Every compound has a peak. A race tyre is designed to reach that peak at about 90°C; a climbing shoe is designed to reach it in ordinary air.

**The honest caveat:** I'm confident about the physics of the rubber. I'm much less sure how much of the "send in the cold" effect comes from the shoe and how much from skin. Fingertip friction depends strongly on moisture. Some moisture softens skin and increases real contact area, but sweat past that point acts as a lubricant, and cold slows sweating. On crimps, skin is probably the bigger factor, but I can't give you a clean split.

**The non-obvious part:** a friction coefficient behaves like the frequency response of a viscoelastic filter. What a datasheet gives you is one point on a Bode plot.

**Meanwhile:**
On 2 September 1859, telegraph operators between Boston and Portland, Maine, disconnected their batteries and kept sending messages for about two hours. The sky was powering the line.

**What happened:** on 1 September, Richard Carrington and Richard Hodgson, observing separately in England, each saw a white-light flare on the Sun. It was the first solar flare ever recorded. The coronal mass ejection it launched reached Earth in about 17.6 hours, where the usual transit takes two to three days. It hit Earth's magnetic field hard and fast. A changing magnetic field induces a voltage in any long conductor, and the telegraph lines of 1859 were hundreds of kilometres of wire, many using the earth as their return path. Those lines picked up enough current to replace their batteries, and in places to spark: operators reported shocks and scorched telegraph paper. Aurora was reported as far south as the Caribbean, and in the northeastern US people said it was bright enough to read by at night.

**The twist:** the danger was never to small electronics. It grows with the length of the conductor, and the induced current is close to DC. Today's longest conductors are high-voltage transmission lines, and quasi-DC current flowing into a large transformer pushes its core into saturation on half of each cycle. On 13 March 1989 a storm much weaker than Carrington's collapsed Hydro-Québec's grid in about 90 seconds, and roughly 6 million people lost power for around 9 hours. In July 2012 a CME estimated to be about as strong as Carrington's crossed Earth's orbit roughly a week after Earth had passed that point. We know about it mainly because the STEREO-A spacecraft was in its path.
