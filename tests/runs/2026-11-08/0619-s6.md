Koji, *Aspergillus oryzae*, is a domesticated line of *Aspergillus flavus*. *A. flavus* is the mold that makes aflatoxin, one of the most potent natural liver carcinogens known, and it's the reason peanut and corn shipments get tested. A 2012 genomics study (Gibbons et al., *Current Biology*) found that koji strains carry the aflatoxin gene cluster in a broken state, with deletions and mutations. In November 2023, Voyager 1 (launched 1977, now more than 24 billion km away) started sending back nonsense. The cause turned out to be one dead chip in its Flight Data Subsystem, which took out about 3% of that computer's memory along with the code stored there. The code had nowhere to go: no free block was big enough to hold it. So engineers cut it into pieces, spread the pieces across whatever gaps were left, and fixed every reference that pointed between them. They worked partly from documentation written in the 1970s, and they had no exact replica of the hardware to test on. Each command took about 22.5 hours to arrive, and the reply took just as long to come back.

On average, your friends have more friends than you do. That holds for almost everyone, and it's a mathematical fact, not a sign that you're unpopular.

Sociologist Scott Feld showed this in 1991, and the cause is sampling bias. Someone with 500 friends turns up on 500 people's friend lists, and a loner turns up on almost none. So when you average over "the friends people have," the well-connected count over and over. It holds in any network where some people have more connections than others, which is nearly every real network. The averages work out as a strict inequality: the average friend has at least as many friends as the average person, and more whenever connection counts vary at all.

**The twist:** people have used this as an early-warning system. In 2009, Christakis and Fowler followed Harvard students through the H1N1 flu season. They picked one group at random and a second group made up of friends those students named. The friends group caught flu noticeably earlier, about two weeks ahead of the random group by one of their measures. The well-connected sit nearer the center, so anything spreading through the network reaches them first. The same idea gives "acquaintance immunization" (Cohen, Havlin and ben-Avraham, 2003). If you can't map the network, vaccinate a random person's friend instead of the random person. You reach the hubs without knowing who they are.

The lesson: when you can't see the structure, ask what your sampling method is biased toward, then use that bias on purpose.
