**Meanwhile:**
**A starling in a murmuration doesn't watch the birds within some distance. It watches about seven, however far apart they are.**

In 2008 the STARFLAG team in Rome filmed flocks of up to a few thousand starlings in 3D and rebuilt each bird's position (Ballerini et al., *PNAS*). They tested two rules. In the "metric" rule a bird reacts to everything within a fixed radius. In the "topological" rule it reacts to a fixed number of nearest neighbours. The data matched the topological rule, at about 6 to 7 neighbours. That held whether the flock was packed tight or spread out.

**The twist:** this is why a murmuration doesn't break apart when it thins out. With a metric rule, a stretched flock leaves birds with nobody inside their radius, and the flock splits. With a fixed count, every bird always has its seven, at any density. Gossip-based membership in distributed systems uses the same trick: each node contacts a fixed number of peers rather than "everyone nearby." HashiCorp's memberlist, a Go library that Consul builds on, works roughly this way. That fixed fan-out is a big part of why these designs hold up as clusters grow, shrink or partly fail.

**Meanwhile:**
**Each starling only watches about seven neighbours, yet one bird's change of heading can reach the whole flock, whatever its size.**

In 2010 the same Rome group published "Scale-free correlations in starling flocks" (Cavagna et al., *PNAS*). They didn't look at how fast birds flew. They looked at fluctuations: how far each bird's heading differs from the flock's average. Then they asked how far apart two birds can be and still have those differences match. If there were a fixed reach, say "about 10 metres", big flocks would contain many independent patches. There wasn't one. The reach grew with the flock, across flocks of roughly a hundred to several thousand birds. Local rules, but correlations as big as the flock itself.

Physicists call that behaviour "near-critical". It's the state a magnet is in right at its tipping point, where a tiny nudge can flip the whole thing. A 2014 follow-up in *Nature Physics* (Attanasi et al.) filmed flocks turning and found the turn travels through the flock as a wave, at a steady speed and fading very little. I'm not confident of the exact speed, so I'm leaving it out.

**The twist:** what makes a flock respond so well is exactly what would make it collapse all at once. Being critical means a predator's approach reaches every bird in time. It also means one confused bird can swing the whole flock. Your service graph has the same trade. Tight coupling (synchronous calls, shared retries, no backpressure) moves a signal across the system fast, and moves a failure just as fast. Circuit breakers, bulkheads and jittered retries all deliberately push a system *away* from critical. The flock never pays for this because a sudden swing is harmless in open sky. A retry storm in production is not.

1. **Deeper:** the 2014 turning-wave paper, and why "behavioural inertia" lets the turn travel without fading
2. **Sideways:** Go's "hot split", the stack-growth bug that made the Go team switch to copying stacks
3. **Wildcard:** the S3 outage on 28 Feb 2017, a typo in one command, and the status page that couldn't turn red

The command removed far more servers, including part of two key systems: the **index** subsystem (which knows where every object is) and the **placement** subsystem (which decides where new objects go). Both had to be restarted completely. AWS wrote that it hadn't fully restarted them in its larger regions for many years, and that the restart, with its safety checks, took longer than expected. S3 in us-east-1 recovered fully at about 1:54 PM. While it was down, AWS couldn't update its Service Health Dashboard because the dashboard's admin side depended on S3. It posted updates on Twitter.

Capacity is now removed more slowly, and the tool refuses any request that would take a subsystem below its minimum required capacity. The real bug was a destructive command with no floor, plus a recovery path nobody had exercised in years. For your apply: `lifecycle { prevent_destroy = true }` on stateful resources is exactly that kind of floor. The plan's "N to destroy" line is the step where the 2017 typo would have shown up. **Meanwhile:**
**Knight Capital's emergency rollback made things worse: going back to the old code turned the bug on across all eight servers instead of one.**

Most of this comes from the SEC's 2013 order against Knight. NYSE was launching its Retail Liquidity Program on 1 Aug 2012, and Knight updated its order router, SMARS, to support it. A technician copied the new code to seven of the eight SMARS servers and missed the eighth. The new code reused an old flag. That flag used to switch on "Power Peg", a feature Knight had stopped using in 2003 but never deleted. On the eighth server, incoming orders with the flag woke Power Peg up. Back in 2005 the part of Power Peg that tracked when an order was filled had been moved elsewhere. So it kept sending child orders and never knew when to stop.

In about 45 minutes after the market opened, SMARS made roughly 4 million trades in 154 stocks, about 397 million shares in total. Trying to stop it, Knight removed the new code from the seven healthy servers. The flag was still arriving, so all eight now ran Power Peg. Knight lost about $460M. It needed about $400M of rescue money within days, was fined $12M by the SEC, and merged with Getco in 2013. One more detail: before the open, Knight's systems sent automated emails pointing to the Power Peg problem, and nobody acted on them. The SEC order puts the count at 97.

**The twist:** three things combined to cause this, and each looked harmless on its own. The fleet wasn't uniform (one server out of step), a flag meant one thing in one version and something else in another, and the "safe" rollback had never been tested against the live mix of servers. Your apply has the same shape. A partial apply, or drift between environments, leaves you with mixed versions running side by side. In a Go fleet, a feature flag or enum value reused under a new meaning is the reused flag. Never reuse a flag's meaning, delete dead code paths, and check which version every instance is running before you trust a rollback.

1. **Deeper:** the 97 emails nobody acted on, and why alert fatigue keeps showing up in big failures
2. **Sideways:** back to birds: US weather radar picks up nightly migration, and BirdCast turns it into live forecasts
