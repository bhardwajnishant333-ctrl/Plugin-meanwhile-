**Meanwhile:**
Postgres can stop accepting writes even with plenty of disk, CPU and memory left, because it has run out of transaction IDs.

**How it works:**
- Transaction IDs (XIDs) are 32-bit numbers and they wrap around. Postgres compares them in a circle, so any given XID sees about 2 billion IDs as "the past" and about 2 billion as "the future."
- Every row stores the XID that created it. If that row's XID got more than about 2 billion transactions old, it would suddenly look like it came from the future and the row would disappear from view. To prevent that, VACUUM "freezes" old rows, marking them as visible to everyone forever.
- If freezing can't keep up, Postgres protects itself. It starts warning when about 40 million XIDs are left. At about 3 million left (the limit since PG14), it refuses any command that needs a new XID. Your data is safe, but the database is effectively read-only until a vacuum finishes, and on a large table that can take hours.
- **Sentry, July 2015:** their main database reached this limit and the outage stretched over most of a day, which they wrote up in a public postmortem.
- **Mandrill (Mailchimp), February 2019:** one shard hit the limit and email sending was disrupted for over a day.

**The twist, which is about your cutover:** busy write traffic usually isn't what causes this. Something holding back vacuum is. A long-open transaction, an abandoned prepared transaction, or a **replication slot nobody dropped** pins the oldest XID vacuum has to keep, so freezing can't advance past it. A logical slot pins `catalog_xmin`, so system catalogs can't be frozen, and that can drag the whole database toward the limit. After you cut over to 17, if the PG14 side keeps running with a slot or subscription left behind, it will build up XID age and WAL quietly until someone notices. Before you close the ticket, check `pg_replication_slots` on both clusters and look at `age(datfrozenxid)` in `pg_database`.

Pick one:
1. **Deeper:** 64-bit XIDs. Why Postgres still hasn't adopted them after years of patches, and what the trade-off is.
2. **Sideways:** the 2038 problem, which is the same kind of overflow with Unix time and is already turning up in embedded systems.

**Meanwhile:**
In the Ottoman Empire, "12 o'clock" meant sunset. Since sunset moves every day, the empire's clocks were wrong by design almost as soon as they were set.

**How it worked:**
- *Alaturka* time, also called *ezanî* time, started the day at sunset. Sunset was 12:00, and the hours counted on from there. To find "3 o'clock," you counted three hours past sunset. In winter that was early evening; in summer it was getting close to midnight.
- Sunset changes through the year, so a mechanical clock running at a steady rate drifted out of step and had to be corrected regularly by hand, often every few days.
- The **muvakkithane** (timekeeper's house) handled this. These were small offices next to major mosques, staffed by a *muvakkit*. He used astronomical tables, quadrants and observation to work out sunset and the prayer times, and the local clocks were set from his figures. Each city effectively ran its own time server.
- Late in the 19th century, clock towers went up across the empire, many around Abdülhamid II's 1901 jubilee. Several had **two faces**, one showing alaturka time and one showing *alafranga* (European) time.
- Railways and the telegraph ran on alafranga time, because a timetable shared between cities can't use a clock that resets at each place's own sunset. The Republic switched to international hours in the mid-1920s (I'm not sure of the exact effective date).

**The twist:** alaturka time wasn't a primitive system. For the question people actually needed answered, "how long until dark, how long until prayer," it was the better clock. It lost because networks need agreement more than they need local accuracy. A town's own time only became a problem once it had to coordinate with other towns. US railroads hit the same wall in 1883 and forced standard time zones.

It's also why your replication doesn't trust wall clocks. Logical replication orders changes by LSN, a position in the WAL that only moves forward, and never by timestamp. Two servers will never agree exactly on what time it is, but they can agree on what happened before what.

2. **Sideways:** Lamport clocks. The 1978 paper that dropped "what time is it" entirely and kept only "what came before what."
3. **Wildcard:** the 1883 "day of two noons," when US cities reset their clocks to railroad time on the same day.
