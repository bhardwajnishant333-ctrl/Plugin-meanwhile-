**The "correct" way to build an accessible combobox has changed twice, and the current spec is mostly a return to the first one.**

- **ARIA 1.0 (2014):** the `<input>` itself has `role="combobox"`.
- **ARIA 1.1 (2017):** that changed. The combobox became a wrapper element, with the input inside it as a separate textbox the wrapper "owned." It looked cleaner on paper, but screen readers handled it unevenly, so many libraries that followed it ended up with something worse in practice.
- **ARIA 1.2 (Recommendation in 2023):** back to `role="combobox"` on the input. It points to its popup with `aria-controls`, uses `aria-expanded`, and marks the highlighted option with `aria-activedescendant` while keyboard focus stays in the input.

It can't tell you whether VoiceOver or NVDA really announce that option 3 of 12 is highlighted. **Meanwhile:**
**For about 150 years, two bishops against a knight was treated as a draw. Then a computer showed it was usually a win, just one that takes longer than the rules allow.**

- **Before computers:** published analysis going back to Kling and Horwitz in 1851 held that the knight side could build a fortress, so the endgame was a draw.
- **1980s:** Ken Thompson (yes, the Unix one) built complete endgame databases by working backward from every checkmate position. They showed two bishops beat a knight in most positions. The catch was that some wins needed more than 50 moves without a capture or pawn move, and the 50-move rule ends the game in a draw before then.
- **FIDE's response:** it added longer move limits for specific endgames, tried changing that list, and in 1992 dropped all the exceptions and went back to 50 moves for everything. Playing 100 perfect moves was beyond any human, and keeping a list of exceptions didn't scale. I'm sure about the outcome; I'm less sure exactly which endgames were on the exception list.
- **2012:** the Lomonosov 7-piece tables, computed in Moscow, found a position needing **549 moves** to force mate.

**The twist:** modern engines (the Syzygy tables) have a label for this, the **"cursed win."** It means a position that is won with perfect play but a draw under the actual rules. The tables have to track two answers: what is true in theory and what the rules let you claim. Your visual tests work the same way. **Meanwhile:**
**Endgame tables never search forward through moves. They start from every checkmate and work backward until nothing new changes.**

The method is called retrograde analysis:

1. **List every position.** For one set of pieces (say king and two bishops against king and knight), give each legal placement of the pieces, with each side to move, a number. With no pawns on the board, the board's 8 symmetries (rotations and mirrors) let you store about 1/8 of the positions.
2. **Mark the checkmates.** These are "lost in 0" for the side to move.
3. **Step backward.** "Un-move" a piece from each lost position. Any position that can reach it in one move is "win in 1" for the side to move. A position where every legal move leads to the opponent winning is "lost in 2."
4. **Repeat** until a full pass labels nothing new. Every position still unlabeled is a draw, because neither side can force anything.

No evaluation function, no pruning, no guessing. It's breadth-first search from the goal over the graph with every move reversed, so the answers are exact. The scale is the hard part: 7-piece sets run to hundreds of trillions of positions. The Syzygy 7-piece files are about 18 TB, and the Lomonosov tables, which also store moves-to-mate, are over 100 TB. I'm confident of the order of magnitude, not the exact sizes.

**The twist:** asking "can every state reach the goal?" backward is usually far cheaper than exploring forward from the start, because you only touch states that matter. It's the same idea behind model checking and dead-code detection.

**For your Combobox:** if the refactor is turning it into an explicit state machine (closed, open, highlighted, filtering, selected), you can run the same backward check. Start from "closed" and confirm every state can get there by keyboard alone, with Escape, Tab or Enter. Any state you can't reach "closed" from by keyboard is a keyboard trap, and accessibility audits fail you for those.

2) **Sideways:** model checking in practice. How Amazon used TLA+ to find bugs in S3 and DynamoDB designs that testing missed
