**Meanwhile:**
**A hive has no thermostat, yet it holds the brood nest at about 34–35 °C whether it's freezing or scorching outside.**

**How:** some workers become "heater bees." They unhook their flight muscles from their wings and shiver them in place. That can push their thorax to around 43 °C. Jürgen Tautz's group at the University of Würzburg (Kleinhenz et al., Journal of Experimental Biology, 2003) found that some of these heaters crawl into the empty cells scattered among the capped brood. From there they warm the pupae on every side, like a heating element pushed into the comb. When the nest runs hot, other workers fan their wings and spread water droplets so evaporation cools it. Each bee reacts only to the temperature right around it. Nobody coordinates them.

**The twist:** a degree or two changes the adult bee. Tautz et al. (PNAS, 2003) raised pupae at slightly different temperatures. Bees raised toward the cool end, around 32 °C, came out with worse learning and memory than bees raised at about 34.5–35 °C. So the colony isn't just keeping its brood alive. It's setting how well its future workers will think. A hive with spotty brood or too few bees to cover it can raise slightly duller foragers, and you'd never see why.

In your service's terms, this is control-loop design with no coordinator. Each instance acts on local signals and the system stays in its target range anyway.

2. **Sideways:** the overshoot problem, i.e. why naive retry and autoscaling loops oscillate when bees don't
3. **Wildcard:** how a swarm picks its new home by voting, and the quorum number they use
