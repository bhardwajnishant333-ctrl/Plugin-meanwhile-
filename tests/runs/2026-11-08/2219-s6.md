**Nobody can fully explain why ice is slippery. The answer most of us learned in school is wrong.**

The textbook explanation is pressure melting: the skate blade presses on the ice, the pressure lowers the melting point, and the skate rides on a thin film of water. Run the numbers and it doesn't hold up. Pressure lowers ice's melting point by only about 0.0074 °C per atmosphere, so even a skate edge gets you a few degrees at most. Yet people skate fine at -30 °C, and you slip on ice just standing still in boots.

That leaves two main ideas, and they're still being argued:
- **Frictional heating**, proposed by Bowden and Hughes in 1939: sliding warms the contact enough to melt a microscopic layer. That fits fast skating but not standing still.
- **A premelted surface layer**, first suggested by Faraday in the 1850s: the top few molecular layers of ice are disordered and liquid-like even well below zero, because surface molecules have fewer neighbours holding them in place.

In 2018, Daniel Bonn's group in Amsterdam measured friction across a range of temperatures (Weber et al., Physical Review Letters). Friction was lowest around -7 °C. They described the surface as loosely bound molecules that roll like tiny ball bearings, not as a water film. Speed-skating rinks are kept at about that temperature.

**The twist:** a wrong explanation stayed in textbooks for more than a century because it was neat and nobody did the arithmetic. People use ice every winter, and the physics behind it is still an open question 170 years after Faraday.
