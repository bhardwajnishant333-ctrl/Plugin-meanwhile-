In 1846 Urbain Le Verrier noticed that Uranus kept drifting off its predicted path. Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a storm bigger than Earth, people have watched it shrink for 150 years, and nobody can say whether it's dying.

In the late 1800s it measured about 40,000 km across. Voyager in 1979 measured about 23,000 km. Hubble and Juno measurements around 2017 put it near 16,000 km, now a bit larger than one Earth. Through the 2010s it shrank by several hundred km a year and became rounder. Juno, which has orbited Jupiter since 2016, found that it reaches roughly 300–500 km down into the atmosphere: very shallow for its width, like a pancake spinning in a pan. Its age is uncertain too. A 2024 study argued that the "permanent spot" Cassini drew in the 1660s was probably a different storm, and that today's spot dates from the 1830s.

There are two leading ideas for why it has lasted. Jupiter has no land to break a storm up the way continents break up hurricanes. And the spot seems to keep itself going by absorbing smaller vortices that drift into it. If that supply is what's thinning, the shrinking could be a slow starvation. Or it could be a phase the storm comes out of. Both are open.

The twist is that it may last not because it's stable, but because it keeps eating. Remove the inflow and something that looked permanent turns out to have been a flow, not a thing.

Your 8" Dob can track this yourself: on a steady night at 150–200x the spot shows as a pale salmon oval in the south equatorial belt. Time when it crosses Jupiter's central line, and planning apps will predict the transits.
