**A kiln can hit the right temperature and still underfire the pots, because clay responds to heat over time, not to peak temperature.**

**How it works.** Potters don't trust the kiln's thermometer alone. They put small, slender cones of ceramic material on a shelf where they can see them. **You're probably not counting wrong. Salsa is arranged so that beat 1 is hard to hear.**

**How it works.** A salsa dance count of 8 covers two bars of music. Two instruments that usually help you find a downbeat stay off it:

- **The bass anticipates.** The typical salsa bass line (the *tumbao*) lands on the "and" of 2 and on 4, and seldom plays on 1. If you count from the bass, beat 1 feels like it's missing, because it mostly is.
- **The congas mark 4 and 8.** The conga's loudest open tones fall around beats 4 and 8. Those are exactly the beats you *don't* step on (step 1-2-3, pause on 4, step 5-6-7, pause on 8). The loudest drum hits land on your pauses. That's by design.

Holding it all together is the **clave**, a five-stroke pattern spread over the same two bars. Played "3-2", its strokes fall on 1, the "and" of 2, and 4, then on 2 and 3 of the next bar. Every instrument is arranged around it, the way a layout sits on a grid nobody sees.

**The twist.** In this music the beat isn't one loud sound. It's implied by sounds that fall around it. So stop trying to hear "1" as an event. Listen for the **cowbell** in the faster, louder sections, since it often hits the downbeats. Or treat the conga's open tones as your "pause here" cue and count backwards from there.

How it applies to your site: the clave works like an invisible grid. You never see it, but it's why everything else lines up. That's the same job your Figma layout grid does. When the AI-built page comes back, check that its spacing still follows your grid and not just the elements you can see.

**LA style and New York style use the same steps and the same pauses. The only thing they move is which step you change direction on, and that one beat changes how the dance feels.**

**How it works.**

- **The footwork is shared.** In both styles you step 1-2-3, pause on 4, step 5-6-7, pause on 8.
- **The difference is the "break".** That's the step where you change direction, like the rock step in a basic. In **LA style ("on 1")**, you break on 1 and 5. In **New York style ("on 2")**, you break on 2 and 6.
- **Where they came from.** On 2 was mainly codified and taught by Eddie Torres in New York, starting around the 1970s and 80s. LA style grew up in the 1990s, largely through the Vazquez brothers (Luis, Johnny and Francisco). It took on the linear "slot" dancing of New York, plus speed, spins and showy, show-oriented tricks. Cuban casino is a different thing again: circular instead of in a slot.
- **Why on 2 dancers say it "fits the congas".** The conga pattern has a sharp slap on 2 and 6. On 2 dancers put their break right on that accent, so the change of direction rides a drum hit that's already there. I'm describing how they explain it, not a measured fact. Plenty of great dancers feel at home on 1.

**The twist.** LA style breaks on beat 1, which is the beat the bass deliberately skips. So your "1" won't come from the low end. Here's the practical fix. Song phrases usually start on 1: a vocal line coming in, a horn hit, the piano pattern starting over. Count 1-8 from where a phrase starts, then check that your pauses land on the loud conga tones (4 and 8). If both line up, you're right. If the pauses feel like they land on nothing, you've probably slipped one beat.
