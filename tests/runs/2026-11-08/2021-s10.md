**Meanwhile:**
One of the most famous product flops ever was an air freshener, and the reason was that the people who needed it most couldn't smell their own homes.

When Procter & Gamble launched Febreze in the 1990s, sales were weak. Researchers visited a woman whose house smelled strongly of her nine cats. She noticed nothing. Her nose had stopped registering the smell. (The story is told in Charles Duhigg's 2012 book *The Power of Habit*.) Febreze only took off once it was sold as a pleasant final touch after cleaning, not as a fix for bad smells.

How it works: smell is built to notice change, not to keep reporting what stays the same. The first time you smell something in a room, your scent receptors fire hard. Then they quickly fade, often within minutes. If you live with a smell, your brain also files it as "background" and stops passing it on. In one study at the Monell Chemical Senses Center (Dalton and Wysocki, 1996), people slept with a scent in their bedroom for two weeks. Their sensitivity to it dropped a lot, and it took roughly another two weeks away from it to come back.

**The twist for your work:** scent is the one part of a room the owner can't experience but every guest can. New paint, a leather sofa, oiled wood, a candle the client loves: your client stops smelling all of it within days. Visitors smell it fresh every time. And your best moment to judge a space is the first minute after you walk in, before your own nose adapts. Take note of what you smell then, because 20 minutes later you won't notice it.

The little jar of coffee beans in perfume shops is mostly theatre.

At least two small studies have tested it. Secundo and Sobel (Chemical Senses, 2006) and Grosofsky and colleagues (2011) had people smell coffee beans, lemon, or plain air between scents. Coffee did no better than plain air at restoring their sense of smell. Coffee is just another strong smell, so it gives your nose one more thing to tire of.

What professionals actually do:
- **Short sniffs, not long inhales.** A few quick sniffs give a clearer read than one deep breath, which tires the receptors faster.
- **Rest and fresh air.** Step away for a minute or so between scents. Time is the real reset.
- **Paper strips first, skin later.** Perfumers smell from blotter strips, then check again after an hour and after several hours. A perfume changes as its lighter notes evaporate first and its heavier base notes linger.
- **Smelling your own skin.** Shop staff often suggest sniffing your inner arm. Your nose is permanently used to your own smell, so it works as a blank page. This is common practice, but I haven't seen solid research behind it.

**The twist:** the reset that works is time and absence, not another strong smell. Your eyes work the same way. After staring at one colour scheme for an hour, looking at a different strong colour doesn't help you see it fresh. Walking away does.
