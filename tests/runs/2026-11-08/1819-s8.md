**A spring-loaded cam holds just as well whether you hang on it lightly or take a huge whipper. How hard you pull barely matters.**

In the 1970s Ray Jardine designed the cam that became Wild Country's "Friends" (sold from 1978). He shaped each lobe as a logarithmic spiral, the one curve where the angle between the lobe surface and the line to the axle stays the same at every point. So wherever the cam sits in its range, it presses on the rock at the same angle. On many designs that angle is around 13.75°, though I'm not sure that number applies to every brand.

Here's the mechanism. When the rope pulls down, the lobes push outward against the rock in proportion to that pull. Whether the cam slips depends only on the angle compared with how grippy the rock is: if tan(camming angle) is less than the friction coefficient, it holds. The load cancels out of that test, so a bigger pull just means a bigger outward push. A placement that holds bodyweight should hold a fall too, until the metal or the rock gives out.

**The twist:** cams don't usually fail by sliding. They fail by "walking" deeper into the crack, by sitting in rock that flares open, or by over-camming at the edge of their range, where the constant-angle trick stops working. The physics is solid; where you put it is the weak point.

1) **Deeper:** why designers picked about 13.75° and not 20°, and the trade-off between range and holding power
2) **Sideways:** the Swift version of "one fixed rule, input size doesn't matter": why `String.count` is O(n) and not O(1)
3) **Wildcard:** how a dynamic rope gets certified, the standard 80 kg drop test, and how much force your body takes in a fall

**In Swift, the family emoji 👨‍👩‍👧 has a `count` of 1. It is also 5 Unicode scalars, 8 UTF-16 units and 18 bytes. The 1 is the most expensive of those to get.**

A Swift `Character` is one user-perceived letter, which Unicode calls an extended grapheme cluster (rules in Unicode Annex #29). That emoji is three people joined by two invisible "zero-width joiner" characters. A flag like 🇺🇦 is two "regional indicator" letters that display as one flag. Nothing in the stored bytes says where one Character ends. Since Swift 5, strings are stored as UTF-8, so `count` has to walk the whole string and run the segmentation rules. That makes it O(n). For a native Swift string, `s.utf8.count` is O(1) because the byte length is stored.

That's also why `String.Index` isn't an `Int`. Jumping to "the 10th character" would quietly cost O(n) every time. The awkward index API is Swift refusing to hide that cost.

**The twist, for your app:** a "max 30 characters" limit on a route-name field can mean three different things. In SwiftUI, `text.count` counts graphemes. UIKit's `shouldChangeCharactersIn` hands you an `NSRange` in UTF-16 units, so the delegate method sees 👨‍👩‍👧 as 8. Your backend column probably limits bytes, and there it's 18. A climber names a route "Crimp City 🧗‍♀️🔥", one layer says it fits, another rejects it, and a third cuts the emoji in half into a broken glyph. Pick one unit (usually graphemes for the UI, with a byte check before sending to the server) and put an emoji-heavy name in a UI test.

Also, write `isEmpty`, not `count == 0`: it's O(1).

**In May 2015 a single text message could knock an iPhone off its feet. It wasn't the content that did it, it was trying to fit the message into a notification banner.**

The string mixed English ("effective. Power"), Arabic with heavily stacked diacritics, Marathi vowel signs, and a Chinese character. Receive it while locked, or with Messages closed, and iOS crashed and restarted its home screen. If it was the latest message in a thread, Messages kept crashing every time you opened it. Reported analyses pointed at CoreText, Apple's text layout engine, failing while it shortened the preview to fit the banner width and placed the "…". So the crash depended on the screen it appeared on. Apple's official workaround was to ask Siri to read the message, reply with a photo so the bad message was no longer last, then delete the thread. The fix shipped in iOS 8.4 (June 2015).

It happened again. In February 2018 one Telugu syllable, జ్ఞా, made of several joined letters, crashed iOS 11 apps that displayed it, including Messages, WhatsApp and Mail. Apple fixed it in iOS 11.2.6.
