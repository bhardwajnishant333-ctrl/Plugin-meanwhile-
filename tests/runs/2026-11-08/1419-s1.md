**"21 days to form a habit" doesn't come from habit research. It comes from a 1960 self-help book by a plastic surgeon.**

Maxwell Maltz's *Psycho-Cybernetics* said patients took "a minimum of about 21 days" to get used to a new face or a lost limb. Over the years "minimum" got dropped and the number got attached to habits.

The real measurement came in 2009, when Phillippa Lally's team at UCL followed 96 volunteers for 12 weeks. Each picked one daily behaviour, such as drinking water with lunch or running before dinner, and rated every day how automatic it felt. Plotted over time, automaticity rises fast and then flattens out, like a learning curve.
- **Median time to reach the plateau: 66 days.**
- **Range: 18 to 254 days.** The top of that range is projected from the curve, since the study only ran 84 days.
- Harder behaviours (exercise) took longer than easy ones (a glass of water).

**The twist:** the paper also found that **missing a single day didn't materially affect habit formation.** The curve barely dipped.

So the classic streak counter, which drops to zero after one miss, punishes exactly what the data says doesn't matter. It treats a habit like a chain that snaps. The data shows something closer to a slope that keeps rising through the occasional gap. The emotional hit of a reset is real, and it's probably what makes people quit.

1. **Deeper:** how Lally measured "automatic" (the self-report scale behind the curve) and what that means for what your app should track
2. **Sideways:** F1 tyre degradation, the "cliff," and why strategists treat wear as a curve rather than a countdown
3. **Wildcard:** why copying only your SwiftData `.sqlite` file can quietly lose your users' latest check-ins

**An F1 tyre can lose grip every single lap while the lap times keep getting faster.**

The reason is fuel. A car starts the race with up to about 110 kg of it and burns a couple of kilos a lap. As a rough rule of thumb, every 10 kg less makes the car around 0.3 s a lap quicker (the exact figure varies by track). So two curves run against each other:
- **Fuel burn** makes the car faster, lap after lap, at a steady rate.
- **Tyre wear** makes it slower. The loss starts gently and gets worse over the stint.

Add them up and the lap times can look flat, even improving, while the tyre is quietly dying. So strategists don't read raw lap times. They take out the fuel effect first, and only then does the real wear curve show up. From that corrected curve they estimate the **cliff**, the point where a gentle loss suddenly turns into seconds a lap.

Hungary 2019 is the textbook case. Verstappen led on old hard tyres. Mercedes pitted Hamilton a second time with about 20 laps left, dropping him roughly 20 s behind on fresh mediums. On paper that gave the lead away. In reality they had bet on the shape of Verstappen's corrected wear curve. Hamilton caught him and passed with 3 laps to go.

**The twist:** a flat line in your data can be two strong forces cancelling each other out. The danger isn't in the number you see. It's in the curve hidden behind it, and that curve is the one that hits the cliff.

1. **Deeper:** how teams find the cliff before the race, using long runs in Friday practice
2. **Sideways:** Canada 2010, the race that accidentally became Pirelli's design brief for tyres built to wear out
3. **Wildcard:** 1987's Black Monday, when "portfolio insurance" made the market behave like a tyre falling off a cliff
