**logQ fixes an item being sampled too often. It can't fix an item that is never sampled.**

logQ correction is importance reweighting: you subtract log q(item) so that popular items stop getting pushed down just because they show up as negatives more. The trick comes from sampled softmax in language models (Bengio & Senécal, early 2000s; Jean et al. 2015). Reweighting needs support, though. Many long-tail and fresh items almost never land in a batch, so q ≈ 0. Their gradient as a negative isn't mis-scaled, it's simply missing. No correction term brings back a signal you never saw.

What happens in practice is that the item tower never learns to push those embeddings *away* from users. Cold items can end up with oddly high dot products and leak into ANN retrieval. Offline recall looks fine because your eval set is drawn from the same popularity skew. Google's fix was Mixed Negative Sampling (Yang et al., WWW 2020): add a set of negatives drawn uniformly from the whole corpus alongside the in-batch ones, so every item gets some push-away gradient.

**Twist:** this is the same failure as the onion ban. If an item never shows up as a negative, the model gets no signal about it, and its score drifts unchecked, the way onion prices did with no futures market.

**In bfloat16, 256 + 1 = 256.**

bfloat16 ("brain float") was designed at Google Brain and shipped in hardware starting with TPU v2 around 2017. It has 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits and 7 mantissa bits. fp16 splits the same 16 bits into 5 exponent and 10 mantissa. The bet behind bf16 was that neural nets care about *range* far more than precision. It keeps fp32's full exponent, so values go up to about 3.4e38, against fp16's limit of 65,504. That's why bf16 training mostly doesn't need loss scaling. Converting from fp32 is just dropping the low 16 bits and rounding. The cost is about 8 significant bits, roughly 2–3 decimal digits. At 256 the gap between representable numbers is 2, so adding 1 rounds back to 256.

**Twist:** any update smaller than about 0.4% of a weight's size disappears. Dense layers get away with it because big gradients arrive every step. The rows of your item embedding table don't. A long-tail item gets a rare, small gradient, which rounds to zero if the table and its updates are kept in bf16. Then the item never moves, which is the same cold-item problem as before. In JAX, check that your params and optimizer state are fp32 and that only the matmuls run in bf16. Or use stochastic rounding, which makes tiny updates survive on average.

1. **Deeper:** stochastic rounding: why randomly rounding up or down is unbiased, and where it beats fp32 master weights
2. **Sideways:** why fp8 training needs two formats (E4M3 for the forward pass, E5M2 for gradients)
