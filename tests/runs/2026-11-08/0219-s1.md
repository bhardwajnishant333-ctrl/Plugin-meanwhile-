The record format underneath your library catalogue was designed for magnetic tape in 1966, and it's still in use.

How it works: Henriette Avram, a programmer at the Library of Congress, designed MARC (MAchine-Readable Cataloging). The pilot ran from 1966 to 1968, and MARC II became the base for everything that followed. Each record starts with a fixed 24-character leader. Then comes a directory listing each field's tag, length and starting position, so a program reading the tape could jump straight to a field without parsing the whole record. Tags are three digits: 245 is the title, 100 is the main author, 650 is a subject heading. Subfields are marked with a delimiter and a letter, like $a or $b. In 1999 USMARC and CAN/MARC were merged into MARC 21, which is what most library systems still exchange today. Your app probably gets JSON from a library system (ILS) or discovery API that was built on top of these records.

The twist: the Library of Congress launched BIBFRAME in 2011 as MARC's replacement, built on linked-data (RDF) principles. Fifteen years later, most libraries still run on MARC. The format isn't what holds them back. Every library tool, workflow and cataloguer's habit assumes those three-digit tags, so replacing the format means changing everything around it. That's the same reason your Ktor 3 migration is a 30-minute agent job and not a one-line version bump.

Liechtenauer's Zettel was never meant to teach anyone longsword. It was a way to look up something you had already been taught.

How it works: no writing by Liechtenauer himself survives, and we can't firmly date his life. Everything we have comes through other people's manuscripts. The earliest is MS 3227a, the Nuremberg Hausbuch, usually dated to around 1389 from a calendar in it, though that dating is debated. The Zettel is a set of short rhymed couplets, and the text says outright that it's written in hidden, concealed words so that not everyone can understand it. A line like the one about the Zornhau gives the move a name, a trigger and a counter in a few words. That's useless to someone who has never been shown the move, but fast to recall if you have. Later writers added prose explanations (glosses) under each couplet: the Pseudo-Peter von Danzig gloss in Cod. 44.A.8 (dated 1452), the gloss attributed to Ringeck (whose main surviving copy, in Dresden, is from the early 1500s), and others. On some techniques the glosses disagree.

The twist: modern reconstruction is reverse-engineering. You have a deliberately compressed spec, several commentaries written decades apart that don't always agree, and nobody left who learned from the source. When two glosses conflict on a technique, which one is "correct" depends on which tradition you treat as authoritative. Your club has made that choice, whether or not anyone said it out loud.

1) Deeper: where exactly the Danzig and Ringeck glosses disagree, and how reconstructors decide between them
2) Sideways: Fiore dei Liberi's Italian tradition, which took the opposite approach with pictures and plain explanations
3) Wildcard: why medieval memory training used rhyme and vivid images, and what that has in common with how mnemonics are taught today

Rhyme and meter work like a checksum: they make memorised text resistant to errors.

How it works: in Memory in Oral Traditions (1995), the psychologist David Rubin argued that oral poetry survives because several constraints act at once. Meter fixes how many syllables a line can have and where the stress falls. Rhyme limits which word can end the line. Meaning and imagery narrow things further. Each constraint on its own is weak, but together they leave very few words that fit a given slot. If you misremember one, the line stops scanning or rhyming and you notice. The vivid-image part has a much older source. The Rhetorica ad Herennium (around 80s BC, author unknown, long credited to Cicero) describes the method of loci: you place images along a familiar route in your mind. It recommends "imagines agentes", images that are active, striking, ugly or absurd, because ordinary ones fade. Mary Carruthers' The Book of Memory (1990) shows medieval scholars used these techniques as everyday tools, not as tricks.

Modern data: Maguire et al. (Nature Neuroscience, 2003) found that top memory competitors did not have better general memory or unusual brain structure. Nine out of ten said they used the method of loci. Dresler et al. (Neuron, 2017) trained people with no prior experience for six weeks, about 30 minutes a day. Their recall of a 72-word list went from roughly 26 words to roughly 62, and the effect was still measurable four months later. My numbers may be off by a few words, but the size of the effect is right.

The twist: a couplet in the Zettel isn't cryptic just to keep secrets. Its form is what made it hard to corrupt as it was taught and copied across generations. The few words that do vary between manuscripts are a useful lead: they tend to be the places where scribes didn't understand what they were copying.

1) Deeper: Simonides and the collapsed banquet hall, the origin story of the method of loci, and how much of it is plausibly true
2) Sideways: spaced repetition, from Ebbinghaus's 1885 forgetting curve to the scheduling algorithms in Anki
3) Wildcard: how the Vedas were passed down orally for more than two thousand years using interleaved recitation patterns that work as error correction
