Starlings in a murmuration don't keep a set distance from each other. They keep a set number of neighbours.

In 2008 the STARFLAG team in Rome (Ballerini et al., PNAS) filmed flocks over Termini station with synchronized stereo cameras and rebuilt each bird's position in 3D. The old assumption was that each bird reacts to everything within some fixed radius. That isn't what they found. Each bird tracks about 6 or 7 of its nearest neighbours, however near or far those neighbours are. As the flock stretches out and squeezes together, the actual spacing changes a lot, but the count stays about the same.

Here's why that matters. A rule based on distance breaks when density changes: spread the birds out and each one ends up with nobody inside its radius, and the flock falls apart. A rule based on a fixed number of neighbours stays connected at any density. That's how the flock holds together when a falcon blows a hole in it. Your map runs into the same choice. MapKit clusters annotations by distance on screen, so at different zoom levels the same bike docks merge or split in ways a rider can't predict. The starlings would cluster by count.

The first bike-share was a political stunt, and it was the police who shut it down.

Amsterdam, July 1965. Provo, an anarchist art movement, painted bikes white and left them unlocked around the city, free for anyone to ride. The idea came from industrial designer Luud Schimmelpenninck. The full "White Bicycle Plan" asked the city to buy thousands of white bikes a year and keep cars out of the centre. I think the figure was 20,000 a year, but I'm not certain. Council said no. The police then impounded the bikes Provo had put out, on the grounds that leaving a bike unlocked broke a local rule because it invited theft. Provo responded by fitting combination locks and painting the combination on each bike.

The twist is that every later generation of bike-share solved the same problem the police raised: nobody was responsible for the bike.
- **1965, Amsterdam:** free and anonymous. Bikes got stolen or impounded.
- **1995, Copenhagen (Bycyklen):** a coin deposit, like a supermarket trolley. Still anonymous, and people still walked off with the bikes.
- **1998, Rennes:** smart cards tied to a named user. This is the model behind Vélib' in Paris (2007) and most systems that followed.
- **Today:** GPS, docks and app accounts.

So bike-share was never really a bike problem. It's a problem of trusting strangers, and each generation fixed it by making the rider identifiable. Your app is that identity layer. The annotations Claude is refactoring right now are the 2026 version of the lock with the combination painted on.
