**Meanwhile:**
**Until 1948, every plate in Curtis's Botanical Magazine was coloured by hand.**

That's about 160 years of the same 1700s workflow, and the magazine started in 1787. A copper plate printed only the black line work. Then teams of colourists, many of them women working at home and paid by the sheet, painted each print with watercolour. They copied a "pattern plate": one master print the artist had coloured as the reference. Early on Curtis printed about 3,000 copies, so each illustration was painted by hand thousands of times. That magazine paid Curtis's bills. His grand earlier project, *Flora Londinensis*, lost money.

**The twist:** the pattern plate was a design system. It was one source of truth, copied by many hands working at different speeds. And like every design system, it drifted. Pull two copies of the same plate from the same year and the greens often don't match, because each colourist mixed their own paint. So the "original colour" of a 1700s botanical print doesn't really exist. Every copy is its own version.

**Meanwhile:**
**Elizabeth Blackwell drew, engraved and hand-coloured 500 botanical plates herself, to get her husband out of debtors' prison.**

In the 1730s her husband Alexander's London printing business failed. One reason was that he'd never served the apprenticeship the printers' guild required. He ended up in debtors' prison. Elizabeth had trained as an artist and spotted a gap: doctors needed a good illustrated guide to medicinal plants. She took lodgings near the Chelsea Physic Garden, drew the plants straight from life, and published *A Curious Herbal* in weekly parts from 1737 to 1739, four plates a week. Alexander, still in prison, supplied the plant names. The Royal College of Physicians endorsed it, and the money paid off his debts.

**The twist:** she did all three jobs. Drawing, engraving and colouring were normally three separate trades, done by three different people. She did the whole pipeline herself. Then the story turns dark. Alexander moved to Sweden and was beheaded there in 1747, convicted of plotting over the Swedish royal succession. A well-known (possibly embellished) story says he laid his head on the wrong side of the block, then joked that it was his first time.

1) **Deeper:** how she managed to engrave on copper with no formal training
