**Google traced its entire production fleet by deliberately throwing away 1,023 of every 1,024 requests, and it worked.**

That comes from Google's Dapper paper (Sigelman et al., 2010), which is the direct ancestor of what Claude is adding to your services right now. Sigelman later co-created OpenTracing, which merged with OpenCensus in 2019 to become OpenTelemetry. Dapper found that on high-traffic services a 1/1024 sample still captured almost every latency pattern worth seeing, and it made the tracing overhead almost unmeasurable.

The catch is that the keep-or-drop decision happens at the root span, before anything has gone wrong. That decision then travels with the request in the `sampled` flag of the W3C `traceparent` header, so all four of your services agree on it. That's head sampling. For latency it works well. For rare failures it's a poor fit: if 0.1% of your route calculations time out, a 1/1024 sampler keeps about one of every thousand of them.

**The twist:** you can't fix this with a better ratio. You need a different place to decide. Tail sampling in the OTel Collector waits until a trace is finished, then keeps every error and every slow trace. It only works if every span of a trace reaches the same Collector instance, so with more than one Collector you have to route by trace ID (the `loadbalancing` exporter does that). Also note that the Go SDK's default sampler is `ParentBased(AlwaysSample)`. Unless Claude sets a sampler, you're tracing 100% of traffic, which is fine on staging and expensive in production.

**Meanwhile:**
**In 1928 Joseph Bédier looked at the family trees editors had drawn for French medieval texts and found that nearly all of them split into exactly two branches. By his count it was something like 105 of about 110. Real copying histories almost certainly don't look like that, so the method was shaping the result.**

The method is Karl Lachmann's, from the 19th century. You don't group manuscripts by what they get right, because correct readings can survive along any line of copying. You group them by the errors they share. If two manuscripts have the same omission or the same garbled word, they probably come from a common lost exemplar. Follow enough of these errors and you get a stemma codicum: a tree that ends at a reconstructed archetype nobody has ever seen. Lachmann's best-known proof was his Lucretius (1850). The key witnesses are two 9th-century Carolingian manuscripts now in Leiden, the Oblongus and the Quadratus. From passages that had been moved out of place, he worked out features of their lost common ancestor, reportedly down to its page layout. I'm sure of the method and the manuscripts but less sure of his exact page figures.

This maps closely onto your current work. A shared error works like a propagated trace ID: something every descendant carries, which shows a common origin. The archetype is the root span. Bédier's problem is a bias in the instrument: if the method makes it easier to justify a two-way split, you get two-way splits whatever the history was.

**The twist:** what really breaks the tree is *contamination*. A scribe copied from one exemplar and corrected it against a second, so a single manuscript has two parents. A tree can't show that, but a graph can. OpenTelemetry has exactly this escape hatch, called **span links**: a span that relates causally to traces other than its own parent. In 1998, a team published a stemma of Chaucer's *Wife of Bath's Prologue* in *Nature* using phylogenetics software from evolutionary biology. Biologists face the same problem with horizontal gene transfer. Every field that tracks descent finds out that the clean tree is only an approximation.
