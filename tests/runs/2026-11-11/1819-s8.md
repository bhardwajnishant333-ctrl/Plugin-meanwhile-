The *B. linens* you buy to wash your rinds mostly doesn't end up living on the cheese.

In 2008, Goerges and colleagues sampled a South German red-smear cheese whose makers added commercial ripening cultures, including *Brevibacterium linens*. When they typed the microbes on the finished rinds, they couldn't find the added strains in any meaningful number. The orange smear came from bacteria and yeasts that were already living in the ripening rooms. The old "old-young" smearing method spreads them: brine from mature wheels gets wiped onto new ones. The rind also changes in stages. Yeasts like *Debaryomyces hansenii* move in first and eat the lactate, which raises the pH. Once the surface is less acidic, salt-tolerant bacteria can take over. Later, Rachel Dutton's lab (Wolfe et al., *Cell*, 2014) and Bonham et al. (*mBio*, 2017) sequenced rinds from many countries. They kept finding marine bacteria such as *Halomonas* and *Pseudoalteromonas*, which probably arrive with the sea salt.

The twist is that a washed-rind cheese is shaped more by the room's history than by its recipe. The culture you add is a suggestion. The microbes already in the room decide what grows. That's also how incremental models behave: what's already in the target table can outweigh the new SQL until you do a full refresh.

A malaria mosquito can't reliably tell your ankle from a wheel of Limburger, because it isn't looking for "human" in the first place.

The overlap is chemistry. On the cheese, *Brevibacterium linens* breaks down methionine into methanethiol and related sulfur compounds. That's the cabbage-and-socks note. Its relative *Brevibacterium epidermidis* lives between your toes and does much the same with the protein in shed skin. Staphylococci on both surfaces turn leucine into isovaleric acid, the sharp, cheesy-sweat smell. Washed rinds and feet are both damp, salty, protein-rich surfaces, so similar microbes grow on them and give off similar smells.

In the mid-1990s, Bart Knols and Ruurd de Jong in the Netherlands noticed that *Anopheles gambiae*, the main malaria vector in Africa, mostly bit people around the feet and ankles. Washing feet with deodorant soap moved the bites elsewhere. On a hunch, Knols tested Limburger, and the mosquitoes went for it. The paper came out in 1996, and in 2006 it won an Ig Nobel Prize in Biology. Follow-up work used odour blends for trapping mosquitoes, with fatty acids added to ammonia and lactic acid. I'm less sure of the exact details of that follow-up work.

The twist is that the mosquito matches a few chemical markers rather than recognising a person, and anything that gives off the same markers counts as a person. Cheese can fool it, and a trap can too. Your incremental models' `unique_key` works the same way. It doesn't know which rows really are the same thing. It only knows which rows share those columns, so a key that's slightly too coarse will quietly merge records that should stay separate.
