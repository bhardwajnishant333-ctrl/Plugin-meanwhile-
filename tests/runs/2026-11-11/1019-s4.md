Eval loss is mostly a measure of how well the model copies your reference SQL's style. Whether the query is correct hinges on a handful of tokens.

**How it works:** Cross-entropy is averaged over every token, and most tokens in a SQL query are easy: `SELECT`, `FROM`, commas, aliases, the house formatting. A fine-tune drives those toward near-zero loss quickly. The tokens that actually decide correctness are a small minority: the right column out of 40 similar ones, the join key, `LEFT` vs `INNER`, the date filter. If the model gets slightly better at boilerplate and slightly worse at picking columns, the average still goes down. Two other things make it worse:

- **Teacher forcing.** Eval loss scores each token with the *correct* prefix already in place. When generating, the model builds on its own output, so a wrong table alias early on throws off everything after it. Loss never sees that cascade.
- **Valid alternatives get penalised.** `a JOIN b` vs `b JOIN a`, a CTE vs a subquery, `COUNT(*)` vs `COUNT(id)` can all return the same result, but only the reference's form scores well. Lower loss partly means "writes it the way your labelers did."

It can also go the other way: loss can *rise* while accuracy improves. A few confidently wrong tokens get huge penalties even as more queries come out right.

The line everyone quotes as Goodhart's Law, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure," isn't his. It's anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's 1997 paraphrase. Goodhart's 1975 wording was narrower and better: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." He was writing about the Bank of England's money-supply targets, and the US then ran the same experiment at much larger scale.

**How it played out:** In the 1960s and 70s, M1 velocity (nominal GDP divided by M1) moved along a steady, predictable trend. So if you controlled M1, you more or less controlled nominal spending. On October 6, 1979, Volcker's Fed switched to targeting reserves and money growth and let the fed funds rate float. The rate swung wildly and peaked near 20% in 1981. Meanwhile the thing being targeted changed underneath them. The 1980 deregulation act brought interest-bearing checking (NOW accounts went nationwide in 1981), and money market funds boomed. Money moved between "M1" and "not M1" for reasons that had nothing to do with spending, and the steady velocity trend broke down. The Fed de-emphasized M1 in late 1982 and stopped setting M1 targets in 1987. In 1993 Greenspan told Congress M2 was being downgraded as a guide too. After that the funds rate was the tool, and money aggregates became just one input.

**The twist:** The regularity had two reasons to break, and only one of them was Goodhart's. One was mechanical: deregulation changed what "money" meant. The other was behavioral: once the target exists, banks and savers react to it, which is the Lucas critique (1976, the same era). People blame the target for both, but you'd have to fix them differently. Better definitions solve the first one. Only a target the system can't route around solves the second.
