**Falconers were doing feedforward control centuries before anyone wrote it down, and your fermenter probably needs it too.**

A falconer weighs the bird every day, usually to the gram. Each bird has a "flying weight": the narrow band where it's keen to hunt and still comes back to the glove. Too heavy and it ignores you or flies off. Too light and it's weak. That's a feedback loop: the scale is the sensor, food is the actuator, and flying weight is the setpoint.

Good falconers don't wait for the scale, though. On a cold night the bird burns more energy just staying warm. So the falconer feeds more *before* the cold arrives, going by the forecast rather than by tomorrow's weight. That's feedforward: you act on a disturbance you can see coming instead of waiting for the error to show up.

Your controller has a disturbance like that. Yeast give off heat. At peak activity, an ale often runs a few °C above the air around it, and the heat builds over hours, not minutes. A plain PID or thermostat on a fridge only sees the error after the beer has already drifted. The compressor's minimum off-time (usually a few minutes, to protect it) adds even more lag. That's why many homebrew controllers overshoot during the first 48 hours, then sit perfectly still once fermentation slows down.

**The twist:** feedback can't beat a disturbance that's large, slow and coming from inside the thing you're controlling. You need a signal that predicts it. For the hawk, that's the weather. For your beer, it could be the time since pitching, how fast the airlock is bubbling, or the gap between the beer probe and the air probe (if that gap widens, the yeast are heating up). A small feedforward term from that gap, added to your output, can cut the overshoot more than any amount of PID tuning.

**Your PID's integrator already estimates the yeast's heat output. It just does it too slowly. Calculate that heat directly and you get feedforward almost for free.**

**1. Use two loops (cascade), not one.**
- Outer loop: beer temp → *air* setpoint (slow PID).
- Inner loop: air temp → compressor or heater on/off, with hysteresis and the minimum on/off times enforced here.
BrewPi uses the same structure. It keeps the compressor's off-time apart from the beer math.

**2. Estimate the yeast's heat with a heat balance.**
The heat the yeast make either warms the beer or leaks out to the air:

`Q_yeast ≈ C·dT_beer/dt + UA·(T_beer − T_air)`

- `C` is the beer's thermal mass: about 4.2 kJ/K per litre, so roughly 84 kJ/K for 20 L.
- `UA` is how fast heat leaks from fermenter to air. Measure it once: put a fermenter of plain water at a different temperature from the chamber, log both probes, and fit the exponential decay. No yeast means `Q_yeast` = 0, so whatever you measure is UA.

**3. Feed it forward.**
To carry away `Q` in steady state, the air has to sit `Q/UA` below the beer. So:

```cpp
float q   = C * dTbeer_dt + UA * (Tbeer - Tair);
float ff  = q / UA;                       // °C of extra cooling needed
float air_sp = beer_sp - pid.update(err) - ff;
air_sp = constrain(air_sp, beer_sp - 8, beer_sp + 5);
```

**4. Gotchas:**
- At 12-bit resolution, a DS18B20 reads in 0.0625 °C steps. A derivative taken from neighbouring samples is just noise. Fit a slope over a 15–30 minute window instead.
- Don't let the integrator wind up. Freeze it while `air_sp` is clamped *or* while the compressor is held off by its minimum off-time.
- Put the beer probe in a thermowell. A probe taped to the outside mostly reads the air.

**The twist:** `Q_yeast` is a fermentation-activity sensor you didn't have to buy. Plot it and you'll see the rise, the peak, and the tail-off, which tells you when to start a diacetyl rest or cold crash without opening the fermenter.

**In falconry, overshooting the setpoint in one direction loses you the bird. Overshooting in the other direction can kill it.**

**What weighing actually measures.** The scale reads grams, but what the falconer cares about is energy reserve and hunger. Grams are only a stand-in, and a noisy one: food still in the crop, a casting (pellet) not yet thrown up, and the time of day all shift the reading. So they weigh at the same time every day, with the crop empty. It's sensor calibration done by routine.

**The ramp limit.** Weight comes down gradually, a little each day, never in one big cut. Falconers rarely do that by feeding less meat. They change how nourishing the meat is. "Washed meat" is meat soaked in water so the bird feels full but gets fewer calories. Lean meat is fed when they want the weight to drop, and richer food like quail when they want it to rise. They control nutrition per gram, not the grams fed.

**The nonlinearity.** How much margin a bird has scales with its size. A big female Harris's hawk can handle a sloppy day. A merlin or a kestrel weighs a few hundred grams or less and has very little reserve. Too low, combined with one cold night, is a well-known way to lose a small falcon. Falconers call a bird that's too low "low" or "light", and an experienced falconer worries more about light than about fat.

**The setpoint moves.** As the bird gets fitter, muscle replaces fat. A fit hawk is often keen at a *higher* weight than it was at the start of the season. A falconer who holds last month's number is starving a bird that no longer needs it.

**The twist:** the two errors don't cost the same. Too heavy, and the bird ignores you or flies off: annoying, and you can get it back. Too light can be fatal. So falconers come at the target from above and accept being slow. Your beer has the same lopsided cost. Overshoot warm early in fermentation and you get fusel alcohols and esters you can't remove. Undershoot cool and the yeast just slow down. That's the reasoning behind "pitch cool, then let it rise". Your controller can do the same: give the outer loop asymmetric limits, so the air setpoint has much more room to go cold than warm during the first 72 hours.

**Meanwhile:**
**A small falcon can starve to dangerous levels overnight because its reserves shrink with its size faster than its fuel use does.**

**The scaling.** Metabolic rate scales roughly with body mass to the power 0.75 (Kleiber's law). Energy reserves scale roughly with mass itself. So the time it takes a fasting animal to burn through its reserves scales with about mass to the 0.25. Run the numbers: a 200 g falcon empties its tank in roughly two-thirds the time a 1 kg hawk does. Small bodies also lose heat faster, so a cold night makes it worse. A falconer who misjudges by a few grams can find a bird in trouble by morning. It isn't a gradual decline: once blood sugar crashes, the brain, which runs on glucose, fails fast. Falconers describe underweight small hawks having seizures or "fits", and the standard first aid is quick sugar by mouth. (The exact clinical thresholds are vet territory. I'm confident about the pattern, not the numbers.)

**The systems lesson.** What protects a system is buffer time: reserve divided by burn rate. While the buffer lasts, mistakes don't cost much. When it runs out, everything goes at once.

**The twist, for your build:** your *beer* has a huge buffer. About 84 kJ/K of thermal mass means it drifts over hours. Your *firmware* has almost none. Three places that bites:

1. **A power blip.** The ESP32 reboots in about a second and its minimum-off timer goes back to zero. If the compressor was just running, restarting it immediately against high pressure can stall it or trip its overload. Fix: start the compressor lockout at boot, as if it had just switched off.
2. **Boot glitches.** Some ESP32 pins (the strapping pins and the UART pins: GPIO 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 12, 15) can flick high or output signals during boot. A relay on one of those can briefly kick the compressor. Use a pin that stays quiet at boot, and add a pull-down so the relay defaults to off.
3. **A hang.** If the loop hangs while a relay is on, the controller can't switch it off, so the fridge keeps cooling or the heater keeps heating. Turn on the task watchdog, and add a hard limit on how long the heater can stay on.

1. **Deeper**: designing a safe fallback for when a probe dies: what the controller should do with one sensor down or both.
2. **Sideways**: the Therac-25. A radiation therapy machine where removing the hardware safety locks and trusting the software killed patients.
3. **Wildcard**: why hummingbirds avoid starving at night by going into torpor, dropping their body temperature to save energy.
