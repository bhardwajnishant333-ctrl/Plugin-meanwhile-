**Meanwhile:**
Vision transformers quietly turn some background patches into scratch memory, and it can make their attention maps misleading.

**How it works:** In "Vision Transformers Need Registers" (Darcet, Oquab, Mairal and Bojanowski, ICLR 2024), the Meta/Inria team found a small set of tokens with very high norms in DINOv2, OpenCLIP and DeiT-III. These tokens sit on low-information patches like sky, wall or plain soil. The model drops their local pixel content and uses them to store global information. That is why attention maps from these models show bright spikes in empty background. The fix was simple: add a few extra learnable "register" tokens that the model is free to use as scratch space, then throw them away at the output. The spikes disappear and the attention maps become clean.

**The twist for your run:** The artifacts were strongest in larger, longer-trained models, so a ViT-B/16 may show them only weakly. I'm not sure how strong they are at base size. Either way, if anyone plans to show agronomists attention maps as "here's where the model saw the disease", check them first. A bright spot on the soil may be scratch memory, not evidence. A quick test is to plot the token norms at a middle layer on 20 validation images. Real artifacts show up as a few tokens with norms far above the rest.

First, a correction. I phrased that option as if ML papers actually use the term "frozen reward signal". I don't know of any that do, so I shouldn't have put it that way. The analogy itself does hold up, so here it is honestly.

A fixed farmgate price works like a `.detach()` in your computation graph. The loss moves, but no gradient reaches the parameters that could reduce it.

**How it works:** Friedrich Hayek made the economic version of this argument in "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (American Economic Review, 1945). No central planner can know what millions of local actors know. A price compresses all of that into one number that tells each person which way to move, so it acts like a gradient. In cocoa in 2024, the world price (the loss) jumped. But Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana fix the farmgate price for the season, so the signal stopped at the marketing board and never reached the farmers' planting and replanting decisions. The supply side kept running in eval mode.

**The twist:** The fixed price isn't simply a mistake. These systems exist to stop farmers' incomes swinging with every move in the market, and in a price crash they protect farmers. In your terms, it's a very low learning rate plus heavy clipping on the farmers' side. That gives low variance, but the system can't adapt when the world shifts for real, and the trees add a 3–5 year lag on top. The same trade-off shows up in your run if you froze the early ViT blocks. That's stable and cheap, but if your cassava photos differ from ImageNet in texture or lighting, the frozen layers can't learn the difference.
