**The story that Ögedei Khan's death saved Europe in 1242 has a hole in it: the general who turned back, Batu, never went to the funeral politics.**

The textbook version goes like this. In April 1241 the Mongols destroyed the Hungarian army at Mohi. Ögedei died that December. In 1242 the army withdrew so the princes could go home and elect his successor. But Batu, the prince who led the withdrawal, didn't go to the election. He stayed on the Volga, and the kurultai that finally chose Güyük met in 1246 without him. So "they went home to vote" doesn't really explain it.

In 2016, Ulf Büntgen and Nicola Di Cosmo published a paper in *Scientific Reports* with another explanation. Tree rings and written sources point to a warm, dry run of summers and then a cold, wet spring in 1242. That flooded the Hungarian plain, ruined the pasture and made the ground marshy. A cavalry army that needs several horses per rider can't run on mud and no grass. They argue it was probably several causes together, with the weather playing a real part, rather than one decisive event. Historians still argue about it, and the paper doesn't claim to settle it.

**The twist:** this is a postmortem problem. The story that lasted for 800 years had one clean root cause that fit the politics. The better explanation is a set of contributing factors, including a boring one about the environment. That's the same pull that makes "the deploy broke it" more satisfying than "the deploy plus a full disk plus the tired on-call person". Clear stories survive. That doesn't make them true.

**Meanwhile:**
**The Mongol Empire's fastest network had the same weak spot as a lot of modern ones: bearer tokens that never expired.**

The *yam* was a chain of relay stations across the empire, often described as about a day's ride apart, roughly 25 to 40 miles. Each station kept fresh horses, food and staff, supplied by the households around it. Ögedei expanded it in the 1230s, and the *Secret History* has him counting the post stations among his great achievements. Marco Polo says urgent riders with bells on their belts could cover a couple of hundred miles a day by switching horses. That's his claim and I can't confirm the number.

Access came from the *paiza*, a tablet of gold, silver or wood depending on rank. Whoever carried one could demand horses, food and lodging at any station. Station staff didn't check who you were. They checked the tablet.

**The twist:** the tablets were easy to give out and hard to take back. Princes, officials and merchants piled them up and used them for private business, which wore out the horses and the households that fed them. In the Ilkhanate around 1300, Ghazan Khan's vizier Rashid al-Din describes stations crushed by paiza holders, and Ghazan reformed the system by limiting who could issue tablets and putting real merchants back on their own transport. In other words, the empire hit the problem of long-lived bearer tokens with no expiry, and the fix was the same as now: limit who can issue them, narrow what they allow, and stop treating holding one as proof you're allowed.

**Meanwhile:**
**The first transatlantic telegraph cable was killed by the man trying to make it work harder.**

The 1858 cable was finished on 5 August after two ships, HMS *Agamemnon* and USS *Niagara*, met mid-ocean and laid it toward opposite shores. It worked, but very slowly. Queen Victoria's 98-word greeting to President Buchanan took about 16 hours to get through. The cable was so long that each pulse came out the far end smeared and faint.

There was a theory for this already. In the mid-1850s William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) worked out that a cable this long acts like a long capacitor, so the delay grows roughly with the *square* of its length. His answer was to keep the voltage low and build a much more sensitive receiver, the mirror galvanometer, which bounced a beam of light off a tiny magnet so a faint current made a big, visible movement. The company's chief electrician, Wildman Whitehouse, didn't accept the theory. He pushed the signals through with induction coils at around 2,000 volts. The insulation, which was gutta-percha with known defects, broke down, and the cable went dead in early September, about three weeks in.

A public inquiry followed, and a working Atlantic cable didn't arrive until 1866, laid by the *Great Eastern*, with Thomson's ideas and instruments at its heart.

**The twist:** when the signal was weak, more force destroyed the channel. What fixed it was a better detector. It's the same mistake as raising retries, timeouts or replica counts on a struggling link when the real problem is that you can't see clearly what's happening. Sometimes the answer is better instruments, not more power.

1) **Deeper:** Thomson's law of squares and why it still describes on-chip wires and long cable runs
2) **Sideways:** the Mongol yam meets the telegraph, in how Britain's "all-red" cable network shaped its empire
3) **Wildcard:** the 1859 Carrington solar storm, when telegraph lines sparked and some ran with their batteries unplugged
