**A starling in a murmuration doesn't watch the birds within a set distance. It watches about seven, however far away they are.**

In 2008, the STARFLAG project (Ballerini, Cavagna and colleagues, PNAS) filmed flocks of up to a few thousand starlings over Rome. They used stereo cameras to rebuild each bird's 3D position. They tested two rules for how a bird decides whom to follow:

- **Metric:** follow every bird within X meters.
- **Topological:** follow your nearest N birds, wherever they are.

The data fit the topological rule. Each bird tracked about 6 to 7 neighbors, and that number held whether the flock was packed tight or spread out. A 2013 follow-up (Young, Leonard and others, PLOS Computational Biology) found that about 7 is close to the best trade-off: it lets a group reach consensus under noisy perception without each bird paying too much attention.

**The twist:** a metric rule breaks when density changes. A stretched flock would split into groups that can't see each other, and a compressed one would overload every bird. A fixed neighbor count keeps the flock connected at any density. That's how a wave of turning can cross thousands of birds in under a second with no leader. In 2010, Cavagna's group showed that a bird's changes in speed and heading are correlated with birds across the whole flock, however big it gets. Every bird is effectively linked to all the others through chains of 7.

**In 1961, British bakers found they could replace time with energy. The real prize turned out to be which wheat they could use.**

A correction to how I teased this: Chorleywood didn't turn hours into minutes for the whole loaf. It removed the long bulk fermentation step, which usually took around three hours. Flour to packaged loaf still takes about three and a half hours.

**How it works:** the British Baking Industries Research Association, at its lab in Chorleywood, Hertfordshire, worked out that dough mainly needs a fixed amount of mechanical work to develop its gluten network. That figure is about 11 watt-hours per kilogram. A long fermentation does that slowly. A high-speed mixer does it in about 3 to 5 minutes, helped by added fat, extra yeast and an oxidant. That oxidant was originally potassium bromate, which the UK banned in 1990, and is now ascorbic acid. The Chorleywood Bread Process went on to make most British bread. The figure usually quoted is around 80%, which I'd treat as approximate.

**The twist:** before 1961, British bakers needed imported hard wheat, mostly from Canada, because softer British flour couldn't make a good loaf the slow way. Intense mixing made up for the weaker flour. A process change became a supply-chain change: it moved Britain's flour buying from imported to domestic wheat.
