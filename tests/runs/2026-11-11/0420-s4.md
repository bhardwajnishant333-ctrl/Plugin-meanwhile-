**Meanwhile:**
The Soviets probably didn't need to buy a single futures contract. The firms that sold them the grain did the buying for them, and the US government covered the bill.

**What's documented.** The US–Soviet agreement of July 8, 1972 gave the Soviets $750 million in credit over three years for buying US grain. They used up that amount and more within weeks. Investigations followed in 1973, by the GAO and by Senator Henry Jackson's Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations. They pieced together the mechanics:

- **Exporters hedged themselves.** A firm that agrees to deliver millions of bushels at a fixed price is effectively short the grain. The standard hedge is to buy futures. When several houses each did that for their secret deals, their combined buying pushed prices up. The Soviets' prices were already locked, so the rally cost them nothing.
- **The subsidy covered the exporters' risk.** USDA paid exporters the gap between the rising domestic price and an export target of about $1.65 a bushel. As prices climbed, the subsidy grew, to close to 50 cents a bushel by late summer. Exporters rushed to register their sales before USDA ended the program that autumn. In effect, the government had sold the market an unpriced call option on wheat.
- **The conflict of interest.** Clarence Palmby, the USDA official who helped set up the credit deal, left in June 1972 to join Continental Grain, one of the main sellers. Investigators looked at this closely. I'm not aware of any finding that he broke the law, but it fed the public view that the system was rigged.

**What's rumored.** People have long claimed that Soviet agents also quietly bought Chicago futures before the news broke. As far as I know, the investigations never documented any Soviet futures position, so treat that as folklore unless someone produces a source.

**The twist.** The best trade in the whole episode never touched an exchange. The Soviets got a long position at a locked price, and the exchange buying and the subsidy handled everything else. When you look for who has an edge, follow who carries the risk, not who places the trades.
