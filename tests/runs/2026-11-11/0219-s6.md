Hook: until late 2024, S3 had no way to say "only overwrite this if nobody else has since I read it."

**How it works:** S3 has been strongly consistent since December 2020. A read after a write always returns the new data. But consistency and safe concurrent writes are different problems. Two clients can both read version A, both make an edit, and both PUT. The second PUT silently replaces the first. Then AWS added conditional writes in two steps:
- **August 2024:** `If-None-Match: *` on PutObject means "create this only if the key doesn't exist yet."
- **November 2024:** `If-Match: <ETag>` means "overwrite only if the object still has the ETag I read." That's a true compare-and-swap. A lost race comes back as HTTP 412 Precondition Failed. I think a write racing another in-flight write can also return a 409, but check that in the docs.

In Go SDK v2 these are just the `IfMatch` and `IfNoneMatch` fields on `PutObjectInput`.

**Why it matters:** For about 18 years, any tool that needed safe writes to S3 had to run a lock service next to it. Delta Lake and Terraform's S3 backend both used DynamoDB for locking. Terraform 1.10 (late 2024) added `use_lockfile`, which locks state in S3 alone and makes the DynamoDB table optional. So "bucket plus lock table" is no longer the only design: the bucket can now act as the referee.

For you: if your sync keeps a manifest or index object, an `If-Match` PUT on it gives you optimistic concurrency with no lock server. Two devices race, one gets a 412, and that device re-reads and merges. A 412 tells you exactly when a conflict happened, so your resolver doesn't need to guess from timestamps.

Myth: a sandpiper probing the mud is hunting worms. A lot of the time, a western sandpiper is grazing the mud's skin.

**How it works:** The shine on a mudflat at low tide is biofilm, a thin film of diatoms, bacteria and mucus. In 2008, Tomohiro Kuwae and colleagues (in the journal *Ecology*) used stable isotopes and stomach contents to show that western sandpipers (*Calidris mauri*) eat a lot of it. By their estimate it was roughly half the diet, though I'm not sure of the exact figure. Bob Elner's group had already described how they do it. The tip of the tongue is covered in fine keratin bristles. The bird presses it to the mud, scrapes off the film, and moves it up the bill using surface tension, the same trick phalaropes use on water droplets.

**The twist:** That makes some shorebirds partly grazers, like tiny cattle on a mud pasture. It also changes what protecting a stopover site means. You have to look after the invertebrates, and also the conditions that let a microbial film grow: sediment, nutrients and how much the tide exposes. Dredging or fresher water can starve the birds while every worm is still there. It also means the sandpiper you see "pecking at nothing" is very likely feeding.

US time zones were set up by the railroads, and for 35 years the federal government didn't recognise them.

**What happened:** Before 1883, most towns set their clocks by local solar noon. The railroads ran on dozens of their own "railroad times", often cited as about 50, and stations sometimes had several clocks showing different times. William F. Allen, secretary of the railroads' General Time Convention, proposed a handful of zones with one-hour steps. On **November 18, 1883**, the "Day of Two Noons," the railroads switched over. Towns on the eastern side of a zone reached noon, set their clocks back, and had noon again. No law required any of it. Congress only made the zones official with the **Standard Time Act of 1918**, and some cities kept local time for years in the meantime.

**The twist:** A shared clock didn't need an authority. It needed the one group whose collisions were the worst, here quite literally trains, to agree on a convention. Everyone else took it up because syncing with the railroad was cheaper than not. The law came last and simply wrote down what people were already doing.
