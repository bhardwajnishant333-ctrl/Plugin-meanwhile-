**The Byzantines kept their best weapon so secret that they lost it themselves.**

Greek fire first shows up around 672. The chronicler Theophanes credits Kallinikos, an architect who fled Syria for Constantinople. **The most famous problem in distributed systems got its name because nobody was left who could complain about it.**

The core result came out in 1980, in a paper by Pease, Shostak and Lamport called "Reaching Agreement in the Presence of Faults". It had no story attached, and Lamport said later that he noticed how much attention Dijkstra's Dining Philosophers had won simply by having a story. So for the 1982 follow-up he wrote one. His first draft used *Albanian* generals, because Albania was isolated enough at the time that he didn't expect anyone there to object. His colleague Jack Goldberg pointed out that plenty of Albanians lived outside Albania. So Lamport switched to Byzantium, an empire with no living citizens to be offended. The paper was published as "The Byzantine Generals Problem" in *ACM TOPLAS* in 1982.

The math is the part that matters. If some generals are traitors who can send different messages to different people, you need **3f+1** generals to tolerate **f** traitors. With 3 generals and 1 traitor, agreement is impossible. If messages are signed and can't be forged, that limit goes away.

**The twist:** almost nothing in your stack pays that cost. Raft (behind etcd, and so the Kubernetes API that CloudNativePG relies on to decide which instance is primary) and Postgres replication both assume nodes **crash, they don't lie**. That's why they only need 2f+1 nodes. Split-brain is what happens when that assumption breaks. A primary that's partly working, still accepting writes after it has lost its lease, is the "traitor" a Byzantine-tolerant system is designed to handle. Fencing (forcibly cutting off a node so it can't keep writing) is the cheap fix: instead of out-voting a liar, you cut off its ability to talk. I'm fairly confident about the CloudNativePG details at that level, but not about their exact fencing internals.

1) **Deeper:** why the 3f+1 bound can't be beaten, using the 3-general impossibility proof, which fits in about four sentences
2) **Sideways:** the first real-world Byzantine fault that nearly hit something important: the Space Shuttle's computers, and why NASA flew 4+1 of them
3) **Wildcard:** the Byzantines never called themselves Byzantine. The name was invented after the empire fell, and the person behind it was a German librarian.

Small correction first: the teaser oversold it. Hieronymus Wolf didn't *invent* the word "Byzantine". He made it the standard name for the empire. You probably know this part, so I'll keep it short and get to the less obvious bit.

**"Byzantine Empire" is a name its enemies gave it, and the Byzantines won the argument about who they were anyway.**

Wolf was a German humanist who worked as librarian to the Fugger banking family in Augsburg. In 1557 he published a collection of the empire's historians under the title *Corpus Historiae Byzantinae*. French scholars under Louis XIV turned it into a field of study, starting with the Louvre series of Byzantine texts from 1648, with Du Cange as a leading editor. In the 18th century Montesquieu and Gibbon turned "Byzantine" into an insult meaning decadent and convoluted. That's the meaning the word still carries today, and it's partly why the generals in Lamport's paper are read as traitors.

The renaming started long before Wolf. Once Charlemagne was crowned emperor in Rome in 800, the Latin West had a political reason not to call Constantinople's rulers "Roman" any more, so it called them "Greeks". The people themselves kept calling themselves *Romaioi*, Romans, to the end.

**The twist:** the name that actually survived was theirs, and their conqueror spread it. After 1453 Mehmed II took the title *Kayser-i Rûm*, Caesar of Rome. The Ottomans grouped the empire's Orthodox Christians as the *Rum millet*, the Roman community. In Turkish, "Rum" still means Greeks from the old Byzantine world, like the Greeks of Istanbul. The West renamed the empire in its books, but the people who conquered it kept the name the Byzantines used for themselves.
