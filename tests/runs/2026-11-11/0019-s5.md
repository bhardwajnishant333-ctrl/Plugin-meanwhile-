The yeast in your starter can't eat the main sugar in your flour, and it survives anyway.

**How it works:** Flour enzymes break starch down mostly into maltose. In many classic starters the dominant yeast is *Kazachstania humilis* (it used to be called *Candida milleri*), and it can't ferment maltose. The dominant bacterium, *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, eats maltose first. It splits each maltose with an enzyme called maltose phosphorylase, keeps one half, and pushes the leftover glucose out of the cell. The yeast lives on that glucose. So the two main residents don't compete for food: one feeds the other. The bacteria also make the starter acidic, which keeps most newcomers out. Lactic acid bacteria usually outnumber the yeast by around 100 to 1. Treat that as a rough figure, because it varies a lot from starter to starter.

**The twist:** Where you live matters less than you'd think. The Global Sourdough Project (Landis et al., *eLife*, 2021) sampled about 500 starters and found that geography explained very little of what lived in them. "San Francisco" bacteria turn up everywhere. A starter stays stable because of this feeding arrangement, not because of local wild microbes. The study also found starter microbes on the bakers' hands. Your starter is partly you.

**Meanwhile:**
Proptest can shrink a failing input down to almost nothing, unless the input came out of `prop_flat_map`. There it often gets stuck on something messy.

**How it works:** QuickCheck (Claessen & Hughes, 2000) kept shrinking separate from generating. Each type had its own `shrink` function, and it knew nothing about the generator's rules. So a shrunk value could break invariants your generator guaranteed, like a sorted vec that's no longer sorted. Proptest fixes this with "integrated shrinking". Every strategy produces a `ValueTree` with `current()`, `simplify()` and `complicate()`, so a value shrinks according to the same rules that built it. `prop_map` keeps that working. `prop_flat_map` is the weak spot: when the outer value shrinks, proptest has to build a new inner strategy, so the inner value gets regenerated and loses its shrinking progress. Hedgehog, Haskell's version of the same design, has the same problem with monadic bind, which is the same operation as flat_map. I'm confident the weakness exists. I'm less sure how proptest's internals work around it.

**The twist:** Hypothesis (David MacIver) avoids the problem by shrinking the recorded sequence of random choices that produced the value, not the value itself. Every composition shrinks for free, flatmap and filter included, and the result is always something the generator could have produced. The method is written up in MacIver & Donaldson, ECOOP 2020.

What this means for your agent's proptest suites: when one value depends on another, draw both independently and combine them with `prop_map`. For example, generate a `Vec<LogLine>` plus a `proptest::sample::Index` and use the index to pick a line, instead of using `flat_map` from a length to a vec. The failures you get back will be much smaller.

1) **deeper**: how Hypothesis's shrinker reorders and deletes chunks of the choice sequence, and why that beats shrinking each field separately
2) **sideways**: delta debugging (Zeller, 1999), the same "shrink the input" idea used on compiler crashes and 100MB logs

In 1852 Jean-Baptiste Boussingault sealed a loaf so it couldn't lose water. It went stale anyway, and when he reheated it, it came back fresh.

**How it works:** Staling is mostly not drying out. It's starch recrystallizing, a process called retrogradation. Baking melts the starch's crystal structure into a soft gel. As the loaf cools, the amylose sets within hours, and the amylopectin slowly packs back into stiff crystals over days. That's why the crumb gets firm and crumbly. Water does move from the crumb to the crust, which is why the crust goes leathery, but the crumb firms up even in a sealed bag. Amylopectin crystals melt at roughly 55–60°C, so a few minutes in a hot oven undoes the staling for a while. Then it starts again, and faster.

**The twist:** The fridge is the worst place for bread. Recrystallization runs fastest just above freezing, and refrigerated bread is often said to stale about 3 times faster than bread at room temperature (I'm fairly but not fully sure of that number). Freezing stops it almost completely, because the starch can't move. So the options are counter, freezer, or toaster, never the fridge. Sourdough's acidity is generally reported to slow staling too. I'm less sure how big that effect is compared with factors like hydration and fat.

**Meanwhile:**
A supermarket loaf that stays soft for two weeks usually isn't full of preservatives against staling. It has an enzyme that edits the starch while the bread bakes and is destroyed before baking finishes.

**How it works:** The main one is maltogenic alpha-amylase, best known as Novozymes' Novamyl from around 1990, and it comes from a *Geobacillus* (formerly *Bacillus*) *stearothermophilus* gene. Staling happens when amylopectin's short outer branches line up and crystallize. This enzyme snips two-glucose units off the ends of those outer branches. That makes them too short to pack into crystals, but it leaves the main starch structure intact. The obvious alternative, an enzyme that cuts the starch backbone at random, gives you a gummy, sticky crumb, because it breaks the structure that holds the bread up. The maltogenic enzyme is heat-tolerant enough to work while the starch gelatinizes in the oven, then it denatures before the loaf is done, so almost no active enzyme is left in the bread. Because it's inactive by the end, it's usually classed as a processing aid, and in many places it doesn't have to be listed on the label. That varies by country, so take it as a general pattern, not a rule.

**The twist:** The fix doesn't block the bad process (recrystallization). It removes the specific short structures that let it happen, and it only works during a limited window. That's a design pattern: when you can't stop a slow failure, remove what it needs to get started. A sourdough baker gets part of the same effect for free, because the long fermentation lets the flour's own enzymes and acid start modifying the starch first. How big that effect is compared with the industrial enzyme, I'm not sure.

1) **deeper**: why some starch crystals in bread are a feature: "resistant starch" and the glycemic-index angle
2) **sideways**: the bounded-work idea in software, from fuel limits on regex engines (backtracking caps) to proptest's own `max_shrink_iters`
3) **wildcard**: how the 1928 discovery of penicillin almost didn't happen because of the weather
