**Medieval churches were the biggest customers of medieval beekeeping, and they wanted the wax, not the honey.**

So a mistake in natural history created centuries of price-insensitive demand: buyers paying for what the wax meant, not for what it did.

**A swarm of about 10,000 bees picks a new home without a leader, and it gets the choice right most of the time. The queen gets no vote.**

How it works (Thomas Seeley's research, summed up in *Honeybee Democracy*, 2010):
- **Search**: A few hundred scouts fly out and inspect cavities. They're looking for about 40 litres of volume, a small entrance and some height off the ground.
- **Campaign**: Each scout comes back and waggle-dances for her site. Better sites get longer dances, which recruit more scouts to go and check.
- **Decay**: Each time a scout returns, she dances fewer circuits for the same site, until she stops. Support for a weak site fades away on its own instead of hardening into a faction.
- **Cross-inhibition**: Scouts headbutt dancers who are promoting rival sites and give a short "stop signal" (Seeley et al., *Science*, 2012). It works much like mutual inhibition between neurons.
- **Commit**: The vote isn't counted back at the swarm. It's counted at the site. When enough scouts are there at the same time (roughly 20 to 30), they switch to "piping", a sound that tells the swarm to warm its flight muscles to about 35°C. Then the whole cluster takes off.

The twist for an engineer: two pieces of this match things you'd build on purpose.
- **Decaying advocacy works like a lease or timeout.** No scout can keep a stale vote alive forever, so the swarm can't deadlock between two good sites. A quorum without expiry can get stuck. A quorum with expiry eventually converges.
- **The quorum threshold sets the speed/accuracy trade-off.** Raise it and the choice is better but takes longer, with the swarm hanging exposed on a branch. Lower it and the swarm moves fast but sometimes picks worse.

It isn't Paxos, though. Nothing guarantees safety. Swarms occasionally split, with two groups committing to different sites. Seeley has described these cases, and they usually end with the swarm regrouping. Evolution settled for "almost always" instead of "provably".
