Godot 4 quietly changed how your dice roll: the global random generator now seeds itself on startup.

In Godot 3, unless you called `randomize()`, `randi()` and `rand_range()` gave the same sequence on every run. A lot of roguelike code relied on that, often without anyone noticing: fixed test dungeons, reproducible bug reports, "seed 1234 always has the stairs next to the shop". From Godot 4.0 the global generator is randomized on project load. So if your code used the global functions and never set a seed, the conversion compiles cleanly but your deterministic runs aren't deterministic anymore. On top of that, `rand_range` became `randf_range`. I'm not sure the same seed gives identical numbers across the two versions, so don't assume old seeds reproduce old maps.

The twist is that the fix is also a design upgrade. Make your own `RandomNumberGenerator` instances, one per job: map generation, combat, cosmetic effects. Brogue does a version of this. It keeps a "substantive" stream separate from a "cosmetic" one, so a sparkle animation can't change which monster spawns. If one global stream drives everything, adding a single extra combat roll reshuffles every dungeon that comes after it. When the port finishes, search for `randi`, `randf` and `rand_range`.

Algol is a clock that runs early and late, and the error comes from where the clock is.

Algol's eclipses repeat every 2.867 days, about 2 days 20 hours 49 minutes. John Goodricke timed them by eye in 1782–83 and suggested a companion passing in front as the cause. But the eclipsing pair isn't alone: a third star, Algol C, orbits the pair about every 680 days (roughly 1.86 years). As the pair swings around the system's shared centre of mass, it sits a little nearer to us for part of that cycle and a little farther for the rest. The light has to travel that extra distance, so eclipses show up early, then late, in a slow wave. My rough estimate is a few minutes each way; I'm not confident of the exact amplitude. Plot observed minus calculated eclipse times (an O−C diagram) and the wave is there. Longer drifts sit on top of it, which people blame on mass transfer between the stars and on magnetic cycles. Those explanations are still argued over.

The twist is that this is the same trick that found the first confirmed exoplanets. In 1992 Wolszczan and Frail timed the pulsar PSR B1257+12 and found its pulses wobbling early and late: planets were tugging it. Kepler later found planets through "transit timing variations", where an unseen planet shifts another planet's transits. A timing residual is a hidden mass you can measure. Your eclipse timings, minute by minute, are the same kind of instrument.

**Meanwhile:**
Pulsar timing arrays found gravitational waves with periods of years, and they did it by watching dozens of pulsar clocks drift in a way no single clock could show.

Millisecond pulsars spin hundreds of times a second, and the best of them can be timed to around 100 nanoseconds or better. A passing gravitational wave stretches and squeezes the space between Earth and each pulsar, so pulses arrive slightly early or late. It's the same O−C residual trick as Algol, scaled up to the galaxy. The catch is that one pulsar's wobble looks like noise: spin glitches, interstellar plasma, errors in our own clocks. In 1983 Hellings and Downs worked out what a real background of gravitational waves would leave behind. The residuals of any two pulsars should be correlated by a specific curve that depends only on their angle apart in the sky. It's a quadrupole fingerprint that noise shouldn't fake.

In June 2023, NANOGrav published its 15-year data on 68 pulsars, alongside the European/Indian, Australian and Chinese arrays. All of them reported evidence of that curve, at roughly 3 to 4 sigma. They called it "evidence", not a detection. The waves are nanohertz, with periods of years and wavelengths of light-years. The leading explanation is pairs of supermassive black holes slowly spiralling together across the universe. The signal came out somewhat stronger than many models expected, so more exotic sources aren't ruled out yet.

The twist is that the discovery isn't in any one measurement. It's in the correlation between measurements. Each clock is too noisy to trust alone, and the shape of how they all disagree together is the signal.

**Meanwhile:**
The Hellings–Downs curve dips below zero because gravity squeezes in two directions at once.

A gravitational wave doesn't push space one way. It stretches along one axis while squeezing along the axis at right angles, then swaps them. So take two pulsars about 90° apart in the sky: when one line of sight is being stretched, the other is being squeezed, and their timing residuals move in opposite directions. Two pulsars on opposite sides of the sky (180°) share the same axis, so they move together again. Plot correlation against angle and you get the curve: +0.5 for pulsars close together, down to about −0.15 near 82°, back up to +0.25 at 180°. The formula is Γ = (3/2)·x·ln x − x/4 + 1/2, with x = (1 − cos θ)/2. I worked those values out from it rather than recalling them.

The twist is that the shape also does the debugging. Every major source of error leaves its own fingerprint across the sky:
- **A bad clock on Earth** shifts every pulsar the same way, giving a flat correlation at all angles (a monopole).
- **A wrong model of the solar system's centre of mass**, for example a slightly-off mass for Jupiter, pushes pulsars on one side early and the other side late, giving a cosine-shaped dipole.
- **Gravitational waves** give the quadrupole-like Hellings–Downs curve.

Because the three shapes differ, you can fit all three at once and pull them apart. That's how the arrays told a cosmic signal apart from their own clock and ephemeris errors.
