**A trout looking up sees the whole sky squeezed into a circle about twice as wide as its depth. Everywhere else, the surface is a mirror.**

**How it works:** Water bends light (refractive index about 1.33). Any light entering from above, even light skimming in from the horizon, gets bent down into a cone about 97° wide. This is called Snell's window. The edge sits about 48.6° from straight up, which is the critical angle. Outside that circle, the underside of the surface reflects the streambed back down. For scale: a trout holding 6 inches deep has a window only about 14 inches across.

**The twist:** Before your dry fly drifts into the window, the trout can't see it at all. It sees only the tiny dents the fly's feet press into the surface film, which show up as sparkles of light in the mirror. Goddard and Clarke photographed this in *The Trout and the Fly* (1980). At the window's edge the light bends so much that the wings show up before the body does.

So on a small stream with shallow fish:
- The window is tiny, so your drift has to land almost in the fish's lane.
- How your fly sits on the film may matter more than its color.
- Near the edge of the window, everything above the bank is squashed into a thin band, including you. That's why crouching works.

**"Use a database as a job queue" was a well-known anti-pattern for about 15 years. A single Postgres clause added in 2016 is why Rails 8 now ships with exactly that.**

**How it works:** Workers used to fight over the same "next job" row. One would lock it and the rest would wait in line behind that lock, so throughput collapsed under load. Postgres 9.5 (January 2016) added `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`, and MySQL 8.0 followed in 2018. With it, a worker that finds a row already locked skips it and takes the next free one, instead of waiting. Solid Queue's workers claim jobs this way, so no Redis is needed.

**The twist:** The best argument for a database queue is enqueueing a job in the same transaction as the data it works on. That kills the classic Sidekiq bug where a job runs before the record it needs is committed and fails with `RecordNotFound`. But Rails 8's default setup puts Solid Queue in its own `queue` database, so that guarantee quietly goes away. If your app depended on Sidekiq-era `after_commit` workarounds, check two things in the upgrade diff: which database Solid Queue connects to, and how the job enqueue-after-commit setting is configured. I'm not sure of the current default for that setting across 7.2 and 8.x, so look in your config rather than trusting me.

**Meanwhile:**
**Paradox: closing a road can make everyone's commute faster. It isn't a hypothetical; cities have done it.**

**How it works:** Dietrich Braess proved this in 1968. Every driver picks the route that's fastest for them. Add a tempting shortcut and enough drivers pile onto it that everyone ends up slower than before. No driver can do better by switching on their own, so the bad outcome is stable (a Nash equilibrium). Seoul tore down the elevated Cheonggyecheon highway between 2003 and 2005. It carried well over 100,000 cars a day, and the expected gridlock never came. Traffic mostly redistributed.

**The twist:** The waste from selfishness has a ceiling. In 2002, Roughgarden and Tardos proved that when delays grow in a straight line with traffic, everyone choosing selfishly is at most 4/3 as slow as perfect central coordination. Never worse, regardless of how the network looks. They called this gap the "price of anarchy." It's one of the rare places where game theory gives you a hard number for "how much does no one being in charge cost?"

**Meanwhile:**
**The 4/3 limit only holds while delay rises in a straight line with traffic. Real roads aren't linear, and once delay climbs steeply, the limit falls apart.**

**The mechanism (Pigou's two-road example, 1920):**
- Road A always takes 1 hour.
- Road B takes x hours, where x is the share of drivers on it.
- Selfish result: everyone takes B, since it's never slower than A, so everyone spends 1 hour.
- Best result: send half to each road, and the average drops to 0.75 hours. 1 ÷ 0.75 = 4/3.

Now make road B's delay x^d, so it stays nearly empty-fast until it suddenly jams:
- Selfish drivers still all pile in and spend 1 hour.
- The best possible average gets close to 0, so the gap has no upper limit.

Roughgarden worked out the limits for each steepness:
- Straight line (d=1): 1.33
- d=2: about 1.63
- d=4: about 2.15

He also proved that the worst case is always this simple two-road network, no matter how complex the real map is.

**The twist:** d=4 isn't made up. The US Bureau of Public Roads' standard congestion formula, which traffic models still use, is a 4th-power curve. So the theory says selfish routing on real roads near capacity can cost up to about twice the best case. Pigou's 1920 fix still works: charge each driver for the delay they impose on everyone else. Then selfish choices match the best possible ones exactly. That's the theory behind congestion pricing in London (2003) and Manhattan (January 2025).

1) **Deeper:** why the "right" toll is so hard to calculate in practice
2) **Sideways:** the same steep curve shows up in your job queue. A Solid Queue worker pool near saturation has latency that shoots up the same way
