**Mount St. Helens didn't erupt because pressure built up past a limit. It erupted because the lid came off.**

By May 1980, magma pushing into the volcano had made its north flank bulge outward by about 1.5 meters a day. Geologists were watching that bulge closely. At 8:32 a.m. on May 18, a magnitude 5.1 earthquake shook it loose. The whole north face slid away in the largest landslide ever recorded, about 2.5 km³ of rock.

With that rock gone, the pressure on the gas-rich magma underneath dropped in seconds. The dissolved gas came out all at once, like opening a shaken soda. Because the lid was missing on one side, the blast went sideways instead of up. It flattened about 600 km² of forest, and 57 people died.

**The twist:** the bulge was a real warning sign, but the trigger was the opposite of what most people pictured. The rock on top had been holding the system together. Remove the weight and the magma doesn't need to grow at all, because the gas already in it does the damage. That's why volcanologists treat a flank collapse as its own hazard, separate from rising pressure.

**One volcano did what climate policy hasn't managed: it cooled the whole planet by about half a degree, and a climate model predicted it before it happened.**

Pinatubo, in the Philippines, erupted on 15 June 1991. It sent roughly 15 to 20 million tonnes of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, more than 20 km up. There's no rain at that height to wash it out. The gas turned into a fine haze of sulfate droplets that circled the globe within weeks and reflected some sunlight back to space. Average global temperature fell by about 0.5°C over 1992. In early 1992, James Hansen's team at NASA published a forecast of that cooling, and it turned out close. That made Pinatubo one of the earliest strong tests of climate models.

**The twist:** this is the main real-world evidence for stratospheric aerosol injection, the idea of cooling Earth on purpose by copying Pinatubo with planes. But Pinatubo also showed the catch. The haze settled out within about 2 to 3 years, and temperatures bounced back. A man-made version would have to be topped up indefinitely. If it stopped suddenly, the warming it had been hiding would come back within a few years ("termination shock"). One 2018 study in *Nature* (Proctor et al.) also used Pinatubo-era data to estimate that the dimmer sunlight reduced crop yields about as much as the cooling helped them. I'd treat that as one study, not settled science.

1) **Deeper:** how sulfur actually cools, and why ash barely matters (most of it falls out within days)

3) **Wildcard:** why the 1991 evacuation of Clark Air Base is studied as a near-perfect warning call, with a typhoon hitting on the same day
