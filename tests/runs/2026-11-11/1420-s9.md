**Meanwhile:**
**Your belfry worked out a standard computer science algorithm about 300 years before computer scientists did.**

The method is "plain changes." It was published in *Tintinnalogia* in 1668 by Richard Duckworth, with Fabian Stedman, and it rings every ordering of n bells exactly once. Between one row and the next, only one pair of neighbouring bells swaps places. In 1962–63, H. F. Trotter and Selmer Johnson each published the same procedure as a way for computers to list every permutation. It's now called the Steinhaus–Johnson–Trotter algorithm. Knuth's *The Art of Computer Programming* (Vol. 4A) credits the English ringers as the first to find it.

**The twist:** the ringers didn't get there by being clever about maths. They got there because of physics. A bell weighing a ton or more, swinging full circle, can only move one place per change. A bell can't jump two places, so the algorithm had to work within that rule, and the rule is what produced it. Computer scientists later found the same idea useful for a different reason. When each step changes as little as possible, each step costs very little to compute. The general lesson: a hard physical limit often produces a better design than freedom would have.

1) **Deeper**: why plain changes only work as a single unbroken run on small numbers of bells, and the group theory that explains where it stops working
2) **Sideways**: why backfilling 3 years of history into TimescaleDB is a different kind of problem from live inserts, and what chunk ordering does to it
3) **Wildcard**: Grey codes, the "change one bit at a time" counting system that keeps mechanical sensors from reporting garbage values

**To ring every possible change on 12 bells, you'd ring non-stop for about 41 years.**
