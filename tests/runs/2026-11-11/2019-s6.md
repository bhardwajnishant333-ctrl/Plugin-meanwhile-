**Meanwhile:**
If you sum every uphill step in a GPX track, the total climb depends more on your code than on the hill.

**How it happens:** GPS is much worse at height than at position. Vertical error is usually about 1.5 to 3 times the horizontal error, so a bike computer without a barometer can show elevation wobbling by several meters on flat road. A naive parser adds up every positive change between points. On noise, half the steps go up, so a pancake-flat hour can log hundreds of meters of fake climbing. It gets worse as you record more often, because more points means more wobbles to add up. That's the same thing that happens when you measure a coastline with a shorter ruler. Strava handles this by replacing the recorded elevation with elevation from a terrain map for devices that have no barometer. Garmin units with barometers mostly avoid the problem in the first place.

**The twist:** "Total ascent" isn't really a fact about your ride. It's a fact about your sample rate plus your algorithm. That matters right now, because your agent is rewriting the parser. The usual fix is a hysteresis threshold: only count a climb once you've gone up more than about 3 to 5 m from the last low point. That works in a single pass with constant memory, so it fits the streaming token loop. Smoothing that looks ahead needs a small ring buffer, which the streaming version won't have unless someone adds it. If your old code smoothed over the whole array, the streaming version can quietly change every climbing number you've ever recorded.

The "San Francisco" sourdough bacterium isn't from San Francisco. More precisely, it's from everywhere.

**How it happens:** In 1971, USDA researchers Leo Kline and T.F. Sugihara studied San Francisco starters and named the main bacterium *Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*. It's now called *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*. The name made it sound local, and bakeries still sell that idea. Then the same species kept turning up in German, Italian and Chinese starters. In 2021 a citizen-science study (Landis et al., in the journal eLife) sequenced about 500 starters from around the world. Geography explained very little about which microbes lived in them. A starter from Belgium and one from Arizona can be close to identical.

**The twist:** What shapes a starter is how it's fed: the flour, the water ratio, the temperature, and how often you feed it. Over a few weeks those conditions pick which microbes survive. So the culture comes from the process, not the place. Moving a famous starter to your kitchen and feeding it your way slowly turns it into your starter. Same rule for codebases: whatever conditions you keep applying decide what lasts, whatever the code was "born" as.

Switching it up: history this time, and a story that rhymes with today.

**On 18 November 1883, America had a "day of two noons". Railroads did that, not the government, and Congress didn't make it law until 35 years later.**

**What happened:** Before then, every town set its clocks by the sun. US railroads were juggling around 50 different "railroad times", and timetables were a mess. A railway official named William F. Allen got the companies to agree on four standard zones. At noon on the switchover day, clocks in many cities were set back a few minutes, so those cities saw noon twice. Most towns just went along with it because the trains ran on it. Federal law only caught up with the Standard Time Act of 1918.

**Today's version:** In November 2022, the international body that governs measurement standards (the CGPM) voted to stop adding leap seconds to UTC by 2035. Big tech companies pushed hard for it. Google and Meta had already been "smearing" leap seconds across a whole day to avoid crashes, because an extra second on the clock broke real systems.

**The twist:** Time standards get set by whoever has the most clocks to keep in sync: railroads then, data centers now. Governments arrive afterwards and make it official. Your GPX timestamps are in UTC because of that same chain of events.

**Meanwhile:**
**The fix that saved half the internet on 1 July 2012 was a single shell command that set the clock to the time it already said: `date -s "$(date)"`.**

**What happened:** At 23:59:60 UTC on 30 June 2012, a leap second was added. The Linux kernel handled the extra second itself, but it skipped a step: it didn't tell the high-resolution timer system that the clock had changed. So the timers' idea of "now" sat one second ahead of the real clock. Any program that waited on a timer for less than a second found its deadline had already passed, so it woke straight up, waited again, and woke again. Java apps and MySQL servers in particular got stuck in that loop at 100% CPU. Reddit, Mozilla, LinkedIn, Gawker and Yelp all had trouble, and the Amadeus booking system that Qantas uses went down, leaving check-in desks working by hand. Setting the clock to its own time forced the kernel to run the notification step it had skipped, and everything recovered without a reboot.

Google wasn't hit. In a 2011 blog post it had described its "leap smear": instead of inserting the second all at once, its servers ran very slightly slow over many hours so the extra second never existed as a moment.

**The twist:** The bug wasn't hard. It sat in code that only ran once every few years, so nothing in production had ever really tested it. That's the "rare path" problem: the most dangerous code is code that's correct in theory but has hardly ever been run.

1) **Deeper:** why the leap-smear approach means Google's clocks and true UTC disagree by up to half a second on purpose
