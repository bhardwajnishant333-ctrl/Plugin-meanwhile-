**Meanwhile:**
**Troitzky worked out one of the most famous wins in endgame theory, and for most of chess history the rulebook has declared it a draw.**

Two knights normally can't force mate against a bare king. Give the defender a pawn, though, and the extra material is what makes it a win. The pawn removes stalemate as a defence, because the defender always has a pawn move available. Troitzky's research in the early 1900s showed the win works if a knight blockades that pawn far enough back, behind what is now called the Troitzky line. The catch is the length. Some of these wins take well over 50 moves, and the 50-move rule ends the game in a draw if 50 moves pass without a capture or a pawn move.

In the 1980s FIDE tried to fix this. It extended the limit, in some versions to 100 moves, for a list of known long endgames, and two knights against a pawn was on it. Then computer endgame databases kept finding more endgames that needed more moves. In 1992 FIDE dropped every exception and went back to a flat 50 moves. As far as I know, that rule still stands.

**The twist:** FIDE didn't drop the exceptions because they were wrong. It dropped them because they never stopped growing. Since 2012, complete 7-piece databases have found forced mates more than 500 moves long. No exception list could keep up with that, so FIDE chose a rule an arbiter can count over a rule that's always true. Your migration makes the same trade. Tailwind gives up some of what styled-components let you express, and in return you get fixed limits that anyone can check just by reading the class names.

1. **Deeper:** how databases "solve" endgames by working backwards from checkmate
2. **Sideways:** why styled-components stopped fitting React. Its maintainers put it in maintenance mode in 2025, and the reasons are about how React renders.
3. **Wildcard:** Troitzky died in the siege of Leningrad in 1942, and much of his unpublished work was lost with him. What survived, and how.

**Meanwhile:**
**styled-components didn't break. React changed how it renders, and styled-components does its work at exactly the point React now wants left alone.**

styled-components does its work while your app is running, every time a component renders. It reads the template plus your props, hashes them into a class name, and writes a new CSS rule into a `<style>` tag on the page. Each new rule can make the browser recalculate styles. That used to be a small cost.

Then came React 18's concurrent rendering, which can pause, restart and discard renders. In the React 18 working group, the React team warned that adding styles during render can force the browser to recalculate styles over and over, possibly on every frame. They added a hook, `useInsertionEffect`, to limit the damage. Their advice for the long run was to generate CSS at build time instead. Server Components hit it harder. styled-components relies on React context, including `ThemeProvider`, which Server Components can't use, so every styled component has to run in the browser.

In 2022, one of Emotion's own maintainers (Sam Magura) wrote a widely read post about leaving runtime CSS-in-JS. If I remember the numbers right, his team's component rendered in roughly half the time once they switched to static CSS. In early 2025, styled-components' maintainer put the library into maintenance mode and pointed to these same React changes.

**The twist:** you're not trading one styling syntax for another. You're moving styling out of the render loop altogether. Tailwind produces a plain CSS file at build time, so a render does nothing more than switch class names. One check for the diff: any styled-component that changed its CSS based on props (`${p => p.active && ...}`) needs a new home. That's usually conditional classes, `data-*` variants, or CSS variables. That's where a mechanical port most often breaks, so look there for silent visual bugs.

1. **Deeper:** how Tailwind decides which classes to ship (and why building class names from string pieces quietly breaks it)
2. **Sideways:** "zero-runtime" CSS-in-JS like vanilla-extract and Panda, the middle path you didn't take
3. **Wildcard:** back to the board. What survived of Troitzky's lost work after the siege of Leningrad.

**Meanwhile:**
**Troitzky's two-knights theory survived the siege because it was already in print. Most of his unpublished work did not survive.**

Alexey Troitzky (1866–1942) was a forester by trade and composed endgame studies on the side, hundreds of them. He worked out the two-knights-vs-pawn theory by hand over decades. The full version came out in his 1934 Russian collection, as a long supplement on that endgame, and was translated into English a few years later. He stayed in Leningrad during the siege and died there in 1942, reportedly of starvation. His manuscripts and unpublished studies are generally described as lost with him. I'm confident he died in the siege. I'm less sure exactly what was in the lost papers.

Decades later, Ken Thompson's endgame databases in the 1980s solved two knights against a pawn by brute force, with no human ideas involved. As I understand it, the database broadly confirmed Troitzky's analysis. It found some errors and some wins longer than he'd realised (the same long wins that ran into the 50-move rule), but his line held. Someone working with a board and a pencil had mapped most of an endgame that later took a computer to settle.

**The twist:** the "lost to history" part of this story isn't really about talent. It's about where the work lived. What he published got checked, translated and built on. What stayed in his drawer is gone. For your codebase, the reasoning behind the styling choice is part of the work. "Why we left styled-components" is worth one short note in the PR or README before it lives only in your head.
