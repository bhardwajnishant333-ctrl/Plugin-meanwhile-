**A bell ringer proved in 1886 that a problem was impossible, after ringers had spent more than a century trying to solve it.**

Grandsire Triples on 7 bells has 5,040 possible rows. Ringing all of them exactly once is the extent. People wanted to do it using only plain leads and bobs, with no singles. Composers tried this by hand for generations and never managed it. In 1886 William Henry Thompson showed why: it can't be done.

The idea behind his proof:
- If you ring only plain leads, the 5,040 rows split into **72 separate round blocks** of 70 changes each. Each block is one plain course.
- Bobs change how blocks join up, but only in fixed groups of three, called Q-sets. Switching a Q-set either merges 3 blocks into 1 or splits 1 into 3. Either way, the block count goes up or down by **exactly 2**.
- You start with 72 blocks, an even number. Moving in steps of 2 keeps it even. A true extent is **1** block, an odd number, so you can never get there.

That's why every extent of Grandsire Triples needs singles. Holt's famous 1751 peal uses two, if I remember correctly. In 1948 R. A. Rankin restated the argument in group theory and extended it to related methods.

**The twist:** the search space was enormous, but one small invariant made all of that searching unnecessary. A program that tried compositions one after another would never stop. Thompson's argument settles the question in four lines. When a search keeps failing, look for a quantity your moves can't change before you buy more compute.

**The bells that survived were the ones someone had labelled before they were taken.**

From about 1940, the Nazi state took church bells to get their copper and tin. Each bell was given a grade before removal:
- **A:** newer and of little historic value, melted first
- **B and C:** older, held back for later
- **D:** the most historically important, left in the tower

The bells were sent to collecting yards. The biggest was the Hamburg docks, near the copper smelters, and it became known as the *Glockenfriedhof*, the "bell cemetery." Bells were also taken from occupied countries. I've seen the total put in the tens of thousands, maybe as high as about 80,000 or more, but don't treat that as exact.

When the war ended, thousands of bells were still sitting in Hamburg because the smelters hadn't reached them. Many were B and C bells. I don't have a reliable count. A committee was set up to send them home in the late 1940s. They could do this because each bell carried an ID: a painted code for its region, parish and grade, along with the founder's inscription cast into the metal.

Bells from former German lands in the east, now in Poland and elsewhere, often couldn't go back to where they came from. They were lent to churches in West Germany as *Leihglocken*, "loan bells," and some still ring there. A few have been returned to their original churches in recent decades.

**The twist:** the bells weren't saved by how much bronze they held. They were saved because they had a record of where they came from that survived being moved and stored.
