**Every web browser ships a built-in drag-and-drop API, and the library you're installing exists so you don't have to use it.**

Microsoft added drag-and-drop to Internet Explorer 5 around 1999. When HTML5 was being written, the spec authors didn't design a new API. They documented IE's existing behavior so that other browsers could copy it. In 2009 Peter-Paul Koch (quirksmode.org) wrote a post called "The HTML5 drag and drop disaster" about the result. A few of the problems he and others pointed out:
- You have to cancel `dragover` before a drop is allowed at all.
- `dataTransfer` data can't be read during the drag, only on drop.
- Touch support on phones was missing or unreliable for years.

dnd-kit skips all of that. It works from basic pointer, mouse, touch and keyboard events through what it calls "sensors," and it calculates collisions itself in JavaScript. The browser only reports where the pointer is, and dnd-kit decides what counts as a drag and a drop.

**The twist:** because dnd-kit handles input through sensors, a keyboard is just another input source. You can move a hold with the arrow keys, and dnd-kit can announce each move to screen readers. You get that almost for free, but only if your agent registers the `KeyboardSensor` next to the `PointerSensor`. Agents often leave it out. When the run finishes, search the diff for `useSensors`.

**People call the Caro-Kann the boring, careful defense. Its best-known win came against the most aggressive attacker who ever held the world title.**

**Origins.** Horatio Caro, an English player based in Berlin, and Marcus Kann, from Vienna, are the two names behind it. Kann played it against Jacques Mieses in Hamburg in 1885 and won in about 17 moves, according to the usual account. Caro published analysis of it soon after, and the opening has carried both names ever since.

**The rematch.** In 1960 Mikhail Tal took the world title from Mikhail Botvinnik by attacking constantly and making the position too complicated to calculate. A year later Botvinnik, nearly 50, got his rematch. As I remember it, the Caro-Kann was his main answer to Tal's 1.e4. He won clearly and took the title back.

**The twist.** The Caro-Kann doesn't avoid a fight. Black develops the light-squared bishop before locking in the pawns, so the classic attacking targets are hard to find. Against Tal, Botvinnik didn't try to calculate better. He chose positions where there was little to calculate. A player who attacks all the time is only as dangerous as the material you give him to work with.

**In the 1997 Deep Blue match, Kasparov resigned a game he could have drawn, partly because the machine played too much like a human.**

**Game 2, May 1997.** Around move 36, Deep Blue passed up a tempting pawn grab with its queen and played a quiet positional move instead (36.axb5). Computers of that era were famous for taking material, so Kasparov suspected a human grandmaster was helping. He resigned a few moves later. Analysts then found that he had a perpetual check, so the final position was a draw. He played the rest of the match visibly rattled, lost game 6 badly, and lost the match 3.5–2.5. IBM refused to release the logs he asked for, and it retired Deep Blue soon afterward.

**The other half.** In *The Signal and the Noise* (2012), Nate Silver reported a claim from Deep Blue engineer Murray Campbell: late in game 1, a bug made the machine play an essentially random move. Kasparov reportedly read it as a sign of calculation deeper than his own. I'm reporting Campbell's account through Silver, and the details are disputed. If it's right, though, one "superhuman" move was a glitch and the other was ordinary search, and Kasparov read a mind into both.

**The twist:** Kasparov didn't lose to how strong Deep Blue was. He lost to the intent he imagined behind its moves.
