**The "px" in your Figma file isn't a pixel. It's an angle.**

The CSS spec, the rulebook browsers follow, defines a "reference pixel" by how big it looks to your eye, not by any physical size. Their example is one dot on a 96-dots-per-inch screen, held at arm's length (28 inches). From there it covers about 0.0213 degrees of your vision. The 96 came from the old Windows default. Macs used 72, which is part of why early web design was such a mess.

So the browser on each device does a conversion for you:

- On a recent iPhone, one CSS pixel is drawn with a 3×3 block of real screen dots.
- On a TV across the room, the spec lets a "pixel" be physically much bigger, because you sit further away.
- A CSS "inch" (always 96px) usually isn't a real inch on any screen.

**The twist:** a 16px heading in your mockup has no fixed size in the physical world. What you're really setting is how big it looks to a person at a normal distance. The studio's site will be a different physical object on a phone, a laptop and a TV in the shop window, yet it should look about the same to each viewer. Without realising it, you've been designing for eyes, not for glass.

**Meanwhile:**
Nearly everyone assumes that, and it's the reason cones are so clever: **they don't measure temperature at all.**

A cone is a small pyramid made from a carefully mixed recipe of clay, flux and silica. As the kiln heats, glass slowly forms inside it until it softens and bends. That bending depends on how hot it got *and* how long it stayed hot. Potters call that combination "heatwork."

Some numbers from Orton's published chart, so they're approximate:
- **Cone 10** goes down at about **1285°C** if the kiln rises 60°C an hour at the end, and about **1305°C** if it rises 150°C an hour.
- **Cone 6** goes down at about **1222°C** slow and **1243°C** fast.

So the same cone can bend at a lower peak temperature if you fire more slowly. Hermann Seger invented them in Germany in the 1880s. Edward Orton Jr. began making them in the US in the 1890s, and his foundation still makes the ones you probably use.

**The twist:** your clay and glazes also respond to heatwork, not to a number on a dial. A digital thermocouple tells you the temperature at one spot. The cone shows you what your pots actually went through. That's why potters still stand cones on the shelf even in computer-controlled kilns, and why holding the kiln at its top temperature for a while (a "soak") can mature a glaze without firing hotter. It's the slow roast versus the hot oven.

1. **Deeper**: why a cone "standing at 6" and one "bent to 4 o'clock" tell you different things, and how to read a cone pack

**Meanwhile:**
**In the 1760s and 1770s, a potter from Staffordshire was running what we'd now call influencer marketing.**

- **A royal endorsement (1765).** Wedgwood made a tea set for Queen Charlotte, then got her permission to call his everyday cream-coloured earthenware "Queen's Ware." It was the same pottery, sold to the middle class with a royal name on it.
- **A launch event (1774).** Catherine the Great ordered the "Frog Service," about 950 pieces painted with English country houses. Before shipping it to Russia, he put it on show in his London showroom, and society queued up to see it. The commission was nearly a loss; the publicity was the real payoff.
- **The product, jasperware (mid-1770s).** It's an unglazed stoneware coloured all the way through, with white relief figures in the Greek style that was in fashion. His notebooks record thousands of test firings to get it right. I've seen "over 5,000" quoted but haven't checked that number myself.
- **A cause (1787).** He made the jasper medallion "Am I Not a Man and a Brother?", a kneeling enslaved man, and gave it away to the anti-slavery movement. People wore it as jewellery. It's one of the first mass-produced political badges.
- **A link to your last question.** He built a clay heat gauge and was elected to the Royal Society for it in 1783. His temperature numbers at the hot end were wildly too high, but the gauge was still useful for comparing one firing with the next.

**The twist:** historians (Neil McKendrick in particular) argue he didn't just sell pottery, he helped invent the consumer: someone who buys a thing because of what it says about them. He split his range into "useful" and "ornamental" wares, priced the showpieces high to set the aspiration, and let the prestige flow down to the cheaper lines. Jasperware blue is basically a brand colour that's lasted 250 years.

(Bonus: his grandson was Charles Darwin, who married a Wedgwood cousin.)
