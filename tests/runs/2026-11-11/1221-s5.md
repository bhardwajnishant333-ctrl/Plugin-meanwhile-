**A cart with no motor has driven straight downwind faster than the wind pushing it.**

In July 2010 Rick Cavallaro's cart *Blackbird* did it on a dry lake bed in California, at about 2.8 times the wind speed. The North American Land Sailing Association officially ratified the run. The cart has a large propeller, and its wheels drive that propeller. The wheels are not driven by it. As the cart rolls, the ground turns the wheels, the wheels spin the propeller, and the propeller pushes air backward. The air it pushes against is already moving forward with the wind. So the propeller takes in less power than the wheels can supply, and the leftover drives the cart forward.

**The twist:** at exactly wind speed, you'd feel dead calm on the cart, with zero apparent wind, and it keeps speeding up anyway. It isn't running on apparent wind at all. Its power comes from the difference in speed between the air and the ground. Your Laser has only one medium to work with, so it can't do this dead downwind. That's why you gybe downwind angles instead. In 2021 a UCLA physicist bet Veritasium's Derek Muller $10,000 that it was impossible, and he paid up.

**Loading a save file with BinaryFormatter can run code that whoever made the file chose.**

`BinaryFormatter.Deserialize` doesn't just fill in your fields. The stream names the .NET types to build, and the formatter creates whatever serializable type it's told to, then runs those types' deserialization hooks. People have found "gadget chains": standard framework types that, wired together in a crafted file, end in something like `Process.Start`. In 2017 Alvaro Muñoz released ysoserial.net, which makes these payloads in one command. Microsoft's own docs say BinaryFormatter "is insecure and can't be made secure." It was marked obsolete in .NET 5, and in .NET 9 the methods simply throw. Unity's runtime still ships a working one. So in a roguelike where players swap saves or download modded runs, a save file is effectively a program you agreed to run.

**The twist:** moving to JSON doesn't fix this on its own. Newtonsoft Json.NET with `TypeNameHandling` set to anything other than `None` reads a `$type` field from the file, which is the same bug in a new format. Roguelikes tend to want exactly that setting for polymorphic items and relics. If Claude's rewrite uses it, ask for a `SerializationBinder` that only allows your own types. Also look at how old `.dat` saves get migrated. Code that still deserializes them with BinaryFormatter keeps the hole open.

**The death roll is mostly something you do to the boat yourself, in a feedback loop.**

You're running downwind with the vang eased. The top of the sail twists forward past square, so the upper leech starts pushing the rig to windward while the lower sail pushes to leeward. That sets up a roll. The boat heels to windward and the hull's shape steers it away from the wind, toward by-the-lee. That loads the twisted top section even more, so the roll gets bigger. If each swing lines up with the boat's natural roll period, the amplitude grows until the boom digs in and you go in to windward, which is the surprising direction. (Some explanations add alternating vortex shedding off the sail as the driver of the rhythm. I'm less sure how much that contributes than I am about the twist and feedback part.)

**The twist:** how you fight it is often the fuel. Hiking and steering hard in step with each roll pumps energy into the oscillation. Engineers call this pilot-induced oscillation: the controller's own correction comes late enough to land in phase with the motion. The real fix is changing the system rather than reacting faster. Pull on more vang to kill the twist, sheet in a bit, and keep the hull steered under the rig.

**The YF-22 crash came from the airplane's control software switching modes while the pilot was correcting.**

On 25 April 1992 test pilot Tom Morgenfeld was making a low pass over the runway at Edwards in the YF-22 prototype. He lit the afterburners and raised the gear to go around. Retracting the gear switched the flight-control system to a different set of control laws with different gains. A few feet off the ground he began over-controlling in pitch. The tail surfaces hit their maximum rate of movement, so they could no longer keep up with his stick inputs, and each surface movement came later than the one before. The oscillation grew, the jet hit the runway, slid and caught fire. Morgenfeld survived. Investigators blamed pilot-induced oscillation driven by rate limiting.

**The twist:** rate limiting turns a lag you can predict into one that grows with how hard you push. The harder the pilot corrects, the further the surfaces fall behind, and the more each correction lands in phase with the motion instead of against it. The JAS 39 Gripen lost a prototype in 1989 and a production jet over a Stockholm air show crowd in 1993 the same way. What fixed it was redesigning the control laws to handle saturation, not training pilots better. Your death roll works the same way: a sail you can't get to stop twisting is an actuator stuck at its limit.
