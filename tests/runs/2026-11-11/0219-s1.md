**A honeybee can keep dancing directions for hours after she's back in the hive, and she corrects them for where the sun has moved while she's been in there.**

**How it works:** In the waggle dance, the angle of her run away from straight up on the comb is the angle between the food and the sun. The length of the waggle stands for distance. Karl von Frisch decoded this and shared the 1973 Nobel Prize for it. He also saw something stranger. Some bees kept dancing for hours inside the dark hive, and their angle slowly turned to match the sun moving across the sky. They couldn't see the sun at all. They were working out where it should be from an internal clock.

Distance turned out to be odd too. Bees don't measure distance by effort. They measure how much scenery streams past their eyes. In a 2000 study in *Science* (Srinivasan and colleagues), bees flew through a short, narrow tunnel lined with patterns. Afterwards they danced as if the trip had been far longer. The paper says "tens of times", but I don't remember the exact number.

**The twist:** That dance is a scheduled job. "Go 40° right of the sun" only makes sense if you fix it to a reference point that moves. The bee doesn't save the old angle; she works it out again when she needs it. That's the same choice your appointment reminders face. If you work out "UTC time to send" when the booking is made, a reminder booked before a daylight-saving change can fire an hour off. If you store the local time plus the time zone and work it out later, you don't have that problem. I can't see your agent's code, so treat this as something worth checking, not a bug I've found.

**Myth-bust: `unique:` in Oban doesn't mean a job runs only once. It means the same job can't be inserted twice. Those are different promises.**

**How it works:** Oban keeps its queue in Postgres. A node claims a job with `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` and marks it `executing`, so two nodes can't grab the same row. If the node dies mid-job, from an OOM kill, a deploy, or a hard restart past the `shutdown_grace_period`, the row just stays `executing`. Nothing in the database knows the process is gone.

The Lifeline plugin picks up these orphans. After a timeout it moves them back to `available`. I believe the default `rescue_after` is 60 minutes, and Lifeline only runs if you list it in `plugins:`, so check your config. Oban Pro's DynamicLifeline can tell when a node has died and rescues sooner. In both cases the job runs again from the top. That's at-least-once delivery.

**Where it breaks for you:** Picture this run:

1. `perform/1` sends the SMS or email.
2. The node dies before Oban marks the job `completed`.
3. Lifeline rescues it and the job runs again.
4. The co-op member gets the reminder twice.

No database transaction can prevent this, because the message goes out to a provider outside Postgres. You can only pick which way it fails:

- **Mark first, then send.** Write `reminder_sent_at` and commit before sending. A crash means a missed reminder (at-most-once).
- **Send first, then mark.** A crash means a duplicate reminder (at-least-once).

For bike-repair appointments, a duplicate is probably more annoying than a missed one, but that's for your co-op to decide.

**The bigger risk:** rescheduling. A reminder job inserted with `scheduled_at` still fires after the appointment moves or gets cancelled, unless something stops it. The safe pattern: put only `appointment_id` in the args. Inside `perform`, reload the appointment, and if it's cancelled, already reminded, or the time has changed, return `{:cancel, reason}`. Calling `Oban.cancel_job` when someone reschedules is a nice extra, but the check inside `perform` is what you can rely on.

**Worth checking when the agent finishes:** does `perform` reload the appointment, or does it trust a time copied into the args?
