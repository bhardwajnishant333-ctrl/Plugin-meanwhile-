The price of a variance swap is set largely by options far from the money.

The math comes from Neuberger (1994) and the Goldman note by Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal and Zou (1999). If you delta-hedge a contract that pays the log of the final price, you collect realized variance. You can build that log payoff from a static strip of out-of-the-money puts and calls, with each strike K weighted by 1/K². That weighting tilts the strip toward low strikes, so deep OTM puts carry a lot of the price. On an index with skew, those puts are bid, which is why the var swap strike usually sits above ATM implied vol. Since 2003, VIX has been this same formula applied to SPX options.

The twist: the formula assumes a strip that runs all the way down to zero, and real markets stop listing strikes well before that. A dealer who sells variance is therefore short a crash tail they can't fully hedge. 2008 showed what that costs. After that, var swaps were commonly capped, often at 2.5 times the strike, and single-stock variance largely dried up. The textbook "model-free" price is model-free only as far as the listed strikes go.

The best-known evidence that QWERTY beat a better keyboard came from a study that Dvorak himself had a hand in.

Paul David's 1985 AER paper "Clio and the Economics of QWERTY" made the keyboard the textbook case of lock-in, where an inferior standard wins and can't be dislodged. The key evidence was a 1944 US Navy study claiming typists retrained on the Dvorak layout recovered the retraining cost within about 10 days. In 1990, Liebowitz and Margolis published "The Fable of the Keys." They found the Navy study was run under August Dvorak's own supervision, the Dvorak patent holder, and that its comparison groups weren't matched. A 1956 study for the US General Services Administration, run by Earle Strong, retrained typists on both layouts and found Dvorak didn't come out ahead. Typing contests in the 1880s and 1890s, before QWERTY was entrenched, also don't show it losing to clearly faster layouts.

The twist: lock-in is a real mechanism, but this famous example of it leans on an unproven premise, that the alternative was actually better. The claim that "the inferior one won" was taken on trust for decades. The question to ask is who ran the benchmark. That applies to your eval set as well.

**1. Make sure both numbers were decoded the same way.** Re-run base whisper-small with exactly the fine-tuned model's generation settings: forced `language="hi"`, `task="transcribe"`, the same beam size, the same normalizer. If the base model was free to pick its own language, some of its output may have come out translated or in Urdu script. That alone can inflate 31.2.

**2. Split the error into substitutions, deletions and insertions.**
```python
import jiwer
o = jiwer.process_words(refs, hyps)
print(o.wer, o.substitutions, o.deletions, o.insertions, o.hits)
```
Run it for both models. If most of the gain is fewer insertions, the fine-tune mostly stopped hallucinating on hold music and silence. That's a real improvement, but a narrow one, and it says little about recognition.

**3. Score with both sides in one script.** Transliterate the references and the hypotheses into a single script (AI4Bharat's IndicXlit can map Latin-script English into Devanagari), then recompute WER for both models. If the gap shrinks a lot, much of the 12.6 points came from learning which script your annotators use.

**4. Check the normalizer.** If `BasicTextNormalizer` is in the scoring path, compute WER with and without it. It may be splitting Devanagari words at the vowel signs, which changes word counts differently for the two models.

**5. Look for leakage.** This is the usual suspect with call-centre data.
- Was the split made by call or by speaker, or by segment? Segments from the same call, or the same agent, on both sides of the split is leakage.
- Agents read from scripts. Count how many held-out references appear word for word, or nearly so, in the training transcripts, and compute WER separately for that group and for the rest. If the gain sits in the overlap group, the model memorized the scripts.

**6. Break WER down by bucket.** Report it separately for Hindi-heavy, English-heavy and mixed utterances, and for agent vs customer speech, and add CER. A real acoustic gain shows up fairly evenly. A gain that only shows up on agent speech or one script is a convention or memorization effect.

**7. Listen to 20 random utterances** where the fine-tuned model improved most. Ten minutes of listening usually settles it.

Check 5 is the one most likely to change your conclusion. If the split wasn't made by call, fix that first and re-score before trusting any of the other numbers.
