The green glow in your OIII frames is light that atoms on Earth almost never get to emit.

In 1864, William Huggins pointed a spectroscope at the Cat's Eye Nebula and saw a bright green line near 500.7 nm that matched no known element. For about 60 years, astronomers credited it to a new element they called "nebulium." In 1927, Ira Bowen showed it was ordinary oxygen with two electrons stripped off (O III), making a "forbidden" transition. That's quantum-mechanically legal, just wildly improbable. A normal transition fires in roughly nanoseconds. This one takes on the order of seconds or more (I'm not certain of the exact lifetime).

In any gas you can make on Earth, the atom gets bumped by a neighbour long before it has the chance, and the energy leaves without making that green light. A nebula has maybe a few hundred to a few thousand atoms per cm³. Air has about 2.5×10¹⁹. Out there, nothing interrupts the atom, so the unlikely event happens on a huge scale.

The twist: your OIII filter is tuned to a line that only exists because the nebula is emptier than nearly any vacuum we can build. "Forbidden" only meant "too slow to happen here," and the brightest colour in those nebulae comes from the rare event that had time to happen.

If 1 call in 100 is slow, a request that waits on 100 calls is slow 63% of the time.

The math: 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.63. Jeff Dean and Luiz Barroso of Google made this the centre of "The Tail at Scale" (Communications of the ACM, 2013). When one request fans out to many servers, the user waits for the slowest one, so a rare hiccup on one server becomes the usual case for the whole request. Averages hide this. Only the 99th percentile shows it.

Their fix sounds wasteful. If a reply hasn't come back by about the 95th-percentile wait, send the same request to a second replica and take whichever answers first. In one benchmark they report, this cut 99.9th-percentile latency from about 1.8 seconds to about 74 ms for roughly 2% more requests (that's from memory, so check the paper before quoting it).

The twist: past a certain size, duplicating work on purpose makes a system faster, because a request is only as fast as its slowest dependency. A booking request that touches availability, pricing, payment and notifications is already a small fan-out. While Claude is porting it, the path to watch is the one that waits on all four.

The "38 witnesses who did nothing" story behind the bystander effect is mostly wrong.

In March 1964, Kitty Genovese was murdered in Queens. Two weeks later, The New York Times reported that 38 neighbours watched and none called the police. That story led to John Darley and Bibb Latané's lab work on the bystander effect (1968), and it's still in every psych textbook. Later digging, including a 2007 paper in *American Psychologist* by Rachel Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins, found that few people actually saw the attack, that at least one person shouted at the attacker, and that a neighbour went to help her. In 2016 the Times itself said the original report had overstated the case.

The twist: when Richard Philpot and colleagues looked at CCTV of real public fights in Amsterdam, Lancaster and Cape Town (published 2019–2020), at least one bystander stepped in about 9 times out of 10. More people around made it more likely that someone helped. The lab finding that each person is less likely to act may be real. But crowds as a whole don't stand there and watch. The famous version became textbook fact on the strength of one newspaper story.

Two of psychology's most famous findings may have been partly made up by the people in them, without anyone lying.

**Stanford Prison Experiment (1971).** Philip Zimbardo's study made "good people turn cruel in a bad role" a cultural fact. Then the archive tapes came out. In 2018 the French researcher Thibault Le Texier, and journalist Ben Blum in "The Lifespan of a Lie," showed that guards were briefed on how to act, and that one recording has the student "warden," David Jaffe, pushing a reluctant guard to be tougher. Some participants later said they were performing what they thought the study wanted. When Alex Haslam and Stephen Reicher re-ran a version for the BBC in 2002, the guards didn't turn into tyrants. The group fell apart and then reorganised.

**Ego depletion (1998).** Roy Baumeister's team found that people who had to resist warm chocolate cookies and eat radishes instead gave up sooner on an unsolvable puzzle. Willpower became a fuel tank. In 2016, a pre-registered replication across 23 labs with about 2,000 participants (led by Martin Hagger) found an effect close to zero. A later test co-designed with Baumeister also came out very small (I don't remember the exact size).

The twist: in ego depletion, the belief may have been doing the work. Veronika Job, Carol Dweck and Greg Walton (2010) found that depletion showed up mainly in people who believed willpower runs out. If you think you're a battery, you act like one. Both studies may have measured what people expected, and then the textbooks taught everyone to expect it.
