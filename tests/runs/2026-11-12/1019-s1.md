**For years, US radio ratings came from a sound nobody could hear, and some music was harder to "hear" than others.**

Arbitron's Portable People Meter (rolled out commercially around 2007, and owned by Nielsen since 2013) is a pager-sized device that panelists carry all day. Stations broadcast with an inaudible code mixed into their audio. The meter picks up that code from any nearby speaker and logs the station and the minute. To stay inaudible, the code hides under the music using psychoacoustic masking: it gets tucked into parts of the sound where louder content covers it up.

The catch is that a code that needs loud sound to hide behind gets weaker when the audio is quiet or sparse. Classical music, jazz and talk radio with pauses gave the meter fewer chances to detect it, so listening to those formats could go undercounted. In 2014 a box called Voltair (from 25-Seven Systems) appeared. It boosted how detectable the codes were, and stations that used it reported jumps in their ratings. Nielsen then had to address how reliable its own encoding was.

**Twist:** this was the measurement shaping the result, not a measurement mistake. Once a number depends on a signal the measured party can change, people will change it. Your dashboard has the same weak spot: a stream "listener" is usually a connection, not a person.

**NES Tetris remembers exactly one piece. That one piece of memory explains both why it feels fair and why I-piece droughts can kill you.**

Here's how it works. The game runs a 16-bit pseudorandom generator (a linear-feedback shift register; I'm confident about the type but not about the exact bit taps). For each spawn, it adds the spawn counter to an RNG byte and keeps the low 3 bits, which gives a number from 0 to 7. There are only 7 pieces, so 7 means nothing. If it rolls 7, or rolls the same piece you just got, the game rerolls once using a modulo-7 roll, and it keeps whatever comes out, even if that's a repeat.

The math, assuming the rolls are evenly spread: a first roll forces a reroll 2 times in 8. The reroll repeats the last piece 1 time in 7. So a back-to-back repeat happens about **1 in 28 (around 3.6%)**, compared with 1 in 7 (about 14%) for a truly fair die. Every other piece comes up about 16% of the time instead of 14.3%.

**Twist:** this anti-repeat rule makes the game feel fairer, but it only remembers one piece, so nothing stops a long gap between I-pieces. Modern Tetris uses a "7-bag": all seven pieces dealt in a shuffled set, which caps the longest possible gap between two I-pieces at 12. NES Tetris has no such cap. A 20-plus piece drought is just bad luck, and much of classic competitive skill (clean stacking, burning lines, knowing when to give up on a Tetris) is a way of coping with that missing cap.

1) **Deeper:** why the spawn counter makes some sequences more likely than others, and whether players can actually use that
2) **Sideways:** the 7-bag idea applied to your dashboard, i.e. "fair" sampling vs. random sampling of listener data

**Rigging a slot machine to show you a near miss is illegal in Nevada. Building the machine so near misses happen far more often is legal.**

The trick is the "virtual reel," patented in 1984 by Inge Telnaes (US 4,448,419). The physical reel might have 22 stops, but the random number generator chooses from a much larger virtual reel, often 64 positions or more, and each virtual position maps onto a physical stop. The designer decides how many virtual positions point to each physical stop. Give the jackpot symbol one position and the blank right above or below it many positions, and the jackpot shows up just next to the payline over and over, while actually landing on the payline almost never.

The line was drawn in the late 1980s. Universal built machines that first decided a spin was a loss and then picked a "near miss" display to show. Nevada regulators ruled that illegal around 1989. I'm sure of the gist of that case but not of every detail. Their reasoning was that the display must honestly show the random outcome. Virtual-reel weighting passed because every display really is the random result. Only the odds behind it were shaped.

Why it matters: a 2009 study by Clark and colleagues in *Neuron* found that near misses activate reward-related brain areas that overlap with the ones wins activate, and that near misses increase people's urge to keep playing, even though a near miss pays exactly what any other loss pays.

**Twist:** the law doesn't regulate how a spin feels. It regulates whether the screen is literally true. You can design something entirely out of honest outcomes and still steer how people feel about them. Every dashboard designer holds that same power, including over what gets shown close to a goal line.
