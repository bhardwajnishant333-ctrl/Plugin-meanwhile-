**The rubbery disc on top of your kombucha isn't really what ferments the tea. It's a raft the bacteria build.**

The disc (the SCOBY, or "mother") is mostly pure cellulose, the same basic material as cotton and paper. Some of the bacteria in the brew, mainly *Komagataeibacter* (it used to be called *Acetobacter*), turn sugar into very fine cellulose threads and weave them into a mat. **Kimchi doesn't come from one set of bacteria. Two groups take turns, and the first group creates the conditions that end its own turn.**

**The first group, *Leuconostoc*.** It dominates the first few days. It turns the cabbage's sugars into a mix of lactic acid, a little acetic acid and carbon dioxide. The CO2 is the fizz and the bulging lid. The milder acids give the fresh, bright taste many people think of as "perfect" kimchi.

**Then it pushes itself out.** The acid *Leuconostoc* makes lowers the pH, and *Leuconostoc* doesn't cope well with strong acid. As things sour, tougher species take over, mainly *Lactobacillus plantarum* and *Lactobacillus sakei* (both recently renamed by scientists). They produce mostly lactic acid and go on souring. That's where the sharp, fully sour taste comes from.

**Temperature decides how long each group lasts.** Cold favours the first group, so kimchi kept in the fridge stays fresh-tasting much longer. In a warm kitchen the sour group takes over within days. This is the main reason Korean households have kimchi fridges.

**Your weird batches, roughly sorted:**
- **Too sour too fast:** too warm. The second group took over early.
- **Soft or mushy:** usually too little salt or too much heat. Natural enzymes break down the cabbage's structure faster than the acid can protect it.
- **Flat white film on top:** usually kahm yeast, which grows where the kimchi meets air. It's generally harmless but can taste off. Skim it and press the vegetables back under the liquid.
- **Fuzzy, raised or coloured spots (blue, green, black, pink):** that's mould. Throw the batch out.

**The twist:** you aren't really making kimchi. You're setting up a contest between the two groups, and salt, temperature and time decide when it ends. "Ready" means you've picked the moment you like best.

**Meanwhile:**
**To get kimchi into space, scientists had to stop it being alive.**

In April 2008, Yi So-yeon became the first Korean astronaut and flew to the International Space Station. Korea wanted her to take kimchi, but ordinary kimchi had three problems:

- **It keeps fermenting.** You know the bulging lid: the bacteria keep giving off CO2. In a sealed pouch in a sealed spacecraft, a package that swells or bursts is a real hazard, not just a mess.
- **The live bacteria.** Crews had concerns about live microbes on board a closed station. Some reports also mentioned worry about cosmic radiation changing them in unpredictable ways. That second point was a precaution rather than a known risk.
- **The smell.** In a small space with recycled air, a strong smell has nowhere to go.

**The fix:** researchers, led mainly by the Korea Atomic Energy Research Institute, treated the kimchi with radiation (gamma irradiation, also used to sterilise some foods and medical equipment). That kills the bacteria, so fermentation stops, and the flavour stays close to the real thing. Press reports at the time said it took several years and a lot of money. I don't fully trust the exact figures, so I won't quote them.

**The twist:** space kimchi is kimchi frozen at one point in the contest from earlier: the taste is locked in and the bacteria are gone. It's "perfectly ripe" forever because nothing in it is alive to keep changing it. Shelf-stable store kimchi is often pasteurised for the same reason. Your homemade jar keeps changing because it's alive.

**Meanwhile:**
**It isn't radioactive. Food treated with radiation never touches anything radioactive, and the radiation passes through it the way light passes through a window.**

**How it's done:** the radiation usually comes from a sealed source of cobalt-60 kept behind thick shielding. Food goes past the source on a conveyor, takes a measured dose of gamma rays and moves on. The radioactive material stays sealed in the machine and never touches the food.

**Why the food doesn't pick it up:** for something to *become* radioactive, the radiation has to hit the centres of its atoms hard enough to change them. Cobalt-60's gamma rays aren't strong enough to do that. International food rules set energy limits for machine-made radiation (electron beams and X-rays) so it stays below that level too. It's the same reason a dental X-ray or an airport scanner doesn't make you or your suitcase radioactive afterward. A torch doesn't leave a room glowing either.

**What it does do:** it breaks DNA. A bacterium's DNA is long and fragile, so it gets cut in many places and the bacterium can't repair itself or reproduce. The food's other molecules are hit much less. It isn't completely unaffected, though: some vitamins can drop a little and flavour can change slightly, which is part of why the space kimchi needed careful work.

**The twist:** you've very likely already eaten irradiated food. It's widely used on dried herbs and spices, among other things, and the WHO and food-safety agencies in many countries consider it safe. In many places such food has to carry a small symbol called the Radura, a stylised plant inside a circle. Look for it on a spice jar sometime.
