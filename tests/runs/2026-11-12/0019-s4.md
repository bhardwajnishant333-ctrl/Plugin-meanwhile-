**If your solubility model's test error is lower than the error between two labs measuring the same compound, that isn't a win. It's a leak.**

Measured aqueous solubility is noisy. Values for the same compound from different labs commonly differ by about 0.5–0.7 log units. My figure is the usual citation, not a precise constant. Public sets are also stitched together from many sources. AqSolDB (Sorkun et al., 2019) combined nine datasets into about 10,000 unique compounds. Any merged set like that has the same molecule turning up in several forms: as a salt, as a different tautomer, with or without stereochemistry, or with a slightly different SMILES string. Exact-string deduplication misses all of these.

The leak works like this. A random split puts one copy of aspirin in train and a near-copy in test. A GNN with enough capacity memorizes the training copy and predicts the test copy almost perfectly. Your RMSE drops, the model has learned nothing new, and the number looks great.

**The twist:** the noise floor works as a free leak detector. If the test labels carry about 0.6 log units of noise, even a perfect model can't score much below that against them. That noise can't be predicted from the structure. So a score clearly under the floor means the test noise is correlated with something in training. In practice that's usually the same compound from the same source. Random splits on merged sets often land there. Scaffold splits (Bemis–Murcko) usually don't, and the gap between the two scores tells you roughly how much of your accuracy comes from memorization.

**Scaffold splits are known as the honest alternative to random splits. In a common implementation, they also quietly put every acyclic molecule in your training set.**

The Bemis–Murcko scaffold (1996) strips a molecule down to its ring systems and the linkers between them. Molecules with no rings, like ethanol or hexane, all get the same empty scaffold. Solubility sets contain a lot of small acyclic compounds, so that empty scaffold becomes the biggest group. DeepChem's `ScaffoldSplitter` sorts groups largest-first and fills train before test. So all the acyclic molecules land in train, and the test set ends up mostly rare, single-member scaffolds. That makes test harder than real use, and it's also *unrepresentative*: your model is never scored on the easy, common chemistry it will actually see.

There's a second flaw. Scaffold identity is all-or-nothing. Swapping a phenyl ring for a cyclohexyl one gives a "novel" scaffold even though the molecules are nearly the same. Adding a long side chain to benzene keeps the "same" scaffold even though solubility changes a lot. A scaffold split measures distance in a way that doesn't match how solubility behaves.

The alternatives:

- **Cluster split:** fingerprint everything (e.g. Morgan, radius 2), cluster with Butina (1999) at a chosen Tanimoto cutoff, and hold out whole clusters. You pick the cutoff, so you control how far test sits from train.
- **Time split:** train on compounds measured before a date and test on those measured after. Robert Sheridan at Merck (J. Chem. Inf. Model., 2013) found time splits gave error estimates closer to real prospective performance. Random splits were optimistic and cluster-based splits tended to be pessimistic. That's my reading of the paper; worth confirming before you cite it.

**The twist:** no split is right in the abstract. Choose the split that matches the question the model will actually face. Take the molecules your chemists will really submit next quarter. Compute each one's nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity to your training set, then pick the split, or the cluster cutoff, whose test set gives the same similarity spread. If you have internal assay dates, a time split on your own data probably beats every public benchmark option.
