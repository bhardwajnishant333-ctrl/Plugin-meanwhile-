An actor isn't a lock, and Swift 6 won't warn you about the difference.

Swift 6 strict checking proves no two tasks touch an actor's state at the same moment. But actors are reentrant: at every `await`, the actor can start other queued calls before yours resumes. Take `if cache[id] == nil { cache[id] = await fetch(id) }`. Two callers can both see `nil`, both fetch, and the second write replaces the first. The actors proposal (SE-0306, Swift 5.5, 2021) made this choice on purpose. Non-reentrant actors can deadlock, so the design accepted interleaving and left non-reentrant actors as possible future work.

The catch: Claude's migration can compile with zero warnings and still have bugs. Strict concurrency stops data races, but it says nothing about logic races across an `await`. A workout-state check followed by an `await` is exactly where they hide.

A lean runner carries enough fat for hundreds of miles but only enough fast fuel for about 20.

The fast fuel is glycogen, carbohydrate stored in muscle and liver. A trained runner holds roughly 2,000 kcal of it, and running costs about 100 kcal a mile, more or less depending on body weight. At marathon effort the body burns mostly carbohydrate, because fat can't be burned fast enough to hold that pace. So the tank runs dry somewhere around mile 18 to 22. Fat is still plentiful, but it can't supply energy as quickly, and pace drops whether you like it or not. Benjamin Rapoport modeled this in a 2010 paper in PLoS Computational Biology. The model predicted from a runner's VO2max and leg muscle mass where that runner would hit the wall, and how much carbohydrate taken during the race would push it back.

The catch: the wall isn't an empty tank, it's a throughput limit. The system fails over to a backup with enormous capacity but much lower bandwidth. That's the same shape as a cache miss falling through to disk. Planned carb intake during a race works like refilling the cache.

Runners who are supposedly spent still sprint the last 400 metres. Something was holding power back.

That puzzle sits at the center of Tim Noakes's central governor theory, which he built out in the late 1990s and 2000s from an older idea of A.V. Hill's. In his model, the brain keeps reading signals from the body (temperature, fuel, how far is left) and limits how many muscle fibres it will fire, so you stop well before real damage. Fatigue, in this view, is a feeling the brain produces to enforce that limit, not a direct readout of the muscles. Exhausted muscles don't come close to running out of ATP, and the finishing kick is hard to explain if the muscles truly had nothing left. The theory is still disputed: critics say it's hard to falsify. Samuele Marcora's rival model puts the limit in perceived effort instead. In his 2009 study, 90 minutes of a demanding computer task cut people's cycling time to exhaustion by about 15%, even though their hearts and muscles measured the same.

The catch: on either model, how far you can go is partly a prediction, and mental tiredness shifts it. A long debugging session can literally shorten your run.

The famous claim that grandmasters burn 6,000 calories a day is almost certainly wrong. But they really do lose weight.

The 6,000 figure went viral from a 2019 ESPN piece and traces back to heart-rate monitoring at tournaments. The problem is that heart-rate formulas assume a racing heart means moving muscles, and a player under pressure has a racing heart while sitting still. The brain is costly, using roughly 20% of your energy at rest, but hard concentration adds very little on top, only a few percent by most estimates. The weight loss itself is well documented. In the 1984–85 world championship against Kasparov, Anatoly Karpov is reported to have lost about 10 kg over five months, and the match was stopped after 48 games without a result. The likely causes are stress hormones, lost appetite, poor sleep and constant fidgeting, not the thinking itself.

The catch: mental exhaustion isn't the brain running low on fuel. It's the body treating a long contest as a threat. That fits Marcora's finding: a long, hard focus session makes your next run feel harder, even though your legs are no less fuelled.
