A hive can starve with 10 kg of honey still in the box. The cold doesn't kill the bees. It traps them where they are.

**How it works:** A winter cluster is a slow-moving ball. It eats the honey it covers and creeps upward as it goes, because heat rises and the stores are usually stacked above the brood area. Moving sideways is much harder. In a long cold snap the cluster pulls in tight to save heat. Any bee that leaves the cluster to fetch honey from the next frame over chills to the point it can't move and dies. So the cluster finishes the comb it's sitting on and then stops, often with full frames a few centimetres to one side. Beekeepers find the sign in spring: a patch of dead bees with their heads pushed deep into empty cells, still searching.

**The twist:** The danger comes from where the honey sits, not just how much there is. A light hive with honey right above the cluster can make it through, while a heavy hive with stores off to the side can starve. That's why experienced keepers do two things. They "heft" the hive, lifting the back to judge its weight. And in late winter they put fondant or sugar directly on top of the cluster, because that's the one direction the bees can always reach. That means the query at `users.service.ts:22` isn't using the table definition you're looking at. Check these in order:

1. **A second schema definition.** The migration may have left another table definition behind:
   ```
   grep -rn "pgTable(" src | grep -i user
   ```

2. **Raw SQL.** Look for `` sql`… from users …` `` or `db.execute(...)` in the service. Raw SQL doesn't go through the table name, so it has to say `"User"` with the quotes, because Postgres folds unquoted names to lowercase.
