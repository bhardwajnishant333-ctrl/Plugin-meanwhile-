**TypeScript can't protect a server action. To the network, it's just an open POST endpoint.**

Every `"use server"` function Claude writes right now gets compiled into an HTTP endpoint with an action ID. The client calls it by sending that ID in a header along with whatever form data it likes. Next 15 made those IDs unguessable and drops actions nothing uses. Even so, any action your UI can call, an attacker can call too, with any payload. A curl command doesn't care about your `(prevState: State, formData: FormData) => Promise<State>` signature. TypeScript types don't exist at runtime, and `formData.get("amount")` gives back `FormDataEntryValue | null`, whatever `as number` claims.

So moving forms from API routes to actions doesn't make anything safer. It makes it *easier to forget*. With an API route, you knew you were writing an endpoint. A server action looks like a function call inside your component, so nobody thinks to put a lock on it.

**The twist:** the risk scales with how good the migration looks. A clean mechanical port that type-checks and passes tests is exactly when this gets missed, because your tests probably call the action the way your UI does, never the way an attacker would. When the build finishes, grep the new actions for two lines at the top of each one: an auth check and runtime validation (zod or similar). If one is missing, it's a public endpoint.

**Meanwhile:**
**An engine can be up a full rook and still not know the game is a draw, because "I'm ahead" and "I can win" are different questions, and it only knows how to ask the first one.**

**How it works.** An engine's score is a guess about the position, made at the end of a very deep search. The guess is mostly built on material, piece activity, king safety and so on. A fortress breaks that. Say the defender has a locked pawn chain and a king the attacker can never get past. The attacker really is ahead on the board, so every line the engine looks at ends in a position where it's still ahead, and the score stays at +3. To prove it's a draw, the engine would have to show that *no* line, however long, ever breaks through. A search with a limited horizon can't prove that. Stockfish does lower its score as the 50-move counter climbs, so the number drifts toward zero eventually. But that only happens once the shuffling has gone on long enough for the engine to see it, and by then you've already committed to the line.

Tablebases make this exact for 7 pieces or fewer. With 8 or more pieces and locked pawns, nothing is exact, and that's where fortresses live.

**The twist:** once everyone has the same engine, the engine's score stops being an edge, and its *mistakes* become the only edge left. At the top of correspondence chess, the vast majority of games are drawn. The games that get won mostly come from someone judging better than the number, for example:
- defending a line the engine scores −1.8 because you can see it's a fortress, or
- turning down a +2 line because the extra material can never break through.

Your job isn't to find the best move. It's to know when the score is lying. That's also a decent working rule for reading an AI agent's "all tests pass."

Here's the version most people tell: a random bug move rattled Kasparov so badly that he lost the match. That merges two different games.

**What actually happened**

**Game 1 (May 1997).** Deep Blue had black and a lost position. On move 44 it played the odd rook move 44...Rd1. Murray Campbell of the IBM team later said a bug caused it: the machine couldn't settle on a move and fell back to a near-random one. That account comes from Nate Silver's *The Signal and the Noise* (2012), quoting Campbell. Kasparov won Game 1. So the bug move came in a game the machine lost.

**Game 2 is what got to him.** Deep Blue had white. On move 36, everyone expected it to grab material with the queen, which is what 1997 engines were famous for. Instead it played the quiet 36.axb5 and followed with 37.Be4: positional moves that refused to grab the pawn. Kasparov didn't believe a machine played like that, and he suggested a human had stepped in. Then he **resigned in a position that was drawn**. Analysts later found a perpetual check for black.

In his book *Deep Thinking* (2017), Kasparov himself rejected the idea that the bug move psyched him out, and pointed to Game 2 as what affected him. I'm confident about the game facts. I'm less sure about the exact wording of each side's account.

**The twist:** it's the fortress story turned inside out. Kasparov didn't lose to the engine's score. He lost to his own guess about how the engine was scoring things. He judged that a machine "can't" play 36.axb5, decided something was off, and stopped trusting the board in front of him. The best human player of his time resigned a drawn game because his picture of the machine was wrong, not his chess.

1) **Deeper:** the Game 2 perpetual. Why nobody, Kasparov included, saw it at the board.
2) **Sideways:** "centaur" chess in the mid-2000s, when amateur teams with laptops beat grandmasters with engines.
3) **Wildcard:** the Mechanical Turk (1770). The first "chess machine" was a man hidden in a box, and it toured for decades, playing (by legend) Napoleon.
