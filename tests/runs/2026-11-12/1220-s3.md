**In the 1760s the Royal Navy's longitude tables ran on dual redundancy with a diff check, and it failed the way replicas fail today.**

Nevil Maskelyne became Astronomer Royal in 1765 and launched the *Nautical Almanac*; the first edition covered 1767. It gave lunar-distance tables at three-hour intervals, so a navigator could measure the angle between the Moon and a star and work out Greenwich time, and from that his longitude. The arithmetic was farmed out by post to "computers", people working at home, often country clergymen and schoolmasters. Each month's tables went to two computers who worked independently, and a third person, the "comparer", checked their results against each other. If they agreed, the figure went to print. It was a two-replica design with a checksum on top.

The twist is that the check only works if the two replicas really are independent. David Alan Grier's *When Computers Were Human* describes computers being caught copying from each other. I'm going from memory on the specific names and dates, so treat those as unverified. Copied work means shared errors, so the comparer saw two matching answers and passed them. The check didn't just miss the mistake, it certified it. Your clusters have the same weak spot: two replicas on the same image, the same glibc and the same collation version aren't two opinions. They're one opinion written down twice. That's also why you're reindexing the text indexes: text sort order comes from the collation library, so a library change can quietly break every replica's indexes at once.

**The most famous sentence in British naval history was edited by a codebook.**

On the morning of 21 October 1805, Nelson told his signal lieutenant, John Pasco, to send "England confides that every man will do his duty." Pasco asked if he could change "confides" to "expects". The fleet used Home Popham's *Telegraphic Signals or Marine Vocabulary* (first issued 1800, expanded 1803), which works like a dictionary. Each common word has a number, and the number is hoisted as a short stack of numeral flags, so one hoist sends a whole word. "Expects" was in the vocabulary. "Confides" wasn't, so it would have had to be spelled out one letter per hoist. Nelson agreed. Even so, "duty" wasn't in the book either and went up letter by letter. The whole message took about a dozen hoists, though I'm not certain of the exact count. Collingwood is reported to have muttered that he wished Nelson would stop signalling, since they all knew what to do.

The twist is that the codebook didn't just carry the message, it shaped it. A word in the dictionary cost one hoist, and a word outside it cost one hoist per letter, so the cheap word won. The same pressure shows up in an alerting schema: what's easy to express gets said, and the nuance that doesn't fit the schema gets dropped. LLM tokenizers work the same way, since words outside the vocabulary cost several tokens. Whoever writes the dictionary quietly edits everyone who uses it.

**Nelson's most important signal at Trafalgar told the fleet nothing it didn't already know, and that's why it worked.**

Soon after "England expects", Victory hoisted signal No. 16 from the general signal book: "Engage the enemy more closely." It took one hoist and needed no spelling. The usual account says it stayed flying until the rigging holding it was shot away. I'm confident of the signal itself but less sure the "shot away" detail is documented rather than legend. The real instructions had gone out two weeks earlier, in Nelson's memorandum of 9 October 1805, the "Nelson Touch": break the enemy line in two places, crush the centre and rear, and, in his words, "no Captain can do very wrong if he places his Ship alongside that of an Enemy." Once the smoke rolled in and masts started coming down, signalling mostly stopped working. Nelson was shot around 1:15 pm and died around 4:30 pm, and the fleet kept fighting effectively without him.

The twist is that the system was declarative, not imperative. The memorandum was the spec. Signal 16 was a desired state that stayed on display, not a one-off message, so each captain could read it whenever he could see it and steer his own ship toward it. That's a reconciliation loop: when the central controller went down, the agents kept closing the gap between what they could see and what the spec said. It's the same design as the PGO operator running your upgrade: you don't tell it what to do step by step, you declare what you want, and it keeps working toward that, including when parts of the system break.

**"No plan survives contact with the enemy" is a misquote, and the original makes a sharper point.**

What Helmuth von Moltke the Elder, chief of the Prussian General Staff from 1857 to 1888, actually wrote in his 1871 essay on strategy was roughly this: no plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main force. That isn't "plans are useless". It means the plan has a known horizon, and past it you need something else. The Prussian answer grew out of the reforms after Napoleon crushed them at Jena-Auerstedt in 1806: give intent, not instructions. Tell a subordinate what to achieve and why, and leave the how to him. The word *Auftragstaktik* itself came later, from the doctrinal debates of the late 1800s. I believe Moltke never used it, but I'm not certain. The US Army formalized the idea as "mission command" (ADP 6-0, 2012). Modern incident response borrowed its structure from the Incident Command System, which came out of the 1970s California wildfire programme FIRESCOPE.

The twist is that mission command costs more than giving orders, not less. It only works if subordinates already share the doctrine, if training is heavy, and if leaders tolerate mistakes made in good faith. There's an often-repeated line, attributed to Prince Friedrich Karl, that the King made an officer a major because he trusted him to know when not to obey; I can't vouch for its source. For runbooks, the lesson is to start each one with its intent, for example "protect write availability; stale reads are acceptable". Step-by-step instructions stop being useful at their first contact with an incident, while a stated intent still tells the on-call engineer what to do past the edge of the steps.
