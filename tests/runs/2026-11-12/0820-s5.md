**Switzerland's rail planners worked to a slogan: "as fast as necessary, not as fast as possible."**

In 1982 Switzerland adopted a clock-face timetable (*Taktfahrplan*): every route runs at the same minutes past every hour, all day. The Bahn 2000 project then added a rule that sounds backwards. Big hubs like Zurich, Bern and Basel act as nodes. Trains reach a node just before :00 or :30 and leave just after, so every connection there takes a few minutes.

For this to work, the trip between two nodes has to take just under a multiple of 30 minutes. A 34-minute trip is useless, because it misses every connection. A 27-minute trip is perfect. Making it 20 minutes gains nothing, because the train would just wait for the next connection anyway.

So they spent money only where it moved a trip under the next boundary. The Mattstetten–Rothrist line opened in 2004 mainly to get Zurich–Bern under one hour. Elsewhere they skipped speed upgrades that wouldn't change any connection.

**The twist:** the timetable came first and the tracks were built to fit it. The schedule is the product and the track exists to serve it. Your app is showing the end result of that thinking, a timetable people can memorize because it repeats every hour.

**The first big public debate over revealing security flaws was about locks, in 1851, not software.**

For about 60 years, Bramah & Co. kept a "challenge lock" in their London shop window. A sign offered 200 guineas to anyone who could pick it. Nobody did.

**The Chubb detector lock was built to report break-ins. Against a skilled picker, those reports helped the attacker.**

The lock exists because of a burglary. In 1817 thieves used false keys to break into Portsmouth Dockyard, and the British government offered a reward for a lock that only its own key could open. Jeremiah Chubb patented his detector lock in 1818. (Company history says a convict lockpicker was offered a reward to beat it, worked on it for months and gave up. Treat that as lore, not record.)

**How it works:** it's a lever lock. Each lever has to be lifted to exactly the right height before the bolt can move. Chubb added a detector lever. If any lever was lifted too high, which is what happens when someone picks by feel, the detector caught it and jammed the bolt. Next time the owner tried the real key, it wouldn't open. They had to reset the lock with a special turn of the key, and that told them someone had tried to get in.

**The leak:** lever locks have the same weakness as the pin locks you pick. If you press on the bolt, one lever is held tighter than the others, and the tool can feel it. A picker who can read that feedback doesn't need the levers to stay silent. The lock leaks its secret through friction, one lever at a time. Hobbs's answer was his own "Protector" lock, designed so that pressure on the bolt never reached the levers and the feedback was gone. (I'm going from memory on that mechanism, so it's worth checking.)

**The twist:** any defense that responds to an attack can teach the attacker. Software found this out again in 2002, when Serge Vaudenay published the padding oracle attack. A server that returned one error for bad padding and a different error for everything else was enough to recover encrypted data, one byte at a time. That's the lever lock again: a system answering "warmer/colder" to someone probing it. The fix in both cases is the same. Give the same response to every wrong attempt.

**Meanwhile:**
**Safecrackers mostly don't listen for clicks. They plot graphs.**

A typical safe combination lock has a stack of wheels, one for each number in the combination, and each wheel has a notch (the gate). The lock opens when all the gates line up under a metal bar called the fence. The dial turns a drive cam at the front, which has its own notch. A lever rests against that cam.

**The manipulation method:** keep light pressure on the lever and turn the dial back and forth. You'll find two "contact points" where the lever hits the edges of the cam's notch. Write them down. Move the wheels a couple of numbers and measure again. When a wheel's gate passes under the fence, the fence drops slightly and the gap between the two contact points shrinks by a tiny fraction of a number. Plot the readings and the gates appear as small dips. A skilled operator reads them by feel and on paper. The stethoscope is mostly Hollywood, although some people do use amplification.

That's the lever-lock leak again: physical contact gives away the internal state.

**The fix:** the US standard UL 768 rates combination locks. A Group 1 lock has to hold out against expert manipulation for 20 working hours. Designers met it by removing the leak instead of hiding it. The fence never touches the wheels while you dial and only drops once the combination is complete. They also added light, uniform wheels and fake gates that give the same reading as real ones. As with the padding oracle, every wrong position has to feel the same.

**The pattern across all three locks:** security fails at the interface, where the internal state shows through to the outside.
