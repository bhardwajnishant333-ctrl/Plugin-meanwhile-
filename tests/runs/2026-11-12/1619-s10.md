**The famous "too many choices" study is real, but the effect mostly disappears when other researchers try to repeat it.**

In 2000, psychologists Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper set up a jam-tasting table in a California grocery store. When it offered 24 flavours, more shoppers stopped (60%) than when it offered 6 (40%). But only 3% of the shoppers who stopped at the big display bought a jar, against 30% at the small one. "Choice overload" became one of the most quoted findings in business.

Then in 2010, Benjamin Scheibehenne and colleagues pooled 50 experiments on choice overload. On average the effect came out at **roughly zero**. Sometimes more options hurt, sometimes they helped, and often they made no difference.

The useful part came later. A 2015 meta-analysis by Chernev and colleagues found *when* the effect shows up. It happens when the options are hard to compare, when the person has no clear preference going in, and when they're trying to choose "the best one" rather than "a good one." So the number of options alone doesn't overwhelm people. It's a large number of near-identical options combined with pressure to choose the best.

Your instinct is right for a sail. Run dead downwind and the faster you go, the less wind you feel. At wind speed you feel none at all, so you can't push past it. The cart worked because it had no sail.

**Blackbird, July 2010, El Mirage dry lake in California.** Rick Cavallaro built a cart with a big propeller and no motor. The wheels drive the propeller. The North American Land Sailing Association (NALSA) ratified a run straight downwind at about **2.8 times the wind speed**.

The trick is that the energy comes from the gap between the moving air and the still ground, not from the wind you feel on board.

- The wheels push against the ground and turn the propeller. This takes a small force over a fast-moving surface.
- The propeller shoves air *backwards*. Seen from the ground, it is slowing the tailwind down.
- Slowing down moving air takes energy out of it, and that holds even when the cart is going faster than the wind. Pushing on slower-moving air is cheaper than what the wheels collect from the ground, so there is energy left over to keep accelerating.

You already know a version of this. Foiling America's Cup boats and iceboats get downwind faster than the wind by gybing at an angle rather than running square. They work off the gap between water or ice and air, not from being pushed. Blackbird does the same thing in a straight line, with a propeller where the sail would be.

It still convinces almost nobody at first sight. In 2021 Derek Muller (Veritasium) bet UCLA physicist Alex Kusenko $10,000 that it worked, and Kusenko paid up.

**Pointing straight into the wind, the same idea runs in reverse.** In 2012 Cavallaro's team drove Blackbird directly upwind at about **2 times the wind speed**. I'm confident of the run but not of the exact ratified figure. There's also a whole race series of wind-turbine cars that drive straight into the wind: Racing Aeolus, held in the Netherlands since 2008.

How it works going upwind:

- The propeller becomes a **wind turbine**. The air hits it at wind speed plus the cart's own speed, which is fast.
- The turbine sends that power down to the wheels. The ground is only passing at the cart's speed, which is slower.
- Power is force times speed. Air rushing past quickly gives you power at a low force. Feed that power to slow-moving wheels and it becomes a larger forward force. The forward push from the wheels is bigger than the backward drag on the turbine, so the cart moves forward into the wind.

It's a gearbox between two things moving at different speeds.

**The downwind and upwind carts are the same machine run in opposite directions.**
- Downwind: the *ground* is the fast-moving side, so the wheels harvest power and the propeller spends it.
- Upwind: the *air* is the fast-moving side, so the propeller harvests power and the wheels spend it.

Neither one cares which way the wind blows. Each needs air and ground moving at different speeds, and something that converts between them.

Your dinghy does the same thing: the sail works the air, the centreboard works the water, and the hull links the two. Blackbird swaps the centreboard for wheels and the sail for a propeller.
