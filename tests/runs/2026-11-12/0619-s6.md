**European robins lost their magnetic compass on a university campus. What broke it was electrical noise from the city, at strengths far below safety limits.**

In the early 2000s, Henrik Mouritsen's lab at the University of Oldenburg kept migratory robins in wooden huts on campus. In orientation tests the birds should have pointed in their migratory direction, and they didn't. Their sense of direction was scrambled. For years the team couldn't work out why.

Then they lined the huts with grounded aluminium mesh, which blocks broadband electromagnetic noise between roughly 50 kHz and 5 MHz: AM radio and ordinary electronics. The robins oriented correctly again. When the mesh was ungrounded, or noise was added back, they lost it again. The results were published in *Nature* in 2014 (Engels et al.). The noise was thousands of times weaker than what guidelines treat as relevant to biology.

**The twist:** a compass made of magnetic iron particles shouldn't notice fields that weak. A quantum mechanism would. The leading idea is a "radical pair" in a protein in the eye called cryptochrome. Light creates two linked electrons in that protein, and Earth's magnetic field slightly shifts how they behave. If that's right, the bird literally *sees* north as a pattern laid over its vision. That makes it fragile in a way only a quantum sensor would be. In 2021, a paper in *Nature* showed that cryptochrome 4 from robins is magnetically sensitive in the lab. Whether this is how robins actually navigate is still being argued about. It's the strongest candidate, not settled science.

So the best evidence for quantum biology in birds came from an experiment that kept failing for reasons nobody had set out to test.

**A widget that's on screen all day gets about 40 to 70 refreshes. That's Apple's own figure: one reload every 15 to 60 minutes, for a widget people look at often.**

Your widget never runs while it's on screen. WidgetKit asks your `TimelineProvider` for a list of entries, renders each one's SwiftUI view into an archived snapshot ahead of time, and flips to the next snapshot at its date. What the user sees is closer to a flipbook than a live view. Your code runs only when a reload happens, and reloads come out of that daily budget. Apple's docs list some reloads that don't count against it, such as ones while your app is in the foreground. I believe reloads after an interactive tap are treated generously too, but I'm not certain of the exact rules, so check the "Keeping a widget up to date" page.

**The twist for your refactor:** with App Intents, a tap runs `perform()` and then the timeline reloads. The button doesn't change the view directly. The new state appears only when your provider builds a new snapshot. So a slow provider shows up as a slow, unresponsive tap. Also, `Text(date, style: .timer)` and `.relative` are drawn and updated by the system itself. They're the only way to get a ticking clock without using up any refreshes.

1) **Deeper:** what can and can't be interactive in a widget, and why only `Button` and `Toggle` made the cut

**Myth-bust: tulip mania probably didn't ruin anyone. The famous crash story was mostly a moral lesson that later writers copied from each other.**

The usual version: in 1630s Holland, ordinary people swapped houses for single bulbs, prices collapsed in February 1637, and the Dutch economy was wrecked. Most of that comes from Charles Mackay's 1841 *Extraordinary Popular Delusions*. Mackay took it largely from 1637 Dutch pamphlets, which were moral dialogues written to scold greed.

The historian Anne Goldgar went through the actual archives: notary records, court files, the buyers' names. Her 2007 book *Tulipmania* reports what she found. Trading was concentrated among a fairly small circle of merchants and well-off craftsmen. She found no wave of bankruptcies she could link to tulips, and no economic damage across the country. Many contracts were for future delivery, and after the crash a lot of them were simply never paid in full. I recall the settlement in Haarlem was a small percentage of the agreed price, but I'm not sure of the exact figure.

**The twist:** prices really did spike and crash. What spread out of all proportion was the *story* of the disaster. It got passed on because it was a useful warning, not because anyone checked the records. The most famous financial bubble may be mainly a bubble in the story told about it.

**The most expensive tulip of the mania, Semper Augustus, with white petals and red flames, got its pattern from a virus. The same virus slowly killed the bulb.**

Its colours came from what is now called tulip breaking virus. It's spread by aphids, especially the green peach aphid. In a normal tulip, a red or purple pigment called anthocyanin covers the whole petal evenly. The virus disrupts that in patches, so the white or yellow underneath shows through as feathers and flames. Growers called these flowers "broken."

But the virus also weakens the bulb. An infected bulb produces fewer and smaller offsets, the baby bulbs that growers used to multiply a variety, and over generations the line gets weaker. Nobody in the 1630s knew why. Breaking seemed random: you could plant a plain bulb and occasionally get a flamed one. Its cause was only worked out in the late 1920s. Dorothy Cayley at the John Innes institute showed that breaking could be passed on by grafting, and aphid transmission was confirmed soon after. I'm less sure of the exact names and year for that second step.

**The twist:** the beauty and the scarcity were one fact, not two. The flamed bulbs were rare *because* the same infection that made them beautiful made them reproduce badly. The market was paying more for the sickest plants. Striped "Rembrandt" tulips sold today are bred to be striped and stable, without the virus.

1) **Deeper:** how a virus can paint a pattern, and why it comes out as flames rather than spots
2) **Sideways:** other cases where a flaw drives the price, like inclusions in amber or misprinted stamps
3) **Wildcard:** aphids give birth to live young that are already pregnant
