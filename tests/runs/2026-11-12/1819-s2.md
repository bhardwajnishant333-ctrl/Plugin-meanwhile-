**Computers proved chess's 50-move rule wrong, and FIDE kept it anyway.**

The rule says a game is drawn after 50 moves with no capture and no pawn move. It was a judgment call: masters assumed any real win would happen faster than that. Then in the 1970s and 80s, Ken Thompson and others built endgame tablebases, which are complete solutions of every position with only a few pieces left. They found endings that are forced wins but need well over 50 moves, for example two bishops against a knight. FIDE responded by adding exceptions that allowed 75 or 100 moves for specific endings. The list kept growing as more wins were found. In 1992 FIDE scrapped all the exceptions and went back to a flat 50. The seven-piece tablebases finished around 2012 later found a forced mate that takes 549 moves.

**The twist:** FIDE decided it was fine for the rule to be wrong, because a rule every arbiter can check with a move counter beats one that's correct but needs a lookup table. **A tablebase starts from checkmate and works backwards.**

It's called retrograde analysis, and it goes in four steps:
1. List every legal position for a given set of pieces, such as K+Q vs K+R.
2. Mark every position where the side to move is already checkmated as "lost in 0".
3. Step backwards one move at a time. If any move from a position reaches a lost position for the opponent, that position is a win. If every move from a position reaches a won position for the opponent, it's a loss.
4. Repeat until a full pass changes nothing. Every position still unmarked is a draw.

There's no search, no evaluation and no guessing. You fill in the whole table until it stops changing.

The cost is the number of positions. Each extra piece multiplies it by roughly the number of squares, so about 64x. Ken Thompson's early tables from the 1970s fit on a minicomputer. The Lomonosov seven-piece tables were computed in 2012 on a Moscow State University supercomputer and came to about 140 TB, because they store the exact distance to mate for every position. The Syzygy seven-piece tables, which store less per position, came out around 2018 at roughly 18 TB. I'm confident about the gap between the two but less sure of the exact figures.

**The twist:** in 1978 Thompson challenged grandmaster Walter Browne to win K+Q vs K+R against his table. That ending is a textbook win. Browne failed to mate within 50 moves on his first attempt, because the table kept finding defences humans had never noticed. He studied the ending and won the rematch. The "solved" endgame was easy only against defenders who also played like humans.

That backwards fill-until-nothing-changes loop is called fixed-point iteration. Compilers use the same idea to analyse loops, and TypeScript's type narrowing inside loops works in a similar spirit.

**Checkers is solved, but nobody has ever worked out every checkers position.**

Chess has about 4.8 × 10^44 legal positions, from John Tromp's count around 2021. Claude Shannon's famous 10^120 from his 1950 paper is a different number: it counts possible games, not positions. Either way, you can't build a tablebase for chess. Storing one bit per position would take far more storage than exists on Earth.

Checkers has about 5 × 10^20 positions. Jonathan Schaeffer's team, who built the Chinook program, started in 1989 and published in *Science* in 2007: with perfect play from both sides, checkers is a draw. They did it with a pincer:
- From the back, they built endgame tables for every position with 10 or fewer pieces.
- From the front, they ran a proof search from the opening that only had to show each line reaches a position the tables already solve.

The search only needed lines good enough to settle the result. It never had to look at most positions. By their own account it examined around 10^14 positions, a small fraction of the total. I'm confident about the order of magnitude, not the exact figure.

**The twist:** checkers is *weakly* solved. We know the result from the starting position and a strategy that guarantees at least a draw. But there are plenty of mid-game positions whose true result nobody knows, because the proof never needed them. "Solved" means proving the result from the start, not knowing everything. Connect Four was solved in 1988, independently by James Allen and Victor Allis: the first player wins if they start in the centre column.

That's also why you can't solve your Caro-Kann. The opening is effectively out of reach. The only "solved" chess is the tablebase endgame, where 7 pieces is the current limit.
