City robins that sing at night probably aren't confused by streetlights. They're dodging the traffic.

In 2007, Richard Fuller, Philip Warren and Kevin Gaston studied European robins in Sheffield, UK (published in *Biology Letters*). Robins singing at night had long been blamed on artificial light. But daytime noise turned out to predict night singing better than light at night did. Where the daytime din was loudest, the robins moved their singing into the quiet hours.

Great tits handle the same problem another way. Hans Slabbekoorn and Margriet Peet (*Nature*, 2003, Leiden) found that great tits in noisier parts of the city sing at a higher minimum pitch. That lifts their song above the low rumble of traffic.

Here's the switch: it's about your own wire this time.

Two TCP optimizations, each a good idea on its own, can stall a request for 40 to 200 ms when they meet.

In 1984 John Nagle published RFC 896 to fix "tinygrams": one byte of keystroke data sent with 40 bytes of headers. His algorithm holds back a small write while earlier data is still waiting to be acknowledged. Separately, delayed ACK (in RFC 1122, 1989) lets the receiver wait before acknowledging, in the hope of piggybacking the ACK on a reply.

Put them together and you get a standoff. The sender won't send until it sees an ACK, and the receiver won't ACK until more data arrives. Nothing moves until the receiver's delayed-ACK timer fires, about 40 ms minimum on Linux and up to 200 ms on Windows. The classic trigger is a write-write-read pattern, such as sending headers and body as two separate small writes.

The twist is that Go turns Nagle off for you: TCP connections in `net` default to `TCP_NODELAY`. So you've probably never been hit by this. A service in another language with default socket settings can still show unexplained 40 ms latency floors that no profiler explains. Nagle himself has said the real culprit is delayed ACK.

2) Sideways: the tinygram problem's cousin in markets, why exchanges batch orders into auctions

You've said meh twice, so here's something completely different: a physics puzzle that went unsolved for about 140 years.

Take a lawn sprinkler that spins as water jets out of its bent arms, and run it in reverse by sucking the water in. Which way does it turn?

Ernst Mach raised the question in the 1880s. Richard Feynman made it famous: he tried it at Princeton and wrecked a large glass bottle of water in the lab. For decades, experiments disagreed. Some saw no rotation, some saw a slight wobble, some saw backward spin.

In 2024, Leif Ristroph's group at NYU published a precise version in *Physical Review Letters*, using near-frictionless bearings. The sprinkler does turn in reverse, slowly, about 50 times slower than in normal mode. The force doesn't come from the nozzles. Water flowing in through the bent arms forms jets inside the central hub, and those jets push the sprinkler around. I'm confident about the slow reverse spin and less sure of the finer fluid details.

The twist is that running a process in reverse isn't the same as rewinding it. Water leaving a nozzle is a narrow jet. Water entering one is pulled in from every direction at once. That asymmetry is why the obvious answers failed for a century.

Blowing pushes air out as a jet. Sucking pulls air in from every direction at once, and that difference matters a lot.

When you blow, the air leaves your lips as a fairly tight stream and keeps its forward momentum. A round jet spreads slowly, and its centre speed drops roughly in proportion to distance (1/r). That's why you can still put out a candle from an arm's length away.

When you suck, there's no stream. Air comes in from a whole half-sphere around your mouth, so the flow is spread over an area that grows with the square of the distance. The speed falls off roughly as 1/r². Move the flame a few centimetres away and it hardly feels anything.

The twist is that nature works around the same limit. Fish that feed by suction, such as bass and sunfish, have to strike from very close, roughly within one mouth-width of the prey, because the pull vanishes beyond that. It's also why a vacuum cleaner nozzle has to almost touch the floor, and why a kitchen range hood hanging high above the stove catches less smoke than you'd expect.
