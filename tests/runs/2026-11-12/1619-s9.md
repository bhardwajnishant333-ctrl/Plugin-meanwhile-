**Your STM32G4 has a Cold War bomber's navigation maths built into its silicon.**

In 1956 Jack Volder, an engineer at Convair, needed real-time trigonometry for the B-58 Hustler's navigation computer. The hardware couldn't afford multipliers, so he invented CORDIC, published in 1959. It rotates a vector by a fixed series of angles, arctan(1), arctan(1/2), arctan(1/4) and so on, deciding each step's direction from a sign bit. Every step is just a shift and an add. After about 16 iterations you have sin and cos to about 16 bits, scaled by a constant gain of about 1.647 that you correct once at the end. Calculators of the 1970s used the same family of shift-and-add tricks. ST put a hardware CORDIC unit into the G4 line, alongside a filter-maths accelerator (FMAC).

**The twist:** FOC is mostly a trigonometry problem. Every PWM cycle, often 20 kHz or faster, the controller needs sin and cos of the rotor angle to run the Park transform and its inverse. That's exactly what Volder's bomber circuit was built for. Portable FOC crates usually compute sin and cos in software, because they can't assume your chip has a CORDIC unit. I don't know which approach your new crate uses. It's worth checking when the agent finishes, because it could free up a real slice of your control-loop time.

**A whale product became the legal definition of a unit of light.**

Spermaceti is the waxy material from a sperm whale's head organ. It isn't quite the same thing as the oil. Candles made from it burned brighter and cleaner than tallow and didn't sag in summer heat, which made them the premium light of the age. On Nantucket, the Whaling Museum is housed in the Hadwen & Barney candle factory of 1847. Spermaceti candles burned so consistently that Britain's Metropolis Gas Act of 1860 made one the official yardstick. "One candlepower" meant a pure spermaceti candle weighing one-sixth of a pound and burning 120 grains an hour. Gas companies were legally tested against a whale.

**The twist:** That unit never fully died. It was replaced by the "international candle" in 1909 and then by the candela in 1948. Each new definition was tuned to roughly match the size of the one before it, the same way you keep a legacy API stable. Today's candela, defined in 1979, contains an oddly specific constant: 1/683 of a watt per steradian at 540 THz. That ugly 683 is there to preserve continuity with the older standards. Part of a measure written into the SI traces back, through that chain of adjustments, to a Nantucket-era candle.

**The sperm whale's huge forehead is mostly a sound machine, and possibly also a weapon.**

The spermaceti organ and the "junk" beneath it make up a nose that can run to about a quarter or a third of the animal's length. A click starts at a valve near the front of the head called the "monkey lips." It travels *backward* through the spermaceti organ, bounces off an air sac at the skull, then travels forward again through the junk, which focuses it like a stack of acoustic lenses. In the early 2000s, Bertel Møhl's team recorded clicks in the wild at about 230+ dB (re 1 µPa), among the loudest sounds made by any animal. It's sonar, built from wax and air.

It's the organ being used for its second job. The ramming theory is still debated. The sonar function is well established.
