**Every crease in your pattern can pass every local test and the sheet still won't fold flat. Checking whether it will is NP-hard.**

The local tests are easy, and you probably already use them. At any single vertex, Maekawa's theorem says mountain and valley folds differ by exactly 2. Kawasaki's theorem says alternating angles around the vertex add up to 180°. If both hold, that one vertex folds flat, and you can check it in linear time.

In 1996, Marshall Bern and Barry Hayes proved that the global question is NP-hard: does the whole crease pattern fold flat? It stays NP-hard even when someone has already marked every crease as mountain or valley. The difficulty isn't in the angles at all. It's in the **layer ordering**: which flap tucks over which. Two vertices that are each fine on their own can force paper layers far apart to pass through each other. Bern and Hayes built crease-pattern "gadgets" that act as logic gates, so solving a fold is as hard as solving a Boolean satisfiability problem.

The twist: tessellation folders mostly dodge this without noticing. Repeating twist and pleat grids are a well-behaved special case where layer conflicts stay local, so passing the vertex checks is roughly enough in practice. The worst case is still out there, just not in the patterns people like to fold. That's the general lesson too: when every part passes its own validation, you've only shown the parts are fine, not the whole.

1) **Deeper**: how those gadgets turn paper into logic gates
2) **Sideways**: origami can trisect an angle, which compass and straightedge provably can't (Abe, 1980)
3) **Wildcard**: the Miura fold that flew a solar array in space in 1995

**"Trisecting an angle is impossible" is only true for compass and straightedge. A sheet of paper does it in one fold.**

In 1837, Pierre Wantzel proved that no compass-and-straightedge construction can trisect an arbitrary angle. Those tools only produce lengths you can reach with +, −, ×, ÷ and square roots. Trisecting an angle means solving a cubic: cos 3θ = 4cos³θ − 3cosθ. A cube root isn't something square roots can build.

Paper has a different primitive. When you fold so a point lands on a line, the crease is always tangent to a **parabola**: the point is its focus and the line is its directrix. Now do it twice at once, folding so point A lands on line 1 *and* point B lands on line 2. That crease has to be tangent to two parabolas at the same time, and finding it means solving a cubic. Margherita Beloch noticed this in 1936. Humiaki Huzita listed it among his folding axioms in 1989, as what's now called axiom 6. Hisashi Abe had already published a practical trisection based on it in 1980: two parallel creases, then one two-points-onto-two-lines fold. Peter Messer used the same move in 1986 to double the cube, folding a length of ∛2.

The twist: Wantzel never proved trisection impossible. He proved it impossible *with that toolset*. A compass is a degree-2 machine and a fold is a degree-3 machine. The ceiling came from the primitive, not from how clever anyone was. Any time a problem has been "proven impossible", it's worth asking which operations the proof assumed.

1) **Deeper**: the full set of seven fold axioms, and the proof that no single fold can do anything beyond them
2) **Sideways**: the neusis, a "cheating" marked ruler the ancient Greeks used to trisect angles anyway
3) **Wildcard**: why a regular 7-gon is impossible with compass but foldable, and what that has to do with 17

**You can fold a regular 7-gon but not an 11-gon, and the reason is the number one less than each.**

Gauss was 18 in 1796 when he found that a regular 17-gon can be built with compass and straightedge. It was the first new constructible polygon in about 2,000 years, and he reportedly chose math as a career because of it. The key is 17 − 1 = 16 = 2⁴. Building the 17-gon comes down to an equation that splits into a chain of four quadratic steps. Each step needs only a square root, which is exactly what a compass can do.

Wantzel finished the rule in 1837. A regular n-gon is constructible exactly when n is a power of 2 times *distinct* Fermat primes, which are primes of the form 2^(2^k) + 1: 3, 5, 17, 257, 65537. For the 7-gon, 7 − 1 = 6 = 2 × 3. That 3 means one cubic step, so the compass is stuck.

Paper solves cubics (the axiom 6 fold from last time), so the rule widens. A polygon with a prime number of sides p can be folded when p − 1 has only 2s and 3s as factors. Those are called Pierpont primes: 7, 13, 19, 37, 73... The heptagon folds. The 11-gon doesn't, because 11 − 1 = 10 = 2 × 5 and that needs a degree-5 step that no single fold provides. That makes 11 the smallest regular polygon origami can't make.

The twist: whether a polygon can be built has nothing to do with how it looks. It depends on how n − 1 factors, meaning which degrees of equations the problem breaks into, and whether your tool's basic operation reaches that degree. Compass: 2. One fold: 3. A tool that solves quintics could build the 11-gon.

1) **Deeper**: how Gauss actually split the 17-gon into four nested quadratics
2) **Sideways**: two-fold origami, where folding along two creases at once reaches degree 5 and the 11-gon
3) **Wildcard**: Fermat guessed every number of the form 2^(2^k) + 1 is prime, and 65537 may be the last one that is
