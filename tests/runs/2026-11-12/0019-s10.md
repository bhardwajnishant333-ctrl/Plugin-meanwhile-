Tyrian purple was made from murex sea snails. A gland in the snail gives off a pale, almost colourless liquid. Sunlight turns it green, then blue, and finally purple. The light is part of what makes the colour.

**In the 1950s, physiologists calculated that a breath-hold diver's lungs would be crushed somewhere around 30 to 50 metres. Divers have since gone past 200.**

Their maths came from Boyle's law: double the pressure and a gas takes up half the space. Every 10 metres of seawater adds one atmosphere. By 90 metres, the air in your lungs has shrunk to a tenth of its surface volume. The experts compared that with "residual volume", the air you can never breathe out, and concluded the chest would cave in once the air got smaller than that.

Divers ignored the forecast. Jacques Mayol reached 100 metres in 1976, and Herbert Nitsch went to 214 metres in 2007.

What saved their lungs is called the **blood shift**. As the air compresses, blood moves out of the arms and legs and into the vessels in and around the lungs. Unlike gas, blood barely compresses, so it fills the space the shrinking air leaves behind. In the late 1960s, US Navy researchers measured roughly a litre of blood moving into a record diver's chest during a deep dive. I'm confident about the mechanism. Treat that exact figure as approximate.

**The twist:** the experts got the physics right but the body wrong. They treated the chest as a rigid box of air, when it can swap gas for liquid. The same mechanism has a limit, though. When it goes too far, fluid leaks into the lungs. That is the "lung squeeze" freedivers cough up blood from, and it's why depth training goes slowly.

1. **Deeper:** why the dive reflex starts the moment your face gets wet, even in a bathtub

**The dive reflex starts in your face, not your lungs. Wet your forehead and cheeks with cold water while you hold your breath, and your heart slows even in a kitchen sink.**

The sensor is the trigeminal nerve, which carries touch and temperature from the forehead, eyes and nose. Cold water there sets off a chain of responses through the vagus nerve, the brain's main "slow down" line to the heart.

- **Heart rate drops.** In untrained people the drop is often modest, around 10 to 25 percent. In elite divers at depth it can fall below 30 beats a minute. The figures vary a lot from person to person, so treat them as rough.
- **Blood vessels in the arms and legs tighten.** Oxygen is kept for the heart and brain. This also starts the blood shift you just read about.
- **The spleen squeezes.** It works like a reserve tank, pushing extra red blood cells into circulation. That's one reason a second or third breath-hold often feels easier than the first.

Colder water makes a stronger response. Holding your breath on top of the cold makes it stronger again.

**The twist:** doctors use the same reflex. When someone's heart races in a certain kind of abnormal rhythm (supraventricular tachycardia), one standard first step is to put ice water on their face, and for small infants sometimes to briefly dip the face in it. The vagus nerve then tells the heart to slow down and reset. Your freediving reflex doubles as an emergency treatment.

And the spleen part has a sequel. A 2018 study in *Cell* found that the Bajau, a sea-nomad people in Southeast Asia who have dived for generations, have spleens about 50 percent larger than their land-farming neighbours, linked to a gene called PDE10A. In this case, natural selection seems to have acted on a diving organ.
