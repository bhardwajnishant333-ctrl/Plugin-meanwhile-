**Meanwhile:**
**About 200 parameters can recover 90% of a full fine-tune.**

In 2020, Aghajanyan, Zettlemoyer and Gupta measured the "intrinsic dimension" of fine-tuning. They froze RoBERTa-Large and trained only a small random subspace, projected back into all ~355M weights. On the MRPC paraphrase task, about 200 trainable parameters got them to 90% of full fine-tuning accuracy. I'm confident about the order of magnitude, a bit less sure of the exact count. Two more findings from the same paper:

- Longer pretraining lowers the intrinsic dimension.
- Bigger models have lower intrinsic dimension than small ones, not higher.

LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) built on this result. Its paper reported that rank 1 or 2 on the attention query and value matrices was competitive on GPT-3 175B.

**The world's largest shipping fleets can add or remove capacity without building or scrapping a single ship. They just change the speed.**

How it works: a ship's fuel burn per day rises roughly with the cube of its speed, so fuel per mile rises with about the square. Cut speed 10% and daily fuel falls about 27% (0.9³ ≈ 0.73), while fuel per voyage falls about 19%. After 2008, when bunker prices were high and freight rates collapsed, Maersk led the industry into "slow steaming." Container ships designed for about 24 knots dropped to around 18, and some "super slow" services ran nearer 12–15. The catch is that a weekly Asia–Europe loop still needs a ship leaving every week. Slower ships mean more of them on each loop, roughly 8 becoming 9 or 10. Slow steaming quietly absorbed a lot of the surplus fleet without anyone scrapping hulls.

**The twist:** speed is a supply valve that works in both directions. When rates spike, owners speed up and release hidden capacity in weeks, long before any newbuild arrives 2–3 years later. That partly caps spikes. Since January 2023, though, the IMO's EEXI and CII carbon rules have pushed many ships to run slower, some with engine power limits fitted. That valve is now partly welded shut. My read, and it's an inference, not a settled result: the soft part of the supply curve is smaller, so future demand shocks could hit rates harder than history suggests. If you watch the Baltic Dry, average fleet speed is a leading indicator worth tracking alongside the orderbook.

**On 26 April 1956, a converted WWII tanker carried 58 truck trailer bodies from Newark to Houston. It cut loading costs about 30×.**

How it works: the ship was the *Ideal X*, and the owner was Malcolm McLean, a trucking magnate, not a shipping man. Loading loose cargo then cost about $5.83 a ton by hand. McLean put his own cost for the boxes at about $0.16. Both figures come from Marc Levinson's *The Box*, as I remember it. Before containers, a ship could spend as much time in port as at sea, with crews of longshoremen moving sacks and crates one at a time. Theft on the docks was so common it was almost priced in.

**The twist:** the box itself wasn't the breakthrough. The standard was. For about a decade, McLean's 35-ft boxes competed with other lines' 24-ft and other sizes, none of which fit each other's ships, cranes or chassis. The big gains came only after ISO fixed the dimensions in the late 1960s, which gave the 20-ft TEU you now see in freight data. After that, a box could move from ship to rail to truck without anyone opening it. A 2016 study by Bernhofen, El-Sahli and Kneller estimated that containerization raised trade among developed countries more than two decades of trade agreements did. Cheaper handling came from the hardware. The global market came from agreeing on the interface.
