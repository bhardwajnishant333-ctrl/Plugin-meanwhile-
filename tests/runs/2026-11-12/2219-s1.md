The blue in Hokusai's *Great Wave* came from a lab accident in Berlin.

Around 1706, a colormaker named Diesbach was trying to make a red. Contaminated potash turned his batch deep blue instead, and the result was Prussian blue, one of the first modern synthetic pigments. It reached Japan as *bero-ai*, "Berlin indigo," but for decades it was too expensive to use widely. Then around 1829–30, cheap Prussian blue made in China began arriving in quantity. Publishers jumped on it, and prints in mostly blue (*aizuri-e*) became a short-lived craze. Van Gogh copied a Hiroshige print, then framed it with Japanese characters he couldn't read.

In 1887 in Paris he painted *Flowering Plum Orchard (after Hiroshige)*, an oil copy of *Plum Park in Kameido* from Hiroshige's *One Hundred Famous Views of Edo* (1857). He made a second copy the same year, *Bridge in the Rain*, after *Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge*. Three changes stand out:

- **Color:** he pushed it much hotter than the original, setting greens against reds deliberately.
- **Border:** he added a wide painted border with kanji copied from other prints. As far as I know, they have nothing to do with the image. To him they were pattern, not text.
- **Volume:** he and his brother Theo bought hundreds of cheap prints, largely from the dealer Siegfried Bing. In July 1888 he wrote to Theo: "All my work is based to some extent on Japanese art." He went to Arles partly hoping the south of France would feel like the Japan he had imagined.

The twist: much of what he copied as a way of seeing was really the grammar of the medium. Flat color areas, hard outlines and bold silhouettes are what you get when every color is its own carved block, pressed by hand with a *baren*. Europe took the limits of a production process for a philosophy and built Post-Impressionism partly on that misreading. The blues in those prints were often Prussian blue too, so Europe sent Japan the pigment and got it back as a style.

For you as a carver: the "badly" in your blocks belongs to the same grammar. The constraints of the cut are a big part of what people see as the style.
