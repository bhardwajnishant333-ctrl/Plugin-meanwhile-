**Your new job queue depends on one SQL clause that Postgres only got in 2016.**

Solid Queue keeps jobs in ordinary database tables. Each worker claims work with roughly this:

`SELECT ... FROM solid_queue_ready_executions ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`

`SKIP LOCKED` tells the database to pass over any rows another worker has already locked instead of waiting for them. Postgres added it in 9.5 (January 2016) and MySQL in 8.0 (2018). Before that, a queue in the database meant workers stacked up behind each other's locks, or you needed advisory-lock workarounds like the `que` gem used. That's a big part of why "don't use your DB as a queue" was standard advice and Redis plus Sidekiq took over. On SQLite there's no `SKIP LOCKED` at all. It still works because SQLite only allows one writer at a time anyway.

**The catch:** the best thing about a database queue is that a job can be committed in the same transaction as the data it needs. That ends the classic Sidekiq bug where a job enqueued in `after_save` runs before the commit and gets `RecordNotFound`. But the Rails 8 generator puts Solid Queue in its own `queue` database in `database.yml`. Two databases means two transactions, so that guarantee quietly goes away. When the upgrade lands, check which setup you got. If it's the separate database, check whether `enqueue_after_transaction_commit` (added in Rails 7.2) is covering you. That bug class only stays fixed if one of those two is true.

**Most of a Fontainebleau hold's strength is in a thin outer skin, and that skin doesn't grow back.**

Bleau sandstone started as Oligocene sand, the Stampian sands, laid down roughly 30 million years ago. Only some of it turned into rock: silica-rich groundwater cemented the grains together in lenses and banks, and the rest stayed loose. That's why the boulders sit on soft sand. The cementing also wasn't even. Over time, water moving through the rock and evaporating at the surface left a harder crust on many blocks, with weaker sandstone underneath.

Water makes it worse. Wet sandstone is measurably weaker than dry sandstone, and lab tests usually show a large drop in strength. The exact figure depends on the rock, so I won't give one. After rain, a crimp that has held for decades can snap off under one hard pull. Once the crust is gone, the softer stone underneath wears away quickly from shoes and brushing, and the hold is lost for good. The crust took geological time to form, so nobody will ever see it come back.

That's where the local rules come from. Wait a day or two after rain, especially in shaded, mossy areas. Use soft brushes, never wire. Be wary of "pof", the traditional pine resin in a cloth: it improves grip in the short term, but over years it leaves holds glassy and polished. This is also why Font has a "conditions" culture. Cold, dry, windy days give the best friction, and damp days cost you both grip and holds.

**Some of the oldest stone art on Earth is being slowly eaten by something you could mistake for a paint stain.**

A lichen isn't one organism. It's a fungus living with an alga or a cyanobacterium, and it works on rock in two ways:

- **Physically:** its threads, called hyphae, grow into tiny cracks. Each time the rock gets wet and dries out, the lichen swells and shrinks and works the grains loose.
- **Chemically:** many lichens give off oxalic acid and "lichen acids" that pull minerals out of the stone. On limestone and other calcium-rich rock this leaves crusts of calcium oxalate, so the surface is literally being rebuilt into something else.

Lichens grow extremely slowly. *Rhizocarpon geographicum*, the yellow-green map lichen, can take a century to grow a few millimetres. In the 1950s Roland Beschel turned that into a dating method called lichenometry: measure the biggest lichen on a glacial moraine and estimate how long the rock has been exposed. A lichen on a carved surface may well be older than anyone looking after the site.

That's why conservators of stone sites worry about it. The moai on Rapa Nui are one well-known case where lichen and other growth are part of the ongoing conservation work.

**The twist:** scientists still disagree about whether lichen harms or protects stone. Some studies find that a lichen crust shields the surface from rain, wind and temperature swings better than bare rock does. Scraping it off can also take carved detail with it, since the lichen is holding the grains it has already loosened. So the debate isn't only "remove the pest". It's whether a 300-year-old tenant now makes up part of the monument. Which one is true depends on the rock, the species and the climate, so it's decided site by site.

**Meanwhile:**
**Lichenometry dates a glacier's retreat from one number: the size of the single biggest lichen on the rock. That's both why it works and why it breaks.**

**How it works:**
1. **Calibrate.** Find surfaces with known ages: gravestones, dated buildings, bridges, rockfalls on record. On each one, measure the largest *Rhizocarpon* thallus. Plot the size against age to get a growth curve.
2. **Apply.** Go to an undated moraine and find the biggest lichen on it. Read its age off the curve, then add an allowance for the colonisation lag, the years before the first lichen settles on fresh rock.
3. **Read the landscape.** Each ridge of a retreating glacier gets a date. For the last few centuries, including the Little Ice Age advances in the Alps and Scandinavia, this was a cheap, fast way to get dates, and a lot of glacier history was built on it.

**Where it goes wrong:**
- **Growth isn't linear.** Young lichens grow quickly at first and then slow down, and the shape of that curve changes with climate. A curve calibrated in a valley's graveyard may not fit a cold, windy moraine 800 m higher up.
- **Identifying the species is hard.** The yellow-green *Rhizocarpon* group contains several look-alike species that grow at different rates. Many studies record "Rhizocarpon" without separating them.
- **Neighbours merge.** Two thalli that grow together look like one big, old lichen.
- **Inherited lichen.** A boulder that already had lichen on it, from rockfall or an older surface, can end up on a young moraine and make it look centuries older.
- **Sampling.** Search a bigger area and you'll find a bigger lichen, so the "age" partly reflects how hard you looked.

These problems are serious enough that a 2015 review in *Quaternary Research* questioned whether many lichenometric dates hold up at all. For older or disputed surfaces, researchers now often use cosmogenic nuclide dating instead: measuring beryllium-10 that builds up in quartz while it's exposed to cosmic rays. I'm confident about that broad shift; I'm less sure how fully it has replaced lichenometry everywhere.

**The twist:** lichenometry is an estimate built on the *maximum*. With a max, one bad data point isn't averaged away. It is the answer. Your p99 latency dashboard has the same weakness: one stray outlier, like a lichen that came with the boulder, and the whole conclusion moves.
