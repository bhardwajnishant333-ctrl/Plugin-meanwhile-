**Electricity breaks the formula every bond trader uses to link today's price to tomorrow's.**

For anything you can store, like gold, oil or Treasuries, cost-of-carry ties the two together: forward ≈ spot × e^{(r + storage − convenience yield)·T}. If the forward drifts too far, you buy spot, hold it and sell forward. Grid-scale power is still too expensive to store at that scale, so that trade mostly doesn't exist. Nothing anchors tomorrow's price to today's. A day-ahead price is just the market's forecast of tomorrow's load, plus a premium for fear of being wrong.

The premium is where it gets interesting. Bessembinder and Lemmon (*Journal of Finance*, 2002) showed that the forward premium doesn't come from average demand. It comes from the shape of demand. The supply curve is convex: past a certain load, the last peaker plants make prices shoot up. So retailers who must deliver power at fixed rates fear the right tail much more than the left. When demand is volatile and right-skewed, as on hot summer days, they overpay for forwards to protect themselves, and the premium turns positive. When demand is calm, it can go negative.

**The twist:** the market doesn't care about your RMSE. It treats a 5% under-forecast on a heat-wave peak and a 5% over-forecast on a mild Tuesday as completely different mistakes, because one can cost far more than the other. TFT already outputs quantiles, so the part of your model worth watching is probably the gap between your P50 and P90 on the hottest days, not that flat loss curve. In bond terms, the day-ahead price is a yield with the term premium built in, and the term premium is set by the tail.

**The timeline:** On Feb 15, 2021, with rotating outages under way, the Texas regulator (PUCT) ordered ERCOT to set prices at the $9,000/MWh cap while it was cutting customers off. Its reasoning was that the market was short, but the prices weren't showing it, because demand cut off by force never gets bid into the price. ERCOT ended the last outages just before midnight on Feb 17. It kept prices pinned at $9,000 until 9:00 AM on Feb 19. That's about 32–33 hours, depending on where you start counting. The "32 hours" comes from ERCOT's own independent market monitor, Potomac Economics. In early March they said holding the cap after the outages ended was a mistake and put the overcharge at about $16 billion.

**What happened next:** The PUCT declined to change the prices after the fact. Its argument was that reopening settled trades would cause more harm than it fixed. The chair resigned within weeks. The legislature argued about forcing a repricing and didn't. Later that year the cap was cut from $9,000 to $5,000. So $16 billion of wrong prices stayed on the books, because unwinding them was seen as riskier than keeping them.

**The twist, for your model:** your load history from those days is censored. At the worst point about 20 GW of demand was cut, so what got recorded is the load that was served, not the load people actually wanted. A forecaster trained on it learns that extreme cold brings a smaller peak than it really does, which is exactly the tail that matters. Any blackout or demand-response event in your training window does the same thing on a smaller scale. Censored targets look like normal data unless you flag them.

**For seven years the most important bond yield in Japan was a number the central bank picked, so the market's information had to come out somewhere else.**

In September 2016 the Bank of Japan began yield curve control. It promised to hold the 10-year government bond (JGB) yield near 0% by buying whatever it took. At first the band was unofficial, about ±0.1%. It was widened to ±0.2% in 2018 and ±0.25% in 2021. Then in December 2022 it was suddenly doubled to ±0.5%. In 2023 the 1% ceiling became a soft "reference," and the policy ended in March 2024, along with negative interest rates.

**Where it broke:** by 2022 the rest of the world was raising rates fast, and the market wanted Japan's 10-year yield higher. The BOJ kept absorbing the selling until it owned more than half of all JGBs. Three things followed:
- **A kink in the curve.** The 10-year yield sat *below* the 8- and 9-year yields next to it. On a normal curve that can't happen, but it does when one maturity is defended and its neighbors aren't.
- **A benchmark that stopped trading.** In October 2022 the current 10-year issue went several days in a row without a single trade on the main interdealer market. That was reported as a record, though I'm less sure of the exact count of days.
- **Pressure moved to the yen.** The yield couldn't adjust, so the exchange rate did. The yen fell to about 151 per dollar in October 2022, and the Ministry of Finance stepped in to support it.

**The twist:** pinning a price doesn't remove the signal. It moves the signal to whatever isn't pinned. Bond traders stopped looking at the 10-year cash yield. They read swaps, futures, the 9-year and the yen instead, because the target number itself carried no information. Day-ahead power markets do the same thing at the price cap. When a target sits on an administered limit, the useful signal is in the neighboring variables, and the hour the limit is removed is a regime break, not just another data point.
