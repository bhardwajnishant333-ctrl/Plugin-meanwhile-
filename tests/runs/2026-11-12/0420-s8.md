**The guy who broke the hour record in 1993 built his bike himself, using bearings from a washing machine.**

Graeme Obree was a Scottish amateur with a bike shop that went under and almost no money. His bike, "Old Faithful," had a narrow bottom bracket built around those bearings so his knees could come in close, plus handlebars he gripped with his hands tucked under his chest. On 16 July 1993 in Hamar, Norway, he failed his first attempt on Moser's 51.151 km. He booked the track again for the next morning, rode 51.596 km and took the record. Chris Boardman beat it a week later in Bordeaux. In August 1993, back at Hamar, Obree won the world individual pursuit title. In April 1994 in Bordeaux he took the hour record back with 52.713 km.

Then the UCI started making rules. They banned his tuck in 1994. Obree simply invented the "Superman" position, arms straight out in front, and won the pursuit worlds again in 1995. Then they banned that too. The 1996 Lugano Charter rules, including a fixed minimum saddle setback, are largely why today's track bikes look the way they do.

**The twist:** most cycling rules regulate the machine, but these regulated the rider's body. That makes sense once you know the rider, not the bike, causes most of the air resistance. The cheapest bike in the sport was beating the expensive ones on body position alone, so the UCI wrote rules about how you sit on it.

**At hour-record speed, cutting your drag by 10% is worth about as much as 10% more power. One costs nothing and the other takes years of training.**

Here's why. On a velodrome at around 50 km/h, almost all your power goes into pushing air out of the way. Tyre friction on a smooth board track is small by comparison. The air part of the power works out to roughly ½ × air density × CdA × speed³. CdA is your drag area, meaning your frontal area times how slippery your shape is. Because of that cube, speed only rises with the cube root of power divided by CdA. So a 10% cut in CdA gives about 3.5% more speed, roughly 1.7 km more in an hour at 50 km/h. That's enough to turn a good ride into a world record.

I don't have trustworthy measured numbers for Obree's tuck or the Superman position specifically, and I'd rather not make one up. The mechanism is clear, though. Both positions put the hips well forward over the cranks, so the back can go flat while the angle between torso and thigh stays open enough to push hard.

The UCI rules attack exactly that:
- **Saddle setback:** the nose of the saddle must sit at least 5 cm behind the bottom bracket. That pushes the hips back, so a flat back now means a cramped hip angle and lost power.
- **Reach limit:** the extensions can't go further than 75 cm in front of the bottom bracket (a little more is allowed for very tall riders). That one rules out the Superman position on its own.

**The twist:** under the cube law, a rule about how you sit works as a cap on your power. Modern hour riders spend so much time on position testing because the real contest is finding the flattest position that still passes the UCI's measuring jig.
