**Meanwhile:**
**Your `--link` upgrade can carry over indexes that are wrong, and Postgres won't tell you.**

`pg_upgrade --link` doesn't rebuild indexes. It hard-links the old index files into the new cluster. A text B-tree index is just keys stored in sorted order, and for most locales Postgres takes that order from the OS C library (glibc), not from Postgres itself. So if your PG 17 container image ships a different glibc than your PG 14 image did, your text indexes are now sorted by rules the new server no longer uses.

The big example is glibc 2.28 (August 2018). It rewrote its collation data, and a lot of strings changed order. The PostgreSQL wiki's classic check is `SELECT '1-1' < '11';`, which gives a different answer on either side of that release. Later glibc versions have changed less, but I wouldn't assume nothing changed. If CloudNativePG moved your base image (Debian bullseye to bookworm, for example), check the glibc versions rather than guessing.

When the order is wrong, nothing errors. An index lookup can miss a row that a full table scan would find. A unique index can accept a duplicate because the search for the existing key looks in the wrong place.

**The twist:** in logistics, the keys most at risk are the ones you rely on most. Tracking numbers, SKUs and dock codes are full of hyphens, and punctuation is exactly where these sort rules changed. Your Go service won't see an error, just a shipment that occasionally "doesn't exist."

Cheap ways to protect yourself:
- Run `amcheck` (`bt_index_check`) on your text indexes, or make sure they're on the reindex list.
- PG 15+ tracks collation versions per database and warns when they don't match, so watch the logs.
- PG 17 adds a `builtin` collation provider (`C.UTF-8`) that doesn't depend on glibc at all.

**The emperor Psellos tutored was nicknamed after a price hike.**

Michael VII Doukas (r. 1071–78) was called *Parapinakes*, "minus a quarter." One gold nomisma used to buy a full measure (medimnos) of wheat; under him it bought a measure minus a quarter (a *pinakion*). The man Psellos educated went down in history as a currency failure.

How it happened:
- **The standard:** Constantine I's solidus (about 309), struck 72 to the Roman pound, roughly 4.5 g of nearly pure gold. It held for about 700 years, and Robert Lopez called it "the dollar of the Middle Ages."
- **The first quiet change:** Nikephoros II Phokas (963–69) issued the *tetarteron*, a lighter gold coin alongside the full-weight *histamenon*. The chroniclers (Zonaras, for one) accuse him of collecting taxes in the heavy coin and paying out in the light one. I'd treat that as a hostile source, but the two weights are real.
- **The slide:** in Psellos's own lifetime, debasement starts around Michael IV, picks up under Constantine IX Monomachos (roughly 18 carats is the figure usually cited), and collapses under Nikephoros III (single digits). By the 1080s some "gold" coins were barely gold. My numbers here are approximate, from memory of Grierson-style tables.
- **The fix:** Alexios I's reform in 1092 introduced the *hyperpyron* (about 20.5 carats). He also issued electrum and billon coins as officially separate denominations with set exchange rates, instead of pretending they were all one coin.

**The twist:** a gold coin's alloy is invisible. You can't tell 18 carats from 24 by looking, only with a touchstone. That's why debasement could go on for decades. People kept trusting the coin while its contents changed, much like a B-tree index that keeps answering queries after glibc changed its sort order. And Alexios's fix is the same move Postgres 15 made: stop pretending the standard never changes, and record which version you're on.

One honest gap: I can't tell you with confidence how much Psellos says about the coinage in the *Chronographia*. He complains loudly about Monomachos's spending, but I don't recall him naming debasement directly. If you've read all of it, you know better than I do.

**Meanwhile:**
**Greek fire may have been lost precisely because the Byzantines protected it so well.**

What's known:
- **Where it came from:** Theophanes says a refugee named Kallinikos, from Heliopolis in Syria, brought it to Constantinople around 672. It was used against the Arab sieges of the city (674–78 and 717–18).
- **How it worked:** what made it special was the delivery, not just the fuel. It came out of a *siphōn*, a pressurized pump with a nozzle, mounted on the bow of a dromon and spraying burning liquid. It kept burning on water. The fuel was most likely a petroleum product, possibly crude from around the Black Sea, with additives. The exact recipe is still unknown, and every reconstruction is a guess.
- **The security model:** about 950, Constantine VII wrote in the *De Administrando Imperio* that his son must never give it to foreigners. He said an angel had revealed it to Constantine the Great, so handing it over was sacrilege. That's an invented divine origin used as a security measure. Historians also argue (inference, not documented) that the knowledge was split up: one group knew the mixture, another knew how to build and run the siphons, and no single person could leak all of it.
- **The loss:** by the 13th century it no longer shows up as a decisive weapon. When the Fourth Crusade took the city in 1204, it didn't save anyone. Later sources describe incendiaries, but it's unclear whether they were the same thing. Nobody records it being lost. It just stops appearing.

**The twist:** splitting the secret protected it against spies, but it made it fragile against everything else. If knowledge lives only in a few guarded heads and is deliberately never written down, it survives only as long as the institution behind it stays healthy. After Manzikert and the civil wars of the 1070s and '80s, it didn't. It's a single point of failure: a scheme that defends well against people trying to steal it, and very badly against losing the people who hold it.
