**In 1886, a mathematician proved that a peal your band might easily try can never be done.**

Grandsire Triples has the same 5,040 rows to cover. The puzzle was to ring all of them using only plain leads and bobs, with no singles. Ringers tried for well over a century. Then William Henry Thompson published a proof that it can't be done. Every combination of plain leads and bobs falls short of covering all 5,040 rows. That's why the famous Holt's Original (1751) uses exactly two singles. They aren't there for style. Holt needed them to make the extent possible at all. In 1948 Rankin restated the proof in group theory, and it became a classic example of mathematics deciding a practical question.

Only a structural argument closes the question. Your tests on a DAG work the same way: they can show the bad case doesn't happen in the runs you tried, but only a structural guarantee, like a uniqueness constraint, shows it can't happen.

What I can say for certain is how the danger worked, and you'll know it from your own tower:

**A bell at rest is upside down, balanced at the top of its swing, and only a wooden stay stops it going further.**

Change ringing depends on swinging each bell a full circle and stopping it mouth-up, just past the balance point. If the stay breaks, or a ringer pulls too hard and the bell goes over, the full weight of the bell suddenly drives the rope. A ringer who keeps hold of the tail end, or gets a loop of rope round a wrist or neck, can be lifted off the floor or thrown. That's why learners are drilled to **let go** when a bell goes over, and why the tail end is never wrapped around a hand.

**The twist:** full-circle ringing is what makes change ringing possible at all. Only a bell that pauses at the balance can be held back or let go a fraction early, which is how bells change places from one row to the next. So the thing that makes the art work is also its main danger.

**Near the balance point, a pendulum's swing time goes to infinity. Change ringing is built on that.**

A bell that only swings partway works like an ordinary pendulum. Its timing is set by its size and how it hangs, so you can't speed it up or slow it down by much. A bell rung full circle is different. Each stroke carries it up to near the inverted balance, and that's an unstable equilibrium. As a pendulum's swing approaches 180°, the time it spends near the top grows without limit. The closer it gets to balance, the longer it lingers there. Mathematically, the period stretches logarithmically as the bell's energy approaches exactly what it takes to reach the balance.

So a slightly stronger pull takes the bell closer to balance and it strikes noticeably later. A slightly lighter pull, or checking it, brings it back sooner. A tiny change in effort on the rope becomes a big, controllable change in timing. That's what lets you hold up to move a place later, or cut in to move a place earlier, which is how bells swap places in each change. Swing-chimed bells can't do it, which is why change ringing grew up only where bells were hung for full-circle ringing.

**The twist:** the control comes from working right next to instability. Aircraft designers use the same idea. The F-16 was deliberately built with relaxed static stability, so it's unstable and kept in check by fly-by-wire, because a machine near its tipping point responds to small inputs far more sharply. The cost is the same in both cases: let control slip, and the bell goes over.

2. Sideways: other systems that get their precision from working near an unstable point, such as Segways, rockets and markets
