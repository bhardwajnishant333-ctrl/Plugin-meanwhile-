**Meanwhile:**
Your Core Data store probably isn't one file. If your agent moves only the `.sqlite`, you can lose data without any error.

**How it works:** Since iOS 7, Core Data's SQLite stores have used write-ahead logging (WAL) by default. Apple documented the change in Technical Q&A QA1809. New writes don't go into `Model.sqlite` straight away. They're appended to `Model.sqlite-wal`, and a `Model.sqlite-shm` file holds the index into that log. SQLite copies the log back into the main file at "checkpoints", by default once the WAL reaches about 1,000 pages. Until a checkpoint runs, your user's most recent changes exist only in the `-wal` file.

So if you copy, back up or relocate the store with plain `FileManager` calls and leave the sidecar files behind, you get a database that opens fine and is quietly missing data. Nothing crashes, and it looks like stale data.

**The twist:** SwiftData doesn't remove this risk because it's built on Core Data, and its default store is still SQLite with WAL. If your agent changes the store path (say, `ModelConfiguration(url:)` pointing somewhere new) or "cleans up" old files, ask:
- Did it move all three files, or use `NSPersistentStoreCoordinator.migratePersistentStore` / `replacePersistentStore`, which handle the sidecars?
- Did it delete an old `-wal` it thought was junk?

Most users will never be affected. The ones who are lose their most recent changes, which are the ones they care about most.

The best weather for running a marathon would feel miserable if you were standing still.

**Why it works that way:** Muscles turn only about 20 to 25% of the energy they burn into movement. Everything else becomes heat. At marathon pace you're producing heat for two to four hours, and you lose it mostly through sweat and blood flowing near the skin. Blood sent to the skin to cool you can't go to your legs. Your heart rate creeps up while your pace stays the same, and your body ends up slowing you down to keep your core temperature safe.

Ely and colleagues published a large analysis of marathon results against weather in 2007 (Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise). Finishing times got steadily slower as temperatures rose above the cool range. My memory is that the best conditions for elites were around 5 to 10°C, but treat the exact range as approximate. Slower runners lost more time than elites as it warmed up, because they're out in the heat longer. So the hot-day penalty is a percentage, and a 3:30 runner loses more minutes than a 2:10 runner.

**The twist:** Colder isn't automatically better. Boston 2018 had near-freezing rain and headwinds. Des Linden won in 2:39:54, the slowest women's winning time there in about four decades, and many elites dropped out. Once you can't stay warm, being wet and cold hurts you as much as heat does. Your feel for "nice weather" was set while you were standing still, which is the wrong reference for race day.
