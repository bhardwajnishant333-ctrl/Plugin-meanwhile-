**User:** fine. then a crazy stat about mushrooms — exactly how many spores does a single morel release in its lifetime?

**Meanwhile:**
I can't give you an exact number for this one either. I don't know of any published lifetime spore count for a morel, and any precise figure would be invented.

Here's what is solid:

- **How a morel shoots its spores:** Morels are ascomycetes. Their spores grow eight at a time in tiny pressurized sacs called asci, which line the pits of the cap. Each sac bursts open and fires its spores out like a cannon. Foragers sometimes see this as a visible "smoke" puffing off a morel when it gets disturbed or warmed.
- **The famous big number belongs to a puffball.** Around 1909, A.H.R. Buller estimated that a large giant puffball (*Calvatia gigantea*) holds about 7 trillion spores. That figure gets quoted everywhere, but it's one old estimate, not a measurement.

**The twist:** Spore-shooting fungi can make their own wind. In a 2010 PNAS paper, Roper and colleagues showed that when thousands of asci fire together, they move the surrounding air. That small airflow carries the spores up out of the still layer of air right next to the ground, where on their own they'd travel only millimetres. That study looked at *Sclerotinia* and similar cup fungi, not morels specifically. It's a reasonable guess that the morel "smoke" works the same way, but nobody has shown it.

So the count isn't the impressive part. Firing in sync is how they get the spores off the ground at all.

**Meanwhile:**
A system can stay down even after whatever broke it is gone.

**How it happens:** Researchers call these "metastable failures," from a 2021 HotOS paper by Bronson et al. Here's the sequence:
- Something brief happens: a slow deploy, a network blip, a cold cache.
- Latency rises, clients time out, and they retry.
- The retries are extra load, so latency rises further and you get more timeouts and more retries.
- The original trigger goes away, but the system stays down because its own retry traffic is now what keeps it overloaded.

The classic example is the AWS EBS outage in us-east-1 in April 2011. A network change sent traffic the wrong way, and EBS nodes lost contact with their replicas. All of them then went looking for new space to copy data to at the same moment. AWS called it a "re-mirroring storm," and it kept the cluster stuck long after the network was fixed.

**The twist:** Adding capacity often doesn't fix it, because new capacity gets swallowed by the same retry traffic. What works is cutting work: shedding load, adding jitter, and capping retries. gRPC has a built-in retry cap. Its service config has a `retryThrottling` setting (`maxTokens`, `tokenRatio`) that stops a client from retrying once too many of its calls are failing. Worth checking whether your regenerated Go clients actually turn it on.
