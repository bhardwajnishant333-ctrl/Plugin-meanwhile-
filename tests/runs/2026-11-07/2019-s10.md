**A good iceboat can go five times faster than the wind pushing it, and the sailor feels a headwind the whole way.**

How it works: you already know about apparent wind. Once a boat moves, the wind its own speed creates adds to the true wind, so the wind you feel shifts forward. On most boats the hull drags in the water, which keeps the gain small. An iceboat sits on steel runners on ice, with almost no drag. As it speeds up on a reach, the apparent wind keeps moving forward and getting stronger. The sail is then working like an aircraft wing in a headwind, not like a parachute being pushed. Speed builds until drag finally balances the drive. DN-class iceboats regularly reach around five times the true wind speed. Foiling America's Cup boats do something similar by lifting the hull out of the water, and they can sail at roughly two to three times the wind speed.

The twist: these boats are fastest *downwind* by not pointing straight downwind. They sail angled reaches and gybe between them, and their speed toward the finish still beats the wind. One strange land yacht, Blackbird, used a propeller instead of a sail and went *directly* downwind faster than the wind in 2010: about 2.8 times the wind speed, by the reported figure. Many physicists insisted that was impossible until it was measured.

On your keelboat this is why a beam reach usually feels faster than a run, even though the run seems like the "push" point of sail.

**Blackbird worked for the same reason your keelboat does: it sits between two things moving at different speeds and takes energy from the difference.**

How it works: the cart's wheels are geared to a big propeller, and the propeller blows air *backward*. People who expect this to fail picture it this way: once the cart matches the wind, it's in dead calm air, so there's nothing left to push on. But the ground isn't calm. Relative to the cart, the ground is still rushing past.

The trick comes down to power = force × speed:
- The wheels take power from the ground, which moves past the cart quickly. They give a small braking force at high speed.
- The propeller spends that power on the air, which moves past the cart slowly or not at all. The same power turns into a large force at low speed.

So the propeller's forward push is bigger than the drag from the wheels, and the cart keeps speeding up past the wind. Friction and propeller losses are what eventually stop it. Your keelboat does the same job with a keel in the water and a sail in the air. Blackbird swaps the keel for wheels and the sail for a propeller.

History: an engineer named Andrew Bauer built a small version in 1969, and it was largely forgotten. In 2010 Rick Cavallaro and John Borton ran Blackbird at El Mirage dry lake in California, at about 2.8 times the wind speed. Later they reconfigured it and drove directly *upwind* faster than the wind as well, at roughly twice wind speed if I remember right. The argument blew up again in 2021, after a popular science video. A UCLA physicist bet $10,000 that it was impossible, then conceded and paid.

The twist: experts didn't get the physics wrong. They pictured the wrong frame. Watch it from the air's point of view and it looks impossible. Watch it from the ground's point of view and it looks obvious.
