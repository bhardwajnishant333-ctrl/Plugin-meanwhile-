**The glaze on your mug can make the mug stronger or weaker, depending on whether it's being squeezed or stretched.**

Fired clay and glass are much stronger when squeezed than when pulled apart. Cracks start under tension. When a glazed mug cools in the kiln, the clay and the glaze both shrink, but by different amounts. How much each one shrinks is called its thermal expansion.

- If the **glaze shrinks more** than the clay, it ends up stretched over the mug like a drum skin. It fails as a web of fine cracks: **crazing**.
- If the **glaze shrinks a bit less**, the clay pulls it tight and the glaze is squeezed. A squeezed glaze actually strengthens the pot, because a crack has to beat that squeeze before it can open.
- If the glaze is squeezed **too hard**, it pops off at the edges in flakes. Potters call this **shivering**.

Good "glaze fit" means aiming for a little squeeze.

**The twist:** your phone screen works the same way. Gorilla Glass is soaked in a hot bath of potassium salt. The large potassium atoms push into the glass surface and take the place of smaller sodium atoms. That packs the surface so tight it's permanently squeezed. Toughened glass in car side windows does the same thing with heat instead of chemistry. So a well-fitted mug glaze and a scratch-tough phone screen run on one idea: build squeeze into the surface so cracks can't get started.

1) **Deeper:** why crazing matters more on a mug than a vase (and when potters crackle a glaze on purpose)

3) **Wildcard:** why most houseplants that die are overwatered, and what that says about designing a plant-care app's reminders

**You can hit the head of a Prince Rupert's drop with a hammer and it survives. Snip its thin tail with pliers and the whole thing turns to powder in a fraction of a second.**

To make one, you drip molten glass into cold water. That gives a tadpole shape: a round head and a long thin tail. The outside hits the water and goes solid at once. The inside is still molten, and as it cools it tries to shrink. But it's already stuck to the rigid shell, so it pulls that shell inward. The result is a hard skin that's squeezed very tightly (the effect from your mug glaze, turned up enormously) wrapped around a core that's stretched and can't relax. A 2017 study measured the squeeze at the surface at roughly 700 megapascals, about 7,000 times the air pressure you're sitting in. A crack in the head can't push through that.

The tail is so thin that its squeezed skin is barely there. Break it, and a crack slips into the stretched core, which is full of stored energy and ready to let go. The crack runs through the whole drop at roughly 1.5 km/s, more than four times the speed of sound in air. That figure comes from high-speed camera work in the 1990s.

They're named after Prince Rupert of the Rhine, who brought some to King Charles II in England around 1660. The newly founded Royal Society tried to explain them, and Robert Hooke wrote about them in *Micrographia* (1665). Getting from his good guess to a full explanation took centuries.

**The twist:** the drop's strength and its fragility are the same thing. The squeezed skin is only possible because the core is under strain, so the drop is tough everywhere except where the skin runs out. Toughened glass in car windows and shower doors works the same way. That's why it has to be cut to shape *before* it's toughened. Cut it afterwards and the whole sheet bursts into little cubes. Anything strengthened by built-in stress has a tail somewhere. The craft is knowing where it is.

1) **Deeper:** why toughened glass breaks into blunt cubes and not into knives (a safety rule that came from car crashes)
2) **Sideways:** back to your wheel: why a mug with thick and thin spots can crack in the kiln, the same stress problem in clay

**The Bologna bottle survived being pounded on a table, then shattered because a tiny chip of flint was dropped inside it.**

It was an 18th-century science-show favourite: a small, thick-walled glass flask, cooled quickly on purpose instead of slowly. That's the Prince Rupert's drop trick again, this time shaped like a bottle. The outer skin set first and ended up squeezed, while the inner layers stayed strained. From the outside it was surprisingly tough. You could knock it about and it held.

The party trick was to drop something small inside. The usual accounts say a soft, rounded object did nothing. A sharp, hard fragment (flint is the one usually named) scratched the inner surface, and the bottle burst. I'm confident about the basic demonstration. I'm less sure exactly which objects each demonstrator used, because the old descriptions vary.

**The twist:** the pebble didn't break the bottle. It barely touched it. All the energy that blew the glass apart was already stored inside, locked in when the bottle was cooled fast. The scratch only opened a path for it. When something fails dramatically from a tiny cause, the tiny cause isn't the explanation. The question is what was storing all that energy. That holds for glass, for a stressed bridge, and for a team that "suddenly" falls apart over one small comment.

1) **Deeper:** why a scratch, not force, is what kills glass. In 1920 an engineer named Griffith showed that real glass is far weaker than it should be, all because of microscopic flaws
2) **Sideways:** back to your app: why overwatering kills more houseplants than forgetting, and what that means for a "time to water!" reminder
3) **Wildcard:** the 1919 Boston molasses flood, when a giant storage tank burst and sent a wave of syrup through the streets
