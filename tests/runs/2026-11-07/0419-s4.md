**Meanwhile:**
**On option expiration days, stocks close on strike prices more often than chance allows. Stocks with no listed options don't.**

**Mechanism.** Ni, Pearson and Poteshman (Journal of Financial Economics, 2005) found this clustering at strikes on expiration Fridays, and only in optionable stocks. Part of the cause is delta hedging. Say a market maker is long a call struck at K right before expiry. Near K, the delta swings sharply between about 0 and about 1. If the price ticks above K, the hedge says sell stock. If it dips below, the hedge says buy. Long-gamma hedging means buying dips and selling rips, and that drags the price toward K. The paper also found evidence pointing to the other side: traders who were short options may have pushed prices toward strikes so those options expired worthless. I'm less sure how much of the effect each cause explains.

**Twist.** The hedging rule is the same in both cases, but the effect on price depends on the sign of the dealers' net gamma. Long gamma means negative feedback, and the market gets pinned and calm. Short gamma means positive feedback: the hedge buys rallies and sells drops, so moves get bigger. Same controller, opposite sign on the loop. That's why "who holds the gamma" is one of the most argued-over questions about 0DTE options today.

1) **Deeper:** does dealer gamma from 0DTE options actually move the S&P? (Still an open fight, with evidence on both sides.)
2) **Sideways:** why your FSDP loss curve can change when you change GPU count: floating-point addition isn't associative, so reduce order changes the bits
3) **Wildcard:** the quantum Zeno effect: watch an atom often enough and it can't change state

**Meanwhile:**
**(a + b) + c ≠ a + (b + c) on a GPU. In fp32, (1e20 − 1e20) + 1 = 1, but 1e20 + (−1e20 + 1) = 0.**

**Mechanism.** Every float add rounds the result. Change the order of the adds and the rounding errors change too. NCCL's reduce order depends on world size: which ranks sum which chunks in a ring, and when, is different at 4 GPUs than at 8. So your gradients differ in the last few bits from step 1. bf16 makes this worse because it keeps only 8 significant bits, so each rounding step is bigger. Some differences also show up even on the same GPU count: cuBLAS split-K matmuls and kernels that use atomicAdd in the backward pass (embedding backward, scatter and index_add) can sum in a different order on every run.

**Twist.** Training amplifies tiny differences, so small bit errors grow into visibly different curves. Summers and Dinneen (ICML 2021) found that changing a single weight's initial value by the smallest possible amount gave about as much run-to-run variation as changing the whole random seed. So two curves pulling apart doesn't prove a bug. What matters is whether the 4-vs-8 gap is bigger than the gap between two seeds at the same GPU count.

**Quick check:** run 4 GPUs with two seeds. If your 4-vs-8 gap fits inside that seed-to-seed spread, it's rounding noise. If the gap is there from the first steps, or one setup is consistently worse, look for a real difference instead. The usual suspect is loss normalization: averaging the loss per rank and then across ranks is not the same as a global per-token mean when ranks have different token counts (padding, variable lengths). This is the same bug class as the gradient-accumulation loss bug that hit HF Transformers in Oct 2024. Also confirm the global batch size, and therefore the learning rate, really is the same in both setups.

1) **Deeper:** how to get bitwise-identical results across world sizes (fixed-order reductions, accumulating in fp32, and what each costs)

**Meanwhile:**
**Between 2005 and 2011 the typical ES–SPY arbitrage window shrank from about 97 ms to about 7 ms, but the profit per opportunity didn't fall. The race got faster and the prize stayed the same size.**

**Mechanism.** That's the core of Budish, Cramton and Shim, "The High-Frequency Trading Arms Race" (QJE, 2015). At one-minute intervals, E-mini S&P futures and SPY are almost perfectly correlated. At millisecond intervals the correlation nearly vanishes. The design flaw they point to is continuous time itself. When public news moves the price, someone has to be first to pick off the stale quotes ("sniping"), and liquidity providers price that risk into the spread. Getting faster doesn't remove that cost. It only decides who collects it. Their fix: uniform-price sealed-bid auctions held at discrete intervals (they discuss around 100 ms). Within one interval, being faster is worth nothing, so firms compete on price instead.

The follow-up, Aquilina, Budish and O'Neill (QJE, 2022), used London Stock Exchange message data. The numbers below are from memory; the order of magnitude is solid, the decimals less so. About 20% of FTSE 100 volume happened in races, often decided within microseconds. The "latency arbitrage tax" came to about 0.5 bp of volume, roughly $5B a year across global equities. Removing it would cut the cost of liquidity by about 17%.

**Twist.** The real world has gone partly the other way. Taiwan's exchange ran periodic call auctions (every few seconds) for decades and switched to continuous trading in March 2020. IEX went for a 350-microsecond speed bump, about 38 miles of coiled fiber, instead of batching. The idea is clean in theory, but adoption is a coordination problem: a single venue that batches risks losing order flow to the continuous venues around it.
