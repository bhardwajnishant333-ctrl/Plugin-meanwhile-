**Meanwhile:**
**In bf16, 256 + 1 = 256.**

The format splits its 16 bits like this:

| Format | Sign | Exponent | Mantissa | Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| bf16 | 1 | 8 | 7 | about 1e-38 to 3e38, same as fp32 |
| fp16 | 1 | 5 | 10 | max 65504, normal numbers only down to about 6e-5 |

Google Brain (the "b" in bfloat) made this trade because neural nets are robust to sloppy precision but fragile when numbers overflow or underflow. Gradients and activations span many orders of magnitude. In fp16, small gradients round to zero, which is why NVIDIA's 2017 mixed-precision recipe needed loss scaling. bf16 removes that problem by keeping fp32's exponent. Since it's just the top 16 bits of an fp32, converting is a cheap truncate-and-round.

There's a hardware reason too. Multiplier silicon grows roughly with the square of the significand width. bf16's 8 significand bits (7 stored plus the hidden 1) need a much smaller multiplier than fp16's 11 bits, and about 1/9 the size of fp32's 24. Treat that as a rule of thumb, not a die measurement. That freed area is how TPUs fit huge matrix-multiply arrays, starting with TPU v2 in 2017.

**The twist:** you get 7 mantissa bits, so near 1.0 the gap between neighbouring numbers is 2^-7, about 0.0078. Any weight update smaller than about half that gap rounds away to nothing. With pure bf16 parameters and a small learning rate late in a cosine decay, training can quietly stall with no NaN or warning. The loss just goes flat. That's why the standard setup is bf16 compute, fp32 accumulation (the TPU's matrix units already accumulate in fp32), and fp32 master weights and optimizer state, or stochastic rounding if you really want bf16 weights.

One JAX detail I'm fairly sure of, but check the docs for your version: on TPU, a matmul on fp32 inputs at default precision still runs as a bf16 pass. You need `precision=HIGHEST` or `jax.default_matmul_precision` to get real fp32.

1) **Deeper:** fp8 (E4M3 vs E5M2), where the range-versus-precision trade gets made separately for forward and backward passes
2) **Sideways:** Kahan summation, the 1965 trick that recovers lost low-order bits, and why it shows up in bf16 optimizers
3) **Wildcard:** the Patriot missile failure in 1991, where a 0.1-second clock counted in 24-bit fixed point drifted about 0.34 s over 100 hours

**Meanwhile:**
**The bug only killed because someone had half-fixed it.**

On 25 February 1991, a Patriot battery in Dhahran didn't engage an incoming Scud. It hit a US Army barracks and killed 28 soldiers. The 1992 GAO report (IMTEC-92-26) traced the failure to the system clock.

The clock counted time as an integer number of tenths of a second. To get seconds, the software multiplied by 0.1 stored in a 24-bit fixed-point register. 0.1 has no exact binary form (0.000110011... repeating), so the chopped constant was off by about 9.5e-8 per tick. The battery had been running for about 100 hours: 3.6 million ticks times 9.5e-8 is about 0.34 s. A Scud travels about 1,700 m/s, so the system looked for the target roughly 600-700 m from where it actually was. It saw empty sky and dropped the track. Israeli crews had already reported the drift, and the Army's advice was to reboot the systems "regularly", without saying how often. The patched software arrived in Dhahran on 26 February, the day after the strike.

**The twist** (from Robert Skeel's 1992 SIAM News analysis): tracking only needs the difference between two timestamps. If every conversion had been equally wrong, the errors would have cancelled almost completely. But the code had been partly upgraded with a more precise time conversion, and not every call site got it. One timestamp came out exact and the other drifted, so their difference kept the full error. A consistent bug cancels in a subtraction. An inconsistent fix lets it through.

For your run: a float32 step counter stops being exact above 2^24, about 16.8M. If you subtract two large float values, they should come from the same code path at the same precision.

2) **Sideways:** Ariane 5 in 1996, where a 64-bit float converted to a 16-bit int overflowed in code that wasn't even needed after launch
3) **Wildcard:** the 1992 Vancouver Stock Exchange index, which lost about half its value to truncation rounding over 22 months
