**In 2012 the European weather model had Hurricane Sandy's left turn into New Jersey about a week before landfall. The US model kept sending the storm out to sea for days afterwards.**

Sandy made landfall on October 29. Around eight days earlier, ECMWF's runs were already bending the storm west into the coast. That's a very rare track, because Atlantic storms almost always curve away northeast. NOAA's GFS didn't settle on the landfall until roughly four or five days out. The usual explanation people reached for was computing power, but the more common expert view pointed to data assimilation. That's the step that combines millions of messy observations (satellites, weather balloons, aircraft reports) into one starting picture of the atmosphere. ECMWF had used 4D-Var since 1997, which fits observations across a time window using the model's own physics. GFS used a 3D approach that treats everything as one snapshot, and it had only just started moving to a hybrid in mid-2012. How much of the gap was assimilation and how much was other differences is still argued about, so treat this as the main suspect, not a proven cause. The result was real either way. Congress paid for NOAA computing upgrades in the Sandy relief bill, and "the Europeans beat us" became a public talking point in the US.

**The twist:** the physics in the two models was broadly similar. The biggest single lever on a forecast was arguably what happens before the physics runs: deciding which observations to trust, how to time-align them, and how to weight a bad sensor. That's ingestion and quality control. In weather, the data pipeline is part of the forecast.

**More sweat isn't the only thing that makes you slip. Skin that's too dry slips too. Finger friction peaks somewhere in the middle.**

How grippy your skin is depends mostly on how much of it actually touches the rock. Slightly moist skin is softer, so it sinks into the rock's tiny bumps and touches more surface. Push it too far and sweat forms a thin wet layer that works like a lubricant. Push it the other way and dry skin goes hard and glassy and touches less. Tribology papers on fingertips keep finding this peak in the middle. That's why some climbers with very dry skin skip extra chalk and use products that put moisture back in. Chalk itself is just magnesium carbonate that soaks up sweat. Lab results on how much it adds to friction are oddly mixed, and I wouldn't trust any single study's number.

Two things decide which side of that peak you end up on:
- **Palms sweat from stress, not only from heat.** Palm sweat is driven mostly by your nervous system's arousal response, which is why a scary top-out leaves you greasy even on a cold day.
- **The rock's own temperature compared with the dew point.** If the rock surface is near the dew point, a thin film of moisture builds up on it that you can't see. That's why a cold, humid morning can feel worse than a cool, dry afternoon. Air temperature alone tells you very little.

**The twist:** the number a climber actually needs is roughly "rock-surface temperature minus dew point, plus wind and whether the rock is in sun". That's derived from forecast fields you already ingest, but no standard forecast publishes it. A few climbing-condition apps try to estimate it, but as far as I know nobody models the rock's surface temperature properly. That's the more general pattern too: the most useful forecast is often a small calculation combining raw fields that nobody bothered to put together.

**A gecko's feet could in theory hold up roughly a person's weight. Sticking isn't the hard part for a gecko. Letting go is.**

Each gecko toe is covered in microscopic hairs called setae. A 2000 Nature paper by Kellar Autumn and colleagues counted about 14,000 per square millimetre. Each seta splits into hundreds of tips around 200 nanometres wide. At that scale the tips get close enough to the surface to use van der Waals forces, the weak attraction between any two molecules that are almost touching. One seta pulled about 200 micronewtons in their measurements. Scaled up to every seta, the often-quoted figure is around 130 kg for a lizard that weighs about 50–100 grams. That's a theoretical maximum, and real geckos use only a small fraction of it. A 2002 follow-up showed the hairs stick even to water-repelling surfaces, which ruled out wet, capillary sticking as the main mechanism. Humidity does boost the grip somewhat, though, so it's not purely dry.

**The twist:** the hairs are angled, so they work like a directional switch. Pull along the toe toward the body and they grab. Raise the angle past about 30° and they peel off with almost no force. Geckos curl their toes up and back as they step, peeling each foot off like tape at that angle, many times a second. Sticking costs no energy, letting go costs no energy, and the hairs clean themselves as they go. Most human adhesives are strong or removable, rarely both. Gecko-inspired grippers that switch on and off this way have been built since Stanford's Stickybot (around 2006), and JPL has tested gecko grippers for grabbing objects in space.

You already rely on the same physics at the crag. A crimp or sloper holds when you pull along the surface, not straight away from it. Change the angle of pull and the friction you were counting on disappears.

1. **Deeper**: why splitting a contact into many tiny tips makes it stronger (contact splitting, and why bigger animals that stick by adhesion need finer hairs)
2. **Sideways**: why tape peels easily at one angle and almost never at another, and the peel equation behind it
3. **Wildcard**: spiders, beetles and tree frogs evolved sticky feet separately, and the frogs do it wet

**Grade:** PASS. Bare "3" worked; surprising-number hook; concrete specifics (Autumn 2000, ~14,000/mm², 200 µN, 30°); twist; 3 options.
