## Turn 1
**User:** Good evening. I've got an agent running a Postgres 14 to 17 major-version upgrade across our Kubernetes clusters (CloudNativePG operator, pg_upgrade in link mode). It'll be a good 90 minutes. I read a lot of history, particularly Byzantium and the late Roman Empire. Teach me something while I wait.

**Meanwhile:**
The best-known "Byzantine" thing in computing is named after an empire that never called itself Byzantine.

People in Constantinople called themselves *Rhomaioi*, Romans, right up to 1453. The label "Byzantine" caught on afterward: the German scholar Hieronymus Wolf helped popularize it in 1557, roughly a century after the city fell. Five centuries later Leslie Lamport needed a name for a fault model. In his 1982 paper with Shostak and Pease, the generals are traitors who don't just go silent, they send different messages to different allies. As Lamport tells it, his first choice was "Albanian Generals." A colleague pointed out that there were real Albanians who might object. Byzantium had nobody left to offend, so it got the name.

The twist is that the name works on two levels. It's a slur about scheming courtiers, pinned on an empire that never used the word. And a Byzantine node is one that tells each peer a different story, which is much worse than a crashed one. Lamport's result is that if you can't sign messages, tolerating *f* liars takes 3*f*+1 nodes. Tolerating *f* crashes takes only 2*f*+1.

That second number is the one your stack runs on. The Kubernetes control plane keeps its state in etcd, which uses Raft. Raft is built for nodes that stop or vanish. It assumes nobody lies. CloudNativePG builds on that for leader election and on Postgres streaming replication for data, and neither is built to handle lying nodes. Your whole upgrade trusts that a node is either up or gone. The dangerous failure is the one in between: a node that's still up but out of date. A stale primary that still accepts writes is a small, very real version of the Byzantine general.

1) **Deeper:** how the "3*f*+1" bound is proven, with three generals and one traitor
2) **Sideways:** the solidus, the Roman gold coin that kept close to the same gold content for about 700 years, and what finally broke it
3) **Wildcard:** 1204, when Constantinople's walls held for centuries and the city still fell, to its supposed allies, from the harbor side

## Turn 2
**User:** 2. The solidus, please.

**Meanwhile:**
For about 700 years, people from the Atlantic to China trusted one coin on sight, and the word "soldier" still carries its name.

Constantine I brought in the solidus around 309–312: 72 to the Roman pound, about 4.5 g of nearly pure gold. In Greek it was the *nomisma*. Emperors came and went, sieges and plagues came and went, and the weight and fineness barely moved until the 11th century. Merchants far outside the empire took it at face value, and Byzantine solidi turn up in finds as far away as China. The historian Robert Lopez called it "the dollar of the Middle Ages" in 1951. Hired fighters were paid in it, and the pay was called the *soldum*, which is where "soldier" comes from. Italian *soldi* comes from the same root.

Then it started to slip. Debasement began quietly under Michael IV (1034–41) and sped up as money ran short and the frontiers gave way, especially after the defeat at Manzikert in 1071. Michael VII got the nickname *Parapinakes*, roughly "minus a quarter," because people said a nomisma under him bought a quarter-measure less wheat. By the early 1080s some coins had only a small fraction of their old gold. I'm not certain of exact figures: estimates differ from study to study. In 1092 Alexios I gave up on repairing it and issued a new coin, the *hyperpyron* ("highly refined"), at roughly 20.5 carats instead of the old 24.

The twist is that the solidus's value came less from the gold than from the promise behind it, and the promise only works if nobody makes exceptions. Every cut in the 11th century was small enough to defend at the time, and together they ended the system in about 50 years. Once it broke, it couldn't be patched. It had to be replaced.

Your 90-minute run depends on a promise like that. `pg_upgrade --link` can be fast because it doesn't copy your data. It rebuilds the system catalogs and hard-links the old data files into the new cluster. That's only safe because the Postgres project treats the on-disk data file format as something you don't break between major versions. Where it has had to change something, such as the visibility map in 9.6, pg_upgrade handles the conversion. The other side of link mode: once the new cluster starts, the old one shares those files and can't be trusted. So the rollback plan is a backup, not the old cluster.

1) **Deeper:** why Constantine could hold the coin steady when Rome's 3rd-century emperors had debased theirs to almost nothing
2) **Sideways:** Gresham's law in the 11th century, when people hoarded old solidi and spent the debased ones
3) **Wildcard:** the 1453 story of the Kerkoporta, the small gate supposedly left unbarred, and how much of it the sources really support

**Meanwhile:**
The most famous story about how Constantinople fell, a gate someone forgot to bar, rests mostly on one historian, and he wasn't there.

The story: early on 29 May 1453, during the Ottoman final assault, a small postern called the Kerkoporta was left unbarred after a sortie. It stood near where the Theodosian land walls meet the Blachernae walls. Roughly fifty Ottoman soldiers slipped through, climbed a tower and raised their banners. Defenders saw the flags behind them, panicked, and the city fell. Steven Runciman's classic *The Fall of Constantinople 1453* (1965) tells it this way, and so do most popular versions.

The source is Doukas, a Greek historian who wrote years later, probably in the 1460s. Eyewitness accounts don't include the gate. That includes the diary of Nicolò Barbaro, a Venetian ship's doctor who was in the city during the siege. Historians still argue over exactly where the Kerkoporta was, and some think Doukas's episode is exaggerated or a garbled version of another event. I'd treat the gate story as plausible but thinly sourced.

What the eyewitnesses do agree on is a different breaking point. Giovanni Giustiniani Longo, the Genoese commander holding the most exposed stretch of wall, was badly wounded at about the same time. Accounts differ on what hit him. His men opened a gate in the inner wall to carry him out. The troops around him saw their commander pulling back and decided the line was lost. The Genoese line broke, and Ottoman troops pushed into the gap. Giustiniani died of his wounds days later. Constantine XI died in the fighting, and his body was never identified for certain.

The twist: the walls had held for over a thousand years, and they probably weren't what failed. What failed was the signal. One man's retreat, maybe some banners in the wrong place, and thousands of defenders made the same wrong guess about the state of the system at once. It's the Byzantine Generals problem from where we started, playing out on the actual walls: the hard part wasn't the attack, it was knowing what was really true.

The same pattern can show up in your upgrade. During the switchover, pods restart, readiness checks flap and replicas lag for a while, and that's expected. The real risk is a person or an automation reading those normal signals as failure and stepping in halfway through. Before it starts, decide which signals actually mean "abort."

1) **Deeper:** the Ottoman side, Mehmed II's huge bombards and the ships hauled overland into the Golden Horn
2) **Sideways:** 1204, the first time the city fell, to Crusaders who came in from the harbor side
3) **Wildcard:** the 1983 Soviet false nuclear alarm, a case where the person reading the signal guessed right

**User:** 1

**Meanwhile:**
Constantinople's best artillery engineer offered his services to the defenders first. They couldn't afford him.

His name was Orban, usually described as Hungarian, though his origins are uncertain. He offered to cast cannon for Constantine XI, who had no money to pay him or the metal. So he went to the 21-year-old Mehmed II, who paid him well. Mehmed had just built the fortress of Rumeli Hisarı on the Bosphorus in 1452. In November that year its guns sank a Venetian ship that tried to run past, and the captain, Antonio Rizzo, was impaled. For the siege Orban cast a giant bombard. The chroniclers describe a barrel of roughly 8 m firing stone balls of several hundred kilos, hauled to the walls by dozens of oxen. Treat those numbers as medieval reporting. You can see a real gun of the same type today: the Dardanelles Gun, cast for Mehmed in 1464, is at the Royal Armouries in the UK. It's about 17 tonnes of bronze and screws apart into two sections for transport.

The giant gun turned out to be awkward. Accounts say it could fire only a few times a day because it had to cool, and it may have cracked early in the siege. The defenders filled each night's breaches with earth, timber and barrels. The walls held for 53 days.

The ships were the more original move. The Byzantines had stretched a chain boom across the Golden Horn, and the Ottoman fleet couldn't force it. On 22 April 1453 Mehmed's men dragged around 70 ships overland on greased log rollers, up and over the hill behind Genoese Galata, and launched them into the Golden Horn behind the chain. Sources give different counts and routes, but the move itself is solid history.

The twist: neither weapon took the city directly. The guns forced constant repairs, and the ships forced the defenders to man the long sea walls along the Golden Horn as well. A garrison of perhaps 7,000–8,000 was holding a perimeter of about 20 km against an army many times larger. Mehmed didn't win with a single breach. He won by making the defenders cover too much ground at once, so that when Giustiniani fell there were no reserves to plug the gap.

1) **Deeper:** how gunpowder ended the age of tall walls, and the angled "star forts" Europe built in response
2) **Sideways:** Rumeli Hisarı, built in about four months in 1452, and what it shows about Mehmed's logistics
3) **Wildcard:** what Mehmed did *after* the conquest: he styled himself *Kayser-i Rûm*, Caesar of Rome. Was the empire really over?
