**Meanwhile:**
There isn't one honest "exact %" for hexagons against round cells. The number depends on how you model the round comb, and anyone quoting a single precise figure made a choice they're not telling you about. Here are the numbers that do hold up:

- **Against squares: about 7% less wall.** Hales proved in 1999 that hexagons are the shortest-wall way to split a flat surface into equal areas. For cells of the same area, a hexagonal comb uses about 7% less wall than square cells and about 18% less than triangular ones. These are exact geometry.
- **Against round cells: roughly half the wax, in a simple model.** On its own, a circle actually has less wall than a hexagon of the same area, about 5% less. But circles can't share walls. Packed as tightly as possible they only touch at points, and about 9.3% of the comb's area is left as gaps. If each round cell needs its own full wall, a hexagonal comb uses about 47% less wall per cell. That assumes thin walls and ignores the wax that would fill the gaps, so treat it as a model figure, not a measurement.

The line for your club: a lone circle beats a lone hexagon, but a comb of hexagons beats a comb of circles, because every hexagon wall does double duty for two cells.

The twist: the bees didn't get it fully optimal. Hales' proof is about the 2D grid of cells. The cell bottoms are three-rhombus pyramids, and in 1964 László Fejes Tóth showed a slightly cheaper shape for them. My memory is that the saving was tiny, well under 1%, but I'm not confident of the exact figure.

Here's the hook: in 2013 a team of engineers claimed bees don't build hexagons at all, that they build circles and physics finishes the job.

The paper was Karihaloo, Zhang and Wang in the *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*. They looked at brand-new comb and reported that the freshest cells were round, and that the same cells had become rounded hexagons about two days later. Their explanation: worker bees warm the wax to around 45°C, well below its melting point of about 62–64°C but soft enough to flow slowly. Then surface tension pulls the walls straight where three cells meet. Soap bubbles do the same thing: a raft of them settles into 120° junctions with no designer. Pirk and colleagues had floated a similar "liquid equilibrium" idea back in 2004.

The pushback came quickly:
- **Bauer and Bienefeld (2013):** they filmed comb building with a thermal camera. As I remember the paper, the wax at the building edge stayed near hive temperature, too cool to flow the way the theory needs. They argued the cells are already hexagonal as they're built.
- **Nazzi (2016, *Scientific Reports*):** argued the shape comes from how bees build. Each cell is moulded against its neighbours, with the bees' bodies acting as templates, and he showed experiments where changing the setup changed the cell shapes.

As far as I know the current view leans towards active building helped by the wax's physics, not physics on its own. I'm not certain of every measurement in those papers, so check the originals before you quote figures to your club.

The twist: this is the same argument as "did the engineer design it, or did it fall out of the constraints?" Soap-bubble physics gives you hexagons for free. So the real question isn't how bees manage to build hexagons. It's whether they gain anything from doing the work themselves, compared with letting the wax settle. That's harder to answer than it looks.
