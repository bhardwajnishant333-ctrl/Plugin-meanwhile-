**Meanwhile:**
Before October 1987, the "volatility smile" in index options barely existed. One day created it, and it has never gone away.

Black-Scholes assumes log-normal returns. Under that assumption, every strike on the same expiry should imply the same volatility. For S&P 500 index options before the 1987 crash, that was roughly true: the implied-vol curve was nearly flat. Then came October 19, 1987. The Dow fell 22.6% in one session. With volatility around 20% a year, that's roughly a 20-sigma day, which a log-normal model says should essentially never happen. Mark Rubinstein documented the before-and-after in the *Journal of Finance* in 1994. After the crash, out-of-the-money puts started trading at much higher implied vol than calls. That's the steep downward skew equity traders still see today, almost 40 years later.

The twist: the skew isn't a pricing error. It's the market's prior. Traders adjusted their beliefs on a single extreme sample and kept the adjustment as a permanent tail-risk premium. Commodities often show the opposite. Natural gas and grain options frequently carry a *call* skew, because the shock people fear there is a supply squeeze that sends prices up, not a crash. The smile's shape shows you which direction a market is afraid of.

1. **Deeper:** Breeden-Litzenberger (1978), the trick that pulls the market's full implied probability distribution out of the smile
2. **Sideways:** April 20, 2020, the day the May WTI oil contract settled at minus $37.63
3. **Wildcard:** Louis Bachelier modelled prices as Brownian motion in 1900, five years before Einstein's Brownian motion paper

**Meanwhile:**
The first person to model prices as random motion got a middling grade for it. A century later, the flaw everyone held against his model rescued the oil options market.

Louis Bachelier defended his thesis, *Théorie de la spéculation*, at the Sorbonne on March 29, 1900, with Henri Poincaré as his advisor. He modelled the prices of French government bonds (rentes) on the Paris Bourse as a random walk. From that he derived what is essentially the heat (diffusion) equation for how the probability of a price spreads out over time, and used it to price options. That was five years before Einstein's 1905 Brownian motion paper. He got "mention honorable" rather than the top grade, which hurt his academic career. In 1926, Paul Lévy's objection to an alleged error in his work cost him a post at Dijon. Lévy later apologized.

The work sat mostly unread until around the mid-1950s, when the statistician Jimmie Savage found it and mailed postcards to economists, Paul Samuelson among them. Samuelson fixed what looked like the obvious bug: Bachelier's arithmetic Brownian motion lets prices go negative. Samuelson's geometric Brownian motion became the foundation for Black-Scholes in 1973.

The twist: that bug became the feature. When WTI settled at minus $37.63 in April 2020, log-normal models broke, because you can't take the log of a negative price. CME switched the pricing for some of its oil options to the Bachelier model, the one rejected for allowing negative prices.

1. **Deeper:** why the Bachelier model is the standard for pricing interest-rate options in a negative-rate world
2. **Sideways:** the same diffusion maths runs the generative diffusion models in your field, adding noise forward and then learning to reverse it
3. **Wildcard:** Ed Thorp, who priced warrants with a Black-Scholes-like formula before 1973 and traded on it quietly

Ha, then here's the part your field mostly skips. The equation that makes diffusion models run backwards was published in 1982 by a control engineer, decades before anyone generated an image with it.

Brian D. O. Anderson's "Reverse-time diffusion equation models" (*Stochastic Processes and their Applications*, 1982) showed that a forward SDE has an exact reverse-time SDE. The reverse drift is the forward drift minus g(t)² times the score, ∇ log p_t(x). The result was exact but practically useless, because nobody had the score of a complicated distribution. The missing pieces came later: Hyvärinen's score matching (2005) and Vincent's link to denoising (2011). Song et al.'s score-SDE paper (2021) put it all together and cited Anderson as the backbone.

The market rhyme: the variance-preserving forward SDE in DDPM-style models is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, which is mean-reversion toward zero plus noise. That's the same process Oldřich Vasicek used in 1977 to model interest rates, and Eduardo Schwartz used in 1997 for log commodity spot prices. The variance-exploding version is plain Brownian motion with a schedule on its variance, which is basically Bachelier's 1900 model. So every noising step in DiffWave or WaveGrad runs a fixed-income model forward, and your network learns to run it in reverse.

1. **Deeper:** why the reverse SDE and the probability-flow ODE share marginals but behave so differently when you sample
2. **Sideways:** Schwartz's 1997 commodity models, and why oil's mean reversion makes long-dated futures behave so differently from spot
3. **Wildcard:** the Jarzynski equality, the nonequilibrium-physics result that inspired Sohl-Dickstein's original 2015 diffusion paper
