The Byzantine calendar put Creation at 1 September 5509 BC, and the date was picked to make the paperwork line up.

The "era of the world" (anno mundi) came out of Septuagint-based biblical chronology. It was in common use by about the 7th century and became the standard way to date official documents by the 10th. Its year started on 1 September, the same day as the fiscal year. The neat part is that the AM year divided by 15 gives the indiction directly, so Creation fell in indiction 1 and the tax cycle had been running since the first day of the world. Rival epochs existed, such as the Alexandrian era of 5492 BC, but Constantinople's version won. Russia kept counting this way until Peter the Great's decree took effect in 1700, which was year 7208.

The twist: in 1583 Joseph Scaliger built the Julian Period from the same administrative cycles: the 15-year indiction, the 19-year lunar cycle and the 28-year solar cycle. 15 × 19 × 28 = 7980 years, counted from 4713 BC, the last year all three cycles began together. Astronomers still count days from that point (Julian Day Numbers). So does Postgres: its date code converts dates to Julian day numbers internally, and `to_char(now(), 'J')` will show you one. Your upgrade is moving dates whose epoch comes from a Byzantine tax schedule.

In 1492 the Orthodox world ran past the end of its own Easter tables, and nobody had written the next page.

The 7,000th year of the Byzantine world era ran from September 1491 to August 1492. A long-standing tradition held that the world would last 7,000 years, one millennium for each day of creation, so the Paschalia (the tables giving each year's Easter date) in use in Muscovy stopped at 7000. Expectations of the end were real enough that when nothing happened, churchmen had to answer critics. Gennady, Archbishop of Novgorod, pushed for the tables to be extended. In 1492 Metropolitan Zosimus of Moscow issued a new Paschalion for the "eighth millennium". In the same year Moscow moved its start of the year from March to September, to line up with Byzantine practice. I'm confident on the outline. The exact split of credit between Gennady and Zosimus is something I'd check before quoting it.

The twist: the arithmetic never ran out. Easter dates in the Julian calendar repeat on a fixed 532-year cycle (19 × 28), so extending the table was just copying it out again. The limit was an assumption written into the data, not a limit of the method. That's the same failure as Y2K, or a timestamp column that "will never need" dates past some year. The edge case is a belief someone held when they wrote the table, not a computing limit.
