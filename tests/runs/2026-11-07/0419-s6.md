**Your kimchi isn't one fermentation. It's a relay where each runner poisons the track for itself and hands it to the next.**

Fresh kimchi starts near neutral, around pH 6, and *Leuconostoc* species take over first. They grow fast in the cold and ferment sugar into lactic acid, acetic acid, ethanol and CO₂. That CO₂ is the early fizz and the bright, clean taste.

But their own acid is what ends them. *Leuconostoc* don't tolerate low pH well, so as the kimchi sours, more acid-tolerant lactobacilli take over, mainly *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* and *Latilactobacillus sakei*. **In 1987 Jim Gray worked out, on the back of an envelope, when data should live in memory. The answer was "5 minutes", and it stayed roughly true for 30 years while hardware got about a million times cheaper.**

Gray and Franco Putzolu at Tandem asked one money question: is it cheaper to keep a page in RAM, or to read it from disk each time it's needed? Keeping it costs RAM. Reading it costs a slice of a disk's limited reads per second. Set the two costs equal and you get a break-even interval: if a page is used more often than that, keep it in memory. With 1987 prices it came to about 5 minutes.

Gray revisited it with Goetz Graefe in 1997, Graefe did again in 2007 with flash as a middle layer, and other researchers did in 2017. RAM and disk prices had both fallen by orders of magnitude. But they fell roughly together, so the ratio, and the answer, kept landing in the same ballpark, give or take for page size. Treat the exact numbers in the revisits as from memory; I'm sure of the 1987 rule and the fact that it was revisited.

**The twist:** it's a pricing rule, not a hardware rule. The architecture follows the cost ratio, not the specs.

**That's the choice you're making all over your dbt project.** Picking view, table or incremental for a model is this exact trade: storage plus build cost on one side, rerunning the query at read time multiplied by how often it's read on the other. On Snowflake, warehouse credits are the "disk reads" and storage is the "RAM". A model queried twice a month probably shouldn't be a table. One hit by 40 dashboards an hour probably shouldn't be a view.

**A blue whale has about 1,000 times more cells than you, and every cell division is another chance at cancer. So why don't whales all die of it?**

That's Peto's paradox, named after epidemiologist Richard Peto, who pointed it out in the 1970s. Within one species, bigger and longer-lived animals do get more cancer. Across species, they don't.

Elephants answer part of it. They carry about 20 copies of TP53, the gene that kills damaged cells, while humans have one. Elephant cells exposed to radiation destroy themselves instead of trying to repair the damage. Studies from 2015 and 2016 put elephant cancer deaths under 5%. Humans are roughly 11 to 25%.

Whales are still an open question. Bowhead whales can live past 200 years, and the leads so far point toward better DNA repair rather than extra copies of a killer gene.

**The twist:** evolution solved "more units, more failures" at least twice, in two different ways. Elephants cut the bad cell out. Bowheads, it seems, fix it.

1) **Deeper**: why naked mole rats almost never get cancer
2) **Sideways**: the Lindy effect, where the longer something has survived, the longer it's likely to last

**Meanwhile:**
**In 1969 an astrophysicist stood at the Berlin Wall and predicted, with no knowledge of politics, how long it would stand. He was right.**

J. Richard Gott reasoned: "I'm not visiting at any special moment in the Wall's life." If so, he's probably somewhere in its middle half. The Wall was 8 years old. That gave a 50% chance it would last between another 2⅔ and 24 years. It fell in 1989, 20 years later. In 1993 he published the method in *Nature* and used it to predict how long Broadway shows then running would last.

That's the Lindy effect: for some things, every year they survive adds to their expected remaining life. The name comes from Albert Goldman's 1964 essay about comedians who gathered at Lindy's deli in New York. Mandelbrot later put it in mathematical form, and Taleb popularized it in *Antifragile*.

The mechanism: it only works when lifetimes follow a power law, meaning most things die young and a few last a very long time. If lifetimes follow a Pareto distribution with exponent 2, something that has survived *t* years can expect about *t* more. People and milk do the opposite, because they age, and every year survived brings the end closer.

**The twist:** Lindy says the expected lifespan of anything new is short. It isn't a compliment to old things. It's a warning about young ones: most of them are about to die, and you can't yet tell which ones won't.

1) **Deeper**: where Gott's argument breaks, and the "doomsday argument" it leads to about humanity's own lifespan
2) **Sideways**: survivorship bias and the WWII bomber armour that's usually told wrong
3) **Wildcard**: why your six-year-old sourdough starter isn't really "six years old"
