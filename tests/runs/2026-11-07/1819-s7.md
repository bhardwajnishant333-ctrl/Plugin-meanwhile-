**Earth may soon need a clock to skip a second, and no software has ever been through that.**

How it works: since 1972, 27 leap seconds have been added to UTC so clocks keep pace with Earth's spin, which is slowly getting slower. Every one of them was an added second (23:59:60). Even that has caused trouble. The leap second on June 30, 2012 triggered a Linux kernel timer bug that sent CPU usage to 100% on Java and MySQL servers. Reddit, Mozilla and the Qantas booking system all had problems. Recently the trend has reversed: Earth's core has sped up the planet's spin. In March 2024 the geophysicist Duncan Agnew published a paper in Nature estimating that a *negative* leap second (the clock jumps from 23:59:58 straight to 00:00:00) could be needed around 2029. His other finding: melting polar ice moves mass toward the equator and slows the spin, which pushed that date back by about three years. Separately, in November 2022 the international body that governs measurement standards (the CGPM) voted to stop adding leap seconds by 2035. Whether a negative one arrives before then is still an open question.

The twist: a negative leap second has never happened, so no code path that handles it has ever run in production. Big providers sidestep the problem by "smearing" the change across a full day. Google does this. On Android, the wall clock (`System.currentTimeMillis()`) can jump or be smeared, but `SystemClock.elapsedRealtime()` is monotonic and can't. Any duration your app measures with the wall clock, like a timeout, a session length or a "within 5 seconds" check, is exposed. A flaky instrumented test that only fails around a time sync is the small, everyday version of the same bug.

**Your clock runs on the Sun. Orion runs on the stars. They disagree by about 4 minutes a day.**

Here's why. In one day, Earth also moves about 1/365 of the way around its orbit, roughly 1°. So once Earth has turned a full 360° against the stars, it has to turn about 1° more before the Sun is back in the same spot. That extra 1° takes about 4 minutes.
- **Sidereal day** (one turn measured against the stars): 23h 56m 4s
- **Solar day** (one turn measured against the Sun, which is what clocks use): 24h 00m

Every star rises, crosses due south and sets about 3m 56s earlier each night by your clock. That adds up to about 28 minutes a week and about 2 hours a month. Orion is highest in the south around midnight in mid-December. By late March it is already sinking in the west at dusk. Around mid-June the Sun sits right in front of it, so you can't see it at all.

The twist: in a year, Earth turns about **366.25 times** against the stars but only 365.25 times against the Sun. Going around the Sun uses up one whole turn. You never notice that extra day because your clock doesn't count it. It's the same as a server clock that drifts by a fixed amount each day: the error is tiny daily and adds up to a full cycle over the year.

Practical upshot for the Dob: right now, in early November, the drift works in your favour. Orion rises about half an hour earlier each week and gets easier to catch until it's well placed in winter evenings. Rule of thumb: a target sits where it was a month ago, but 2 hours earlier by the clock.

**Meanwhile:**
**What the error means:** a `NavController` stays tied to the `ViewModelStore` it was first given once it has a graph and a back stack. `NavHost` then calls `setViewModelStore(...)` again with a different store. The controller already has entries in its back stack, so it throws `ViewModelStore should be set before setGraph call`.

**Why it happens here:** `TestNavHostController` is created outside Navigation's own lifecycle handling and passed in from the test. When `scenario.recreate()` runs, `NavHost` composes again under the recreated activity while that controller still has its old back stack, so the store check fails. There's a second problem: `TestNavHostController` isn't saveable. Even without the crash, its back stack wouldn't come back after a rotation the way `rememberNavController()`'s does. So this test can't check what it's named after.

**Fix: test rotation the way the app actually rotates.** Let the activity own the content and the controller:

```kotlin
@get:Rule val composeTestRule = createAndroidComposeRule<MainActivity>()  // MainActivity.onCreate: setContent { SkyLogApp() }

@Test fun editNote_survivesRotation() {
    // navigate + type the note via composeTestRule.onNode...
    composeTestRule.activityRule.scenario.recreate()
    composeTestRule.onNodeWithText("my note").assertIsDisplayed()
}
```

Two things to check:
- Don't call `composeTestRule.setContent` here. The recreated activity has to rebuild its own UI from `onCreate`. If the content comes from the test, the recreated activity just shows a blank screen.
- `SkyLogApp`'s parameter should default to `rememberNavController()`. That way the nav back stack is saved and restored, and the note text survives as long as it lives in `rememberSaveable` or a `SavedStateHandle`.

**If you also want to check the nav route,** don't inject a `TestNavHostController` at all. Read the route from the screen (`onNodeWithTag`), or expose the controller through a test-only hook that is created inside composition with `rememberNavController()`.

**Faster alternative (no real activity recreate):** `StateRestorationTester(composeTestRule).setContent { SkyLogApp() }`, then `emulateSavedInstanceStateRestore()`. It tests the same save-and-restore path for `rememberSaveable` and nav state, and it's quicker and less flaky on a CI emulator.
