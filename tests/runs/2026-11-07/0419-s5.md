**The most valuable field on an eBird checklist doesn't record any bird. It's a yes/no question: "Are you reporting all species you detected?"**

**How it worked:** eBird was started in 2002 by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology and National Audubon. Each checklist records effort: the protocol (stationary, traveling or incidental), how long you watched, how far you walked, and how many people were in your group. That lets models adjust for effort, the way party-hours do for the Christmas count. The "complete checklist" flag does something more. If you say yes, then any species you didn't list counts as **not detected**, rather than unknown. Without that flag, a sighting list only tells you where birds were seen. With it, you also learn where they weren't, and that's what lets Cornell's Status & Trends models map abundance and range limits. (The 2002 launch and these fields I'm confident about. Total observations are over a billion, but I'm not sure of the current exact count.)

**The twist:** Silence in a log pipeline has the same problem. If a host stops sending, you can't tell whether it's healthy and quiet, dead, or your shipper dropped the data. The fix works like a complete checklist: have the CLI send a periodic heartbeat or summary record per source, such as "source X, window 12:00–12:01, 0 errors, 4,812 lines read, 0 dropped." Then "0 errors" means the host was checked and had none, not that nothing arrived. Alerting on a missing heartbeat catches the outages your error-rate alerts can't see.

**The twist:** Ask the same question of your CLI: where does the shipper report its own errors? **How it worked:** Stanislav Petrov was on duty at the Serpukhov-15 bunker outside Moscow when the new Oko satellite system reported one Minuteman ICBM launch, then several more. His job was to pass warnings up the chain, where a retaliation decision would have only minutes. He reported a malfunction instead. His reasoning, as he later told it: a real US first strike would use hundreds of missiles, not five. The system was new and he didn't fully trust it. And ground radar, an independent sensor, showed nothing yet. He was right. The usual explanation is that sunlight reflecting off high-altitude clouds lined up with the satellites' view and looked like launch flares. (The core story is well documented. **The twist:** He used two checks. First, does the signal match the shape of a real event? Second, does an independent source confirm it? Both work for alerts in your CLI. If every host goes to 100% parse errors in the same second, the likely cause is your shipper, parser or a config push, not the whole fleet failing at once.
