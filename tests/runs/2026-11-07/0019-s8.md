**On New Year's Day 1995, a laser on a North Sea gas platform measured a wave that oceanographers' models said should come along about once every 10,000 years.**

It happened at the Draupner platform off Norway. The sea state was rough but ordinary: significant wave height (the average of the highest third of waves) was about 12 m. It mattered because this was the first rogue wave measured by an instrument. Before that, sailors' reports of "walls of water" were mostly dismissed as exaggeration.

The physics explains why. The standard model treated the sea as many independent wave trains added together (linear superposition), which gives a tidy Rayleigh distribution of wave heights. Under that model, a wave more than twice the significant height is extremely rare. The real ocean is nonlinear. A wave group can pull energy from its neighbours (the Benjamin–Feir, or modulational, instability) and focus it into one monster. Offshore design changed more. Norway's offshore standards (NORSOK) now work at two probability levels:

The practical effect is the **air gap**, the clearance between the highest crest and the platform deck. Draupner-type crests changed how that clearance is calculated.
