**Meanwhile:**
NNUE, the neural network inside Stockfish that judges how good a position is, comes from computer shogi. Its main idea isn't "a smarter brain." It's "only redo the part that changed."

**How it works.** Yu Nasu came up with NNUE ("Efficiently Updatable Neural Network") in 2018 for shogi engines. It came to Stockfish in version 12, in September 2020. The release claimed a large jump over Stockfish 11 in self-play. I remember it as roughly 100+ Elo, but treat that number as approximate.

- The network's input is a huge, mostly empty set of yes/no features. In the original HalfKP scheme, each one means "my king is on square K and piece P is on square S." That's about 41,000 features per side, with only around 30 switched on at once.
- The first layer is a big sum of weights for whichever features are on. Stockfish keeps that sum as an **accumulator**.
- A normal move changes only 2 to 4 features. So the engine doesn't recompute anything. It subtracts a few weight columns and adds a few others. That's cheap enough to run millions of times per second on a plain CPU, no GPU needed.

**The twist.** Every feature is tied to where your own king stands. So when your king moves, every feature for that side changes and the accumulator has to be rebuilt from scratch. That's the one expensive move. The design is fast because the common case costs almost nothing and the rare case pays the full price. It also beat bigger, "smarter" networks because being cheap meant searching deeper. In a blitz game of engines, speed of evaluation is part of strength.

That's the same deal as your migration. styled-components does its styling work while the page runs. vanilla-extract moves that work into the build, so the user's browser pays nothing.

For most of chess history, a clock could only tell you that you'd run out of time. It couldn't tell you when.

**How it worked.** Before clocks, players used sandglasses. Anderssen and Kolisch timed a match with them in 1861. London 1883 is usually named as the first tournament with mechanical clocks. I'm confident about the year; I'm less sure who built them. They were two clock movements on a rocking beam: tip one side down and that clock stopped while the other started. That's where "pressing your clock" comes from.

The **flag** came later, around 1900. A Dutch maker named Veenhoff is usually credited, but I'd treat the exact date as uncertain. It's a small red lever that the minute hand lifts as it climbs toward 12 and then drops past. That's why analog blitz clocks were set to 5:55: so the flag fell exactly at 6:00. It was also the clock's only precise moment. Anywhere else, "how much time is left" was an estimate from watching a hand, and if both flags were down there was often no way to prove which fell first. The tie rules differed by event and era, and I'm not sure of the details.

**The twist.** Bobby Fischer patented the increment clock in the late 1980s: a few seconds added after every move. I'm fairly sure about the patent; I'm not certain of the number or year. It made time something that refills as you play, not only something that runs down. That killed the old "flag them in a lost position" scramble, because now you could always buy time by moving. And mechanically it's a **token bucket**, the same idea behind rate limiters: steady refill, a cap, spend per action. Sudden death is a hard deadline. Increment is a budget that refills.

Fischer increment and Bronstein delay look alike on paper, but in time trouble one lets you climb out and the other only keeps you alive.

**How each one works**
- **Fischer increment (+N):** after every move you get N seconds, however fast you moved. Your clock can climb, even above where you started.
- **Bronstein delay (N):** after each move you get back the time you just used, up to N seconds. Your clock never goes above where it was before the move.
- **Simple ("US") delay:** the clock waits N seconds before it starts counting down. It works out the same as Bronstein: you never lose time on a fast move and never gain any.

Each style is named after the player credited with it, David Bronstein and Bobby Fischer, but I'm not sure exactly when Bronstein proposed his. As I understand it, US events have long allowed delay clocks as standard equipment, while FIDE events mostly use increment (90+30 and so on). I'm confident about that split but not about the exact rules for each event.

**The twist.** Take 2 seconds left, with either +2 increment or a 2-second delay, and play every move in 1 second:
- **Increment:** 2 → 3 → 4 → 5… Fast moves build a cushion, so you can blitz out simple moves and save time for the hard one.
- **Delay:** 2 → 2 → 2… You'll never flag, but you'll never recover either. One move that takes 3 seconds and you're down to about 1, for good.

Delay protects you; increment rewards speed. That's why increment endgames can go on almost forever, with only the 50-move rule capping them: a player who moves fast keeps adding to their clock.

In rate-limiter terms, increment is a token bucket: unused budget carries over, up to a limit. Delay is a free allowance on each request that doesn't carry over.
