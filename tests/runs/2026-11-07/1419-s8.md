**Meanwhile:**
Changing one row in Snowflake can mean rewriting tens of megabytes.

**How it works:** Snowflake stores tables as micro-partitions, each holding 50–500 MB of data before compression. Once written, a micro-partition never changes. So when a MERGE updates one row, Snowflake writes a new copy of the whole micro-partition that row sits in and retires the old one. The old copy still costs you storage. It's kept for Time Travel (1 day by default, up to 90 on Enterprise), and permanent tables then hold it for another 7 days of Fail-safe.

**Why it matters:** an incremental model's cost depends on how many micro-partitions it touches, not how many rows it changes. Say 1,000 late-arriving updates are spread across two years of history. That can rewrite close to 1,000 micro-partitions, which is nearly a full-table rebuild that's still labelled "incremental." The same 1,000 updates packed into yesterday's data might rewrite three. To keep rewrites contained:
- Make sure the table is physically ordered by the column your changes cluster on, usually event date.
- Use dbt's `incremental_predicates` so the merge only scans a recent slice of the target table.

That's worth checking in the query profile when your agent's forty models land: compare partitions scanned to rows updated.

**Meanwhile:**
The "hull speed" formula doesn't set a top speed. It describes one way of holding a boat up, and planing is a different way.

**How it works:** a hull moving through water makes a wave system, and the faster it goes, the longer the wave. Deep-water waves travel at a speed tied to their wavelength (v = √(gλ/2π)). At about 1.34 × √(waterline length in feet) knots, the bow wave grows as long as the boat. The stern then drops into the trough and the bow points up at the crest, so the boat is sailing uphill against its own wave. Drag climbs very steeply from there. In engineering terms that's a length-based Froude number of about 0.4. For a Laser, with roughly 12.5 ft of waterline, it works out to about 4.7 knots.

Lasers plane well into double digits all the same, because once the boat is planing, its weight is carried differently:
- **Displacement:** buoyancy holds the boat up, and the wave rule applies in full.
- **Planing:** the hull's angle to the water pushes it upward, so it rides up onto its own bow wave. The wetted length gets shorter, and the formula's main input stops meaning much.
- **Foiling:** take the hull out of the water entirely. A foiling Moth, an 11-footer with a "hull speed" of about 4.4 knots, has been sailed at over 30 knots.

Each of these has its own drag curve. Only the displacement one has that wall.

**Why it matters:** "hull speed" holds for heavy boats that can't produce enough lift for their weight. For a light dinghy with plenty of sail area, it marks where you change modes. That's why the hump just before planing feels so slow: drag peaks there, and the boat is still too heavy for the lift it's making. You get over it with a gust, a pump or hiking harder, and drag then drops even as speed rises.

The same pattern shows up in the earlier Snowflake idea: an apparent hard limit is often a limit of the mechanism you're using, not of the problem.

The formula behind your hull speed also predicts when you'll stop walking and start running. The same calculation works for a toddler, a horse and a person on the Moon.

**How it works:** walking is an inverted pendulum. Your body vaults over a stiff leg, so your center of mass travels along an arc about one leg-length (L) in radius. Staying on an arc takes a centripetal force of mv²/L, and all you have to provide it is gravity, mg. Once v²/(gL) passes 1, gravity can't hold you down on the arc and you'd lift off the top. That ratio is a Froude number. It's the square of the form used for hulls, so a hull speed of 0.4 corresponds to about 0.16 here.

In practice people and animals switch to running well before 1, at about 0.5. R. McNeill Alexander showed this holds across species, and in the 1970s he used it to estimate dinosaur speeds from fossil trackways. With a leg length of about 0.9 m:
- **On Earth:** √(0.5 × 9.81 × 0.9) ≈ 2.1 m/s, which matches the speed where people actually switch to a jog.
- **On the Moon** (1/6 g): about 0.86 m/s. That's part of why Apollo astronauts loped and hopped instead of walking briskly. Kram and colleagues tested this in 1997 by hanging people in rigs that took away part of their weight. Transition speeds dropped, and the Froude number stayed roughly constant. (I'm confident of the finding, less so of their exact figures.)

**Why it matters:** elite race walkers go about 4 m/s, a Froude number near 2, which is twice the supposed physical limit. They manage it by dropping the hip on each stride so the center of mass stays flat instead of arcing. It's the same move as planing: the "limit" assumed a mechanism, and they changed the mechanism. The rules require one foot on the ground at all times, which is what pushes them into that technique.
