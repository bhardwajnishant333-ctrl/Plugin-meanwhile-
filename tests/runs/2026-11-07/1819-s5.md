**The language you write in is named after a Danish telephone engineer. His math from 1917 shows why a database pool gets into trouble at 90% busy, long before 100%.**

Agner Krarup Erlang worked for the Copenhagen Telephone Company. He was working out how many phone lines a switchboard needed. In 1909 he showed that incoming calls arrive at random in a predictable pattern (a Poisson process). In 1917 he published formulas for how often a caller would get no line at all, or would have to wait for one. That work became queueing theory. The unit of telephone traffic, the "erlang", is named after him: one line kept busy all the time. Ericsson named its language after him in the 1980s, and people often add that the name also works as "Ericsson Language".

In the simplest queue model, average waiting time grows as ρ/(1−ρ), where ρ is how busy the server is:
- 80% busy: you wait about **4x** as long as the work itself takes
- 90% busy: about **9x**
- 95% busy: about **19x**

**The twist:** the last 10% of usage costs more than everything before it. So the database pool doesn't fail when it runs out of connections. It fails first because waits blow up. That's why DBConnection, the library under Ecto's pool, has `queue_target` (default 50ms) and `queue_interval` (default 1000ms). If requests keep waiting longer than the target for a whole interval, it starts rejecting requests on purpose. That stops one slow moment from turning into a growing backlog. Raising `pool_size` can make things worse if the database is the real limit, because you just move the queue into Postgres.

**Every pool size comes from one equation, and it fits on a napkin.**

Little's law (John Little proved it in 1961): **L = λ × W**. The average number of busy connections equals queries per second times how long each query holds a connection. 500 queries/s × 10ms = **5 connections** busy on average. Using Erlang's curve from before, that pool should run at about 60–70% busy at peak. So start around 8. Ecto's default of 10 fits a lot of apps for exactly this reason.

**Why bigger pools often hurt:** each Postgres connection is a separate OS process. Past roughly the number of CPU cores, they don't add capacity. They just take turns on the CPU and fight over locks and memory. A widely quoted starting formula from the Postgres wiki is **(cores × 2) + number of disks**. The HikariCP pool-sizing page describes an Oracle performance demo where shrinking a pool from about 2048 to 96 connections cut response times by about 50x. I'm sure of what the demo shows but less sure of the exact numbers, so check before you quote them.

**The twist, for Elixir specifically:** the number to watch is how long a connection is held, not how long the query takes. `Repo.transaction` keeps its connection for the whole block. An HTTP call, a `GenServer.call`, or a slow `Enum` loop inside a transaction keeps a connection idle and unavailable to anyone else. One 2-second external call inside a transaction ties up a connection as long as about 200 normal 10ms queries would. Fix the hold time, not the pool size. Telemetry already shows this: compare the `queue_time` and `query_time` measurements on `[:my_app, :repo, :query]`. If queue time is high and query time is low, something is holding connections too long.

**A cart with no motor and no battery drove straight downwind faster than the wind pushing it. You've done the same thing on a dinghy.**

On 2 July 2010, Rick Cavallaro's cart Blackbird ran at El Mirage dry lake at about **2.8 times the wind speed**, directly downwind. The North American Land Sailing Association ratified the record. Most physicists' first reaction is that this can't be done. In 2021 Derek Muller (Veritasium) made a video about it, and a UCLA physicist, Alexander Kusko, bet him **$10,000** it was a trick. Kusko later conceded and paid.

**How it works:** the wheels drive the propeller, not the other way round. The cart sits between two things moving at different speeds, the ground and the air. Each wheel turn pushes air backwards through the propeller. It takes a little energy from the ground and pushes back against moving air with more force than the wheels lose. At wind speed a flag on the cart hangs limp, but the air is still moving relative to the ground, so there is still energy to harvest. The energy comes from the difference in speed between air and ground, not from the wind pushing the cart.

**The twist, for you:** a fast dinghy or iceboat sailing downwind already does this. It broad-reaches and gybes, and its progress straight downwind (VMG) can beat the true wind speed. Foiling America's Cup boats do it all the time. Blackbird does that same zig-zag, but the propeller blades are sails wound into a helix. Each blade is always on a "reach", so the cart never has to gybe. So if someone tells you it's impossible, the evidence is the boat you sail on weekends.

**The faster you go, the more the wind seems to come from in front of you. Past a certain speed, a boat heading downwind is sailing into a headwind.**

Apparent wind is what the boat actually feels: the true wind added to the headwind the boat makes by moving. Take a true wind of 10 knots straight from the side:
- Boat stopped: you feel 10 kn at 90°.
- Boat at 10 kn: you feel **14.1 kn from 45°** off the bow.
- Boat at 20 kn (iceboat territory): **22.4 kn from about 27°**.

As the boat speeds up, the wind gets stronger and moves toward the bow. That's why an iceboat can reach several times the true wind speed (3–5x is often quoted for DN class boats; take the exact range loosely). It has almost no drag, so it keeps speeding up and keeps pulling the wind ahead. Even on a downwind leg its sail is pulled in nearly to the centreline, as if it were sailing upwind. Foiling boats and Blackbird's propeller blades all work the same way.

**The twist, on your dinghy:** this explains two habits you probably already have.
- **When a gust hits, bear away.** The gust adds to the true wind faster than the boat can speed up, so the apparent wind swings *aft*. You can turn lower and keep the same angle on the sail.
- **A lull can feel like a header.** True wind drops, your speed hasn't dropped yet, so the apparent wind swings *forward*. Your telltales flutter as if the wind shifted, but it didn't. Before you tack on a "shift", check whether the wind just got lighter.

What you feel on the boat mixes the outside wind with your own speed. A lot of instincts on the water come from untangling those two.
