**In 1452, Constantinople was offered the weapon that ended it, and turned it down because it couldn't pay the salary.**

A cannon founder named Orban (usually called Hungarian; his origins are uncertain) first offered his services to Emperor Constantine XI. The treasury couldn't afford his fee, so he went to Mehmed II, who paid it several times over. He built a bronze bombard roughly 8 metres long. Accounts say it fired stones of around 500 kg, though Kritoboulos and Doukas give different figures. It was so slow to cool and reload that it reportedly fired only a few times a day.

What makes it interesting is the walls. The Theodosian Walls were built around 413 under Theodosius II and used layered defence: a moat, an outer wall, then a taller inner wall with 96 towers. No attacker broke them from the land side for about a thousand years. The 1204 crusaders got in from the sea walls instead.

**The twist:** even in 1453 the cannon didn't win on its own. Every night the defenders filled the breaches with timber, earth and barrels. The real contest was damage done per day against repair done per night, and for about seven weeks the defenders kept up. What finally broke was the people. Giovanni Giustiniani, the Genoese commander who held the key sector, was badly wounded on 29 May. When he was carried out, his men lost heart and the line collapsed. Doukas also says a small gate, the Kerkoporta, was left unbarred. Historians still argue about whether that happened.

So the walls held up fine. The defence failed at the single person everyone depended on.

1. **Deeper:** how the overnight breach repairs actually worked, and why Mehmed dragged ships overland into the Golden Horn.
2. **Sideways:** Greek fire. Byzantium kept it so secret that the recipe was lost, and the empire effectively lost its own best weapon.
3. **Wildcard:** the 2017 S3 outage that went down partly because of one mistyped command and a restart path no one had exercised in years.

**Meanwhile:**
**Byzantium protected Greek fire so well that it lost the recipe itself.**

According to the chronicler Theophanes, it arrived around 672 with Kallinikos, an architect who fled from Heliopolis in Syria. It helped break the Arab sieges of Constantinople in 674–678 and 717–718. The usual way to fire it was a siphon: a pressurised pump with a bronze nozzle that sprayed burning liquid across the water. It kept burning on the sea surface and was very hard to put out.

The secrecy was the policy. Around 950, Emperor Constantine VII wrote a handbook for his son, *De Administrando Imperio*, telling him never to hand the weapon to foreigners, whatever they offered. He also told him to say an angel had given it to Constantine the Great, so sharing it would be sacrilege. That's a cover story. The real know-how seems to have stayed inside a small group, possibly one family of makers, and the details were never written down in any text that survives.

What it actually was is still unknown. The most likely guess is a crude-oil base, perhaps mixed with resins. Anna Komnene, writing around 1100, describes a hand-thrown version made from pine resin and sulphur. Whether that is the same naval weapon is debated.

**The twist:** by the 1200s, mentions of it fade out. Historians disagree about why. Either the knowledge died with its keepers, or Byzantium lost access to the crude-oil sources around the Black Sea. Either way, the result was the same: the empire's best weapon depended on a few people who knew how to make it, plus one supply chain, and nobody noticed until it was gone. No serious account has it playing a decisive role in 1204 or 1453.

It's the Giustiniani problem again, but for knowledge instead of leadership. Strict secrecy and a single person everyone depends on turn out to be the same risk.

1. **Deeper:** the siphon itself, and why "a pump that sprays burning oil" was a hard engineering problem in the 7th century.
2. **Sideways:** the Knights Templar's banking network, and what happened when a king decided to destroy the people who held the records.
3. **Wildcard:** why we still can't fully explain how Roman concrete heals its own cracks, and the 2023 MIT finding about lime clasts.

## Turn 3
**User:** 1. siphon engineering, go

**Meanwhile:**
**Byzantium's flamethrower was a Roman fire engine turned around.**

In Latin, *sipho* already meant a fire-fighting pump. Around 110 AD, Pliny the Younger wrote to Trajan after a fire destroyed much of Nicomedia, complaining that the town had no *sipho* and no buckets. The technology behind it was older still: Ctesibius of Alexandria invented the force pump in the 3rd century BC, and Heron later described a double-cylinder version for fighting fires. So the Byzantines didn't have to invent a pump. They had to make one work with a liquid that wanted to explode.

**What the sources say:**
- Emperor Leo VI's *Tactica* (about 900) describes a siphon mounted at the bow and sheathed in bronze. It also mentions small hand siphons (*cheirosiphona*).
- Anna Komnene describes ships off Rhodes in 1103 fitted with bronze lion heads at the bow that spat the fire.
- The 12th-century Madrid Skylitzes manuscript shows a crewman spraying a jet of flame at an enemy ship.

**The engineering, as the historian John Haldon's group reconstructed it:** Haldon and colleagues built a working model in the early 2000s. My recollection of their figures may be slightly off. Their setup:
1. Heat crude oil in a sealed bronze tank over a small fire. Heat thins the oil and builds vapour pressure.
2. Add more pressure with a hand force pump.
3. Open a valve to send the hot oil out through a nozzle, past an igniter such as a lit wick at the mouth.

Their results: a jet of flame reaching very roughly 10–15 metres, burning around 1000°C, and still burning on the water.

**Why it was hard:** you are running a hot pressure vessel full of flammable oil on a wooden ship. Too little heat and the oil comes out as a weak dribble. Too much and the tank seams fail, so you burn your own ship. The range was short, so crews had to close with the enemy, and a crosswind could turn the jet back on them. The real skill wasn't the recipe. It was working the heat, pressure and valve together under combat stress.

**The twist:** that may be why the knowledge was so easy to lose. Even if someone had written down the ingredients, the hard part was hands-on skill: how hot is hot enough, when to pump, when to open the valve. That kind of skill only passes from crew to crew. Lose a generation of trained crews and the written recipe is useless.

1. **Deeper:** the Arab siege of 717–718, where Greek fire, a brutal winter and a Bulgar army together broke the attackers.
2. **Sideways:** how medieval sappers actually brought walls down by digging under them, and the countermining war fought underground.
3. **Wildcard:** the Vasa, a 1628 Swedish warship that sank about 1,300 metres into its maiden voyage because the king's specs overruled the builders' judgement.

**Where that applies to your cutover:** logical replication copies table rows, not the state around them. Before you call it done, check these on the new primary:
- **Sequences:** they aren't replicated, so set them past each table's current max ID, or the first inserts will fail with duplicate-key errors.
- **Planner statistics:** they aren't copied either, so run `ANALYZE`.
- **Schema changes and large objects:** anything changed or written during the sync window needs checking, since neither replicates.
