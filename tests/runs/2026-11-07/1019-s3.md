**On 21 January 1876 the signals at Abbots Ripton showed "clear" while a coal train was still on the line. Nobody had set them wrong. Snow had frozen them that way.**

The Great Northern used slotted-post semaphores. When the arm dropped into a slot in the post, the signal read clear. The GNR also kept signals at clear by default and only moved them to danger when a train needed protecting. That night in a blizzard, snow and ice packed the slots, and the arms stayed at clear whatever the signalman did with his levers. The up Flying Scotsman ran into a coal train that was being shunted out of its way. Then a down express hit the wreckage. 13 people died.

The fix was not better snow clearing. It was flipping the default. Signals would now rest at danger and show clear only while someone was actively pulling them off. The GNR also brought in the "somersault" signal, an arm pivoted clear of the post so it had nowhere for ice to collect. A stuck or broken signal would now stop trains instead of letting them through.

**The twist, and it's live on your migration:** a logical replication slot fails the old GNR way. If the subscriber stalls, crashes or falls behind, the publisher keeps every byte of WAL the slot still needs, with no limit, until the disk fills and the primary goes down. Postgres added the "rest at danger" fix in version 13 with `max_slot_wal_keep_size`. Your source is PG12, so the setting doesn't exist there. For the next two hours your PG12 primary's disk is only safe while the EKS side keeps consuming. One query to keep an eye on it (works on 12):

```sql
SELECT slot_name, active,
       pg_size_pretty(pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_current_wal_lsn(), restart_lsn)) AS retained_wal
FROM pg_replication_slots;
```

**The 1892 conversion looks like a track job. The hard part was actually the trains.**

You know the headline: the last broad gauge train, the Cornishman, left Paddington on Friday 20 May 1892. Over that weekend a few thousand men (figures are usually given as 4,000 to 5,000) re-gauged roughly 170 route miles west of Exeter, and standard gauge services ran on the Monday. The track was the easy part, because it had been prepared for years. Brunel's baulk road held the rails on longitudinal timbers tied by transoms. Those were pre-cut and marked, so on the day a gang mostly had to free one rail and slide it inward.

The hard part was the rolling stock. Anything broad gauge still west of the conversion point on Monday was stranded, with no line it could run on. So in the final days the GWR moved every last broad gauge locomotive, carriage and wagon east to Swindon, where miles of temporary sidings had been laid to hold them while they were converted or broken up. Some stock had been built as "convertible" years ahead, with wheels and axles designed to be narrowed later. The GWR sized the operation by the thing that could get stranded, not by the miles of track.

**The rhyme with your Monday:** logical replication is the track. It moves the rows, and you're watching it do that well. What it doesn't move through PG16 is your rolling stock. Sequences stay at their starting values on the PG16 side, so the first INSERT after cutover hits a duplicate key error unless you `setval` every sequence from the source's current values while writes are frozen. DDL and large objects don't replicate either. Your Swindon sidings are a sequence-sync script, written now and run at cutover before the app comes back.

**For roughly 20 years the GWR kept building new equipment for a gauge it still officially resisted. Writing off unconvertible stock had become more expensive than staying loyal to Brunel.**

Mechanism: the 1846 Gauge Act required new passenger lines to be standard gauge unless Parliament made an exception. That turned the GWR into an island. From the 1860s it ran mixed gauge, a third rail that let both gauges share the same line, and it was miserable to operate. The pointwork was complex, trains had to be marshalled by gauge, and every junction was a negotiation. It took the South Wales lines over to standard gauge in a single conversion in 1872 (I'm confident of the year, less sure of the exact mileage). That left the West Country main line as the holdout.

From the 1880s, as the last conversion became inevitable but had no date yet, Swindon built broad gauge stock designed to be narrowed later. Some carriages had standard-width bodies sitting on broad gauge underframes, so converting them meant changing the running gear rather than building a new vehicle. Dean's 2-2-2 singles of 1891 to 92 included a batch built as broad gauge convertibles that ran a short while and were then narrowed (I'm fairly sure of the class details, not certain). After May 1892 they simply carried on.

**The twist:** the GWR made the old world and the new world compatible at the asset level before the switch, so the switch itself was cheap. Engineers now call this expand/contract. The mixed gauge years were the painful dual-running phase, and convertible stock was what let them end it in a single weekend.

Your convertible stock is your app and your roles, more than your data. One concrete check: from PG15 onwards, ordinary roles no longer get CREATE on the `public` schema by default in new databases, and your PG16 target databases are new. If any app role or migration tool creates tables in `public` without owning the schema, it works on 12 and fails on 16. Run your app's migration tool against the PG16 side before cutover, not after.
