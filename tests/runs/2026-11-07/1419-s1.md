Your port can turn requests that used to work into 422 errors, and none of your Python code has to change for that to happen.

**How it happens:** Pydantic v1 was loose about input. If a field was `int` and a client sent `1.5`, v1 quietly cut it to `1`. If a field was `str` and a client sent `123`, v1 turned it into `"123"`. Pydantic v2 (with its core rewritten in Rust as `pydantic-core`) has firmer rules in its default "lax" mode:
- `int` accepts `1.0` but rejects `1.5`. It won't throw away part of a number.
- `str` rejects a bare number like `123`. You can turn that back on with `coerce_numbers_to_str=True` in model config.

One correction first: my teaser was off about flour. Flour is a real source of your starter's microbes. The part that's mostly myth is your city.

*Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis* (now officially *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*) was named in 1971 after scientists isolated it from San Francisco sourdough. Since then it has turned up in starters all over the world, including traditional European rye and wheat starters. The bacterium named after the city isn't specific to the city.

**The study:** In 2021 the Global Sourdough Project (Landis, Wolfe, Dunn and others, published in *eLife*) sequenced about 500 starters, mostly from the US and Europe. Geography explained very little of the difference between them. Two starters from different continents could match more closely than two from the same street. A smaller study from 2020 (Reese and colleagues, *Scientific Reports*) gave 15 bakers the same flour and found that each baker's starter shared microbes with that baker's own hands. So the ingredients are partly you.

**The twist:** a starter isn't so much a culture you inherit as a contest your habits decide. Many microbes arrive from flour, hands and your kitchen. Your feeding schedule, temperature, hydration and acidity decide which ones survive. That's why an "heirloom" starter shipped across the country often turns into a local starter within weeks of new feedings. The thing that persists is the routine, not the original microbes.

That's also the honest version of the Pydantic story: what you get out is decided less by where the data came from than by what your validation lets survive.

**Meanwhile:**
The vinegar smell mostly comes from bacteria that have something extra to burn.

**How it works:** Many sourdough lactic acid bacteria are "heterofermentative." They split sugar into lactic acid, CO2, and then either ethanol or acetic acid (the acid in vinegar). Which of those last two they make depends on whether they have an **electron acceptor**, something that soaks up the leftover electrons:
- **Without one**, they have to make ethanol to keep their chemistry balanced. That's where boozy "hooch" comes from.
- **With one** (fructose in the flour, or oxygen), they can make acetic acid instead and get a bit of extra energy for it. *F. sanfranciscensis* does this by turning fructose into mannitol.

**The usual dials (common baker wisdom; the studies back it only in part, so treat these as tendencies):**
- **Stiffer starter (lower hydration):** more acetic. Wetter: more lactic and milder.
- **Cooler fermentation:** more acetic compared with lactic. Warmer: more yogurt-like.
- **Whole grain or rye:** more sourness overall, because there's more food and more minerals that slow the drop in acidity.
- **A second culprit:** the 2021 Global Sourdough Project found true **acetic acid bacteria** (the *Acetobacter* family that makes vinegar) in some starters, and those starters smelled more like vinegar. So sometimes the smell isn't your feeding habits at all. It's a different organism living there.

**The twist:** when your starter smells like vinegar, it isn't sick. It has found extra energy. A sharp nose is the microbes telling you there was an electron acceptor around, and acidity is the cost of a jar where they're thriving.
