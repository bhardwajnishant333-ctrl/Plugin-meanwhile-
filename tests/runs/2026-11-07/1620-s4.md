The mechanics, step by step:

**A warehouse can be stacked with coffee and the futures market can still be running out of it, because only coffee that passes a tasting panel counts.**

The mechanics, as I understand them:

- **The contract.** ICE's "Coffee C" is arabica, 37,500 lb per lot (about 17 tonnes). It is delivered from ICE-licensed warehouses in a handful of ports in the US and Europe.
- **Getting certified.** Before any bag can be delivered, a sample goes to ICE's licensed graders. They count defects (black beans, broken beans, insect damage), check bean size, and roast and *cup* it, which means tasting it the way a buyer would. Coffee that passes becomes "certified stock." Coffee that fails stays physical coffee that just can't be used to settle the contract.
- **Not all origins are equal.** Different origins are deliverable at set premiums or discounts to the base price, so the contract builds in a quality ladder. Brazilian semi-washed arabica was only added as a deliverable origin in the last decade or so, and I'm not sure of the exact year.
- **Old beans lose value.** Certified coffee is marked down step by step the longer it sits, so a warehouse full of old beans is worth less as delivery supply than it looks.
- **Why it matters.** Most traders close out before delivery, as with oil. But the threat of delivery is what keeps the futures price tied to real coffee. And the supply that counts is the *certified* pile, not the total. In 2023, ICE certified arabica stocks fell to their lowest level in roughly two decades. That is my recollection; check the ICE reports before quoting a number. That drop helped push the front-month price up while plenty of uncertified coffee sat around.

**The twist:** deliverable supply is set by the human tasters and the grading rules, not by tonnage. If more lots fail grading, or the rules change, the squeezable supply changes even when not one bean moves. It's the same lesson as Cushing from the other direction: there the limit was tank space, here it's the grading rules.

**Meanwhile:**
**One night of frost in July 1975 permanently moved where Brazil grows its coffee.**

The mechanics:

- **The night.** On 17 to 18 July 1975, a polar air mass pushed into southern Brazil in the middle of the southern-hemisphere winter. It brought a *geada negra*, a "black frost": air so cold and dry that no white ice forms on the leaves. The plant tissue freezes from the inside and turns black within days. The state of Paraná, then Brazil's biggest coffee-growing region, took the worst of it.
- **The damage.** Estimates are usually around a billion trees killed or badly damaged. I'm confident of that order of magnitude, not of the exact figure. A frozen tree doesn't just miss one harvest. It has to be pruned back hard or replanted, and a new arabica tree takes about 3 to 4 years to produce a real crop. So the frost destroyed the *trees that make coffee*, not just one year's beans.
- **The price.** Brazil was then the dominant exporter, so the world market lost a large share of supply for several years, not one season. Arabica futures climbed through 1976 and peaked in spring 1977 at more than $3/lb, roughly four times the pre-frost level. I'm sure of the direction and rough multiple, less so of the exact peak. US shoppers ran organized coffee boycotts in 1977.
- **The aftermath.** Many Paraná growers didn't replant coffee. They switched to soybeans and other annual crops that pay back in one season. Brazilian coffee moved north into Minas Gerais and later the Cerrado, where frost risk is lower, and Minas has been the center of Brazilian coffee ever since.

**The twist:** for a tree crop, a shock destroys *capacity*, not just *output*. The supply recovery runs on biology's schedule, so prices stay high far longer than the weather event itself. And the shock can make producers leave the crop for good. That is why the coffee market still reacts to every cold-front forecast for Minas Gerais in June and July: traders aren't pricing one harvest, they're pricing the next three or four.

1) **Deeper**: the 1994 double frost, and whether moving north actually fixed the risk
2) **Sideways**: the International Coffee Agreement quota system, and what happened when it collapsed in 1989
3) **Wildcard**: why the "capacity vs output" distinction is also how to think about losing a GPU node mid-run vs losing a checkpoint
