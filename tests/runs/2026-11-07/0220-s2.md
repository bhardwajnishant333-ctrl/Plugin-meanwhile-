**The neural network inside today's Stockfish came from Japanese shogi. It's fast because it almost never thinks from scratch.**

In 2018 Yu Nasu built NNUE for computer shogi. The name stands for "Efficiently Updatable Neural Network." Hisayori Noda ported it to chess, and Stockfish 12 shipped with it in September 2020. The jump in strength was big: roughly 100 Elo by the project's own testing.

How it works: the inputs pair your king's square with the square of every other piece. Each input is one yes/no fact, like "my king on g1, enemy knight on f3." Most normal moves flip only 2 to 4 of those facts. So the first layer isn't recomputed. The engine keeps a running sum and adds or subtracts a few weight columns. That's cheap enough to run on a plain CPU at millions of positions per second. A king move is the expensive case, because every input is tied to the king's square and the whole sum has to be rebuilt. The design is a bet that kings rarely move.

The twist: it's the same bet your RSC migration is making. The cost is set by what changes on each move, not by how big the whole thing is. Server components are the part you never recompute on the client, and each `"use client"` boundary is where the running sum lives. Put a boundary too high and every click becomes a king move.

**Your lichess rating isn't one number. It's a guess plus a measure of how sure the system is about you, and that measure decides how hard each game swings you.**

Lichess uses Glicko-2, which Mark Glickman designed as an upgrade to Elo. Each player has three values:
- **Rating**: the best guess at your strength, around 1700.
- **RD (rating deviation)**: how unsure the system is about that guess. New accounts start at 1500 with a very high RD. Lichess adds the `?` (provisional) when RD is above about 110.
- **Volatility (σ)**: how erratic your results have been compared with what was expected.

What it means for you:
- **RD sets the step size.** High RD means big swings per game, low RD means small ones. That's why fresh accounts jump 150 points in a weekend.
- **Your opponent's RD matters too.** If you beat someone with a `?`, the system trusts that result less and moves you less.
- **Taking a break makes RD grow again.** Your rating doesn't drop, but your next games swing harder.
- **Streaky play raises volatility.** A tilt session of five losses followed by five wins tells the system you're unpredictable, so later results move you more.

The twist: this is a Kalman filter, the same math that guided Apollo. It works like this:
1. Keep an estimate and an uncertainty.
2. Make a prediction.
3. See the result.
4. Correct the estimate in proportion to how unsure you were.

Your GPS does the same thing. So does every "how confident are we?" problem in engineering. Elo has a fixed step size and treats every player as equally well known. Glicko assumes it might be wrong about you, and that's why it converges faster.

1) **Deeper**: how volatility shows when "1700 blitz" is really a 1600 player and an 1800 player depending on mood
2) **Sideways**: Microsoft's TrueSkill, the same idea for Xbox team matchmaking, where it has to work out who carried whom
3) **Wildcard**: why chess.com and lichess ratings don't convert cleanly, and what that says about any metric shared across two populations

**You can't convert lichess to chess.com with a formula. A rating only means anything inside its own pool of players.**

A rating predicts one thing: your expected score against other people on the same site. The zero point is arbitrary. Lichess starts new players at 1500. Chess.com has used different starting values over the years and now asks new players to pick their level. After that, each pool drifts on its own based on who joins, who quits and who farms points. A 1700 on lichess and a 1700 on chess.com are two different measurements that happen to share a scale.

The only way to compare them is to look at people who play on both sites. Tables built that way, like the survey-based ones from ChessGoals, put lichess blitz a few hundred points above chess.com at club level, with the gap shrinking near the top. Treat those as rough figures, not exact ones. The gap isn't a constant: it changes along the scale because the two sites have different mixes of players at each level.

The catch is that people who play on both sites aren't typical of either. They tend to be more serious, more active and better at online chess in general. So any conversion table is built from an unusual group and then applied to everyone. Schools hit the same problem converting SAT scores to ACT scores ("score equating"), and so does anyone comparing grades between two schools.

Same trap in code: p95 latency from two different monitoring tools, a benchmark run on someone else's machine, or a conversion lift from another company's A/B test. A number shared across two populations only means something if the people (or requests, or machines) behind it are alike. They usually aren't.
