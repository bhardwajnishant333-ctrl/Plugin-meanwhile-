**Meanwhile:**
**That handy `.if(condition) { $0.modifier() }` helper many SwiftUI codebases use can quietly wipe your view's state.**

SwiftUI tracks each view by *structural identity*, meaning where it sits in the view's code structure. `@ViewBuilder` turns an `if/else` into `_ConditionalContent`, which has two separate branches. When the condition flips, SwiftUI treats the result as a *different view*. The old one is removed and a new one is created. Any `@State` inside resets, `onAppear` fires again, and instead of animating the change you get a fade between two views. Apple covers this in the WWDC21 talk "Demystify SwiftUI". The `.if` extension hides an `if/else` inside something that looks like a plain modifier, so call sites never show the identity change.

**The twist:** the fix often makes the code *simpler*. Put the condition inside the modifier's value, not around the view. Write `.opacity(isHidden ? 0 : 1)` or `.padding(compact ? 4 : 12)`. The view keeps one identity, keeps its state, and animates the change smoothly. If a view "randomly" loses state or animates oddly, look for a branch near it.

**In 1699 Amontons said friction depends only on how hard you press, not on how much surface touches. Climbing rubber ignores that rule, and slab technique is built on the exception.**

The school law works for hard materials like metal on metal. Their real contact is just tiny high points, so a bigger surface doesn't add grip. Rubber is soft and viscoelastic. Under load it flows into the rock's texture at several sizes, from grain-sized bumps down to micro-roughness. (Bo Persson's rubber-friction theory from the early 2000s models this in detail.) Grip then comes from two sources. **Adhesion:** more real contact gives more grip. **Hysteresis:** the rubber deforms over each bump and loses energy as it does. So for rubber, contact area really does matter, and friction coefficients above 1 are possible. I don't have a reliable measured value for climbing rubber on rock, so treat any exact number you see with caution.

**The twist:** this is the physics behind the slab coach's line "drop your heel, trust your feet." A low heel presses more sole flat against the rock, and staying still gives the rubber time to sink into the texture. Toeing in on a smear and shifting around does the opposite. Cold "send temps" help mostly because your *skin* sweats less. Whether cold helps the *rubber* is a trade-off, since stiffer rubber conforms less, and I wouldn't claim more than that.

1) **Deeper:** why rubber grip changes with sliding speed and temperature (the same reason tire compounds are tuned to a temperature range)
2) **Sideways:** skin friction: why fingertips can lose grip when they're *too* dry, not only when they're sweaty
3) **Wildcard:** how SwiftUI's animation interpolation works: why `withAnimation` can animate any `Animatable` value, not just positions

**Fingertip grip peaks at a middle level of moisture. Bone-dry skin can slip almost as badly as sweaty skin.**

Your fingertip pad works much like shoe rubber. Its outer layer, the stratum corneum, is mostly keratin. When dry, it is fairly stiff and touches the hold only at its high points, so the real contact area is small. A little moisture softens that layer, it spreads into the rock's texture, and contact area and adhesion rise, so friction goes *up*. Past a certain point, extra water forms a slippery film between skin and rock, and friction falls again. The result is a peak in the middle. Finger-pad friction research has measured this rise-then-fall pattern; Michael Adams and colleagues wrote a well-known 2013 review in the *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*. Some work also suggests fingertips adjust their moisture level while gripping. I'm less sure of the details there.

**The twist:** "more chalk" isn't always the answer. Chalk's job is to bring wet skin *down* toward the peak. If your skin is already dry, glassy or split, it can push you further past it. That's why some dry-skinned climbers use moisturizing skin balms between sessions, and why breathing on your fingertips before a crimp isn't purely superstition. Lab results on how much chalk actually helps are *mixed*. I remember studies on both sides and wouldn't call the question settled.

1) **Deeper:** why your skin's grip changes over a session: wear, heat, and fingertips going glassy
2) **Sideways:** same idea in iOS: why reacting to touch input too often (debouncing gestures, `onChange` firing too much) has its own best middle point
3) **Wildcard:** why the rock type matters: granite, sandstone and limestone grip differently, and plastic holds in a gym differ again

**Meanwhile:**
**Your skin doesn't run out of strength over a session. It slowly loses the right moisture and thickness.**

Here is what is reasonably well understood. Each attempt rubs off some of the outer layer, the stratum corneum. Thinner skin hurts sooner and splits more easily. Hard effort and friction warm your fingertips, warm skin sweats more, and sweat pushes you past the moisture peak from last time, which is why your grip fades on attempts close together. Those layers regrow on a timescale of days to a couple of weeks, not overnight. I'm not sure of the exact turnover rate, so treat that as a rough figure.

Some of this is climbers' craft knowledge rather than lab science. Examples: resting a few minutes for "skin rest" rather than muscle rest, sanding calluses so they don't catch and tear, and using antiperspirants on very sweaty hands. These are widely used but not rigorously tested, as far as I know. The rock changes too. Popular problems get polished by thousands of hands and feet. Some classic Fontainebleau sandstone is famously glassy because of it, which matches everything from the rubber topic: less texture means less to mold into.

**The twist:** your two limits recover on different clocks. Muscles bounce back in minutes. Skin moisture comes back in minutes too, but skin *thickness* takes days. So on a session where you fail from skin, more rest between goes helps less than stopping early.
