**If your matcher works only from amounts, it's quietly solving an NP-complete problem.**

Say a payout of $4,812.37 covers some unknown set of charges, minus fees and refunds. Finding which ledger entries add up to that total is subset-sum. It's a close relative of knapsack, which was on Karp's 1972 list of 21 NP-complete problems. There's a dynamic-programming fix that scales with n × the target in cents. A $50k payout is 5 million cents, so that's 5 million steps per ledger entry. Doable, but slow.

**The twist:** speed isn't the real danger. When you have lots of charges with similar or round amounts, several different subsets can add up to the exact same total. Same birthday-problem effect as hash collisions. So a matcher that only looks at amounts can return an answer that balances to the cent and is still wrong. The total gives you no way to tell.

The practical escape: for automatic payouts, Stripe will list the exact balance transactions in a payout if you pass the payout ID. Then it's a join, not a search. Worth checking whether the agent's rewrite uses that, or whether it's guessing from amounts.

**"San Francisco sourdough" bacteria aren't really from San Francisco.**

In 1971, USDA researchers Kline and Sugihara isolated a lactic-acid bacterium from SF sourdough and named it *Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*. In 2020 it was reclassified as *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*. It has since turned up in starters across Europe, Asia and the rest of the US. It's one of the most common sourdough bacteria anywhere.

Then the Global Sourdough Project (Landis et al., *eLife*, 2021) sequenced about 500 starters, mostly from the US and Europe. Geography explained very little of what lived in them. A starter from Oregon could match one from Belgium more closely than the one next door.

What it does have is a partner trick. It breaks down maltose and lets glucose leak out. A yeast it often lives with, *Kazachstania humilis*, can't use maltose at all, so it lives on that leaked glucose. Neither competes for the other's food. That's a big part of why the pair stays stable for decades.

**The twist:** your starter is shaped mostly by what you do: feeding schedule, temperature, hydration and flour. Your city matters much less. There's also a 2020 study suggesting bakers' hands carry more starter-type microbes than other people's. I'm less sure how strong that effect is. If it holds, the microbes may be moving from starter to baker as much as the other way.

**Meanwhile:**
**A starter is a fortress, and the bread inherits the walls.**

It has three layers of defense.

1. **Acid.** A ripe starter sits around pH 3.5 to 4, from lactic and acetic acid. Acetic acid does most of the antifungal work. Undissociated, it slips through mold cell membranes and acidifies the inside of the cell. Lactic acid alone is much weaker against mold. That's why a cooler, stiffer starter, which leans acetic, tends to make bread that resists mold longer.
2. **Chemistry.** Some sourdough lactobacilli make antifungal compounds such as phenyllactic acid. One strain isolated from an industrial sourdough, *L. reuteri* LTH2584, makes reutericyclin (Gänzle et al., 2000). That one is an antibiotic against other bacteria, not mold. So the starter is fighting rival bacteria and fungi with different weapons.
3. **Crowding.** A mature starter holds roughly a billion lactic acid bacteria per gram, outnumbering the yeast about 100 to 1. A stray spore lands in a place that's already full, sour and short on sugar.

**The twist:** these defenses carry into the loaf. Sourdough acid holds off "rope", a *Bacillus* spoilage that turns bread sticky and stringy. Commercial bakeries add calcium propionate to get that protection. Long-fermented sourdough gets it for free. Spiking a dough with commercial yeast for speed dilutes it. You get the rise, but less of the fortress.

1. **Deeper**: why a neglected starter grows "hooch", and whether pouring it off or stirring it in actually changes the bread
2. **Sideways**: how 19th-century Alaskan gold prospectors got called "sourdoughs" for the starters they carried to keep alive
