**Meanwhile:**
A swarm of about 10,000 bees picks its new home without any bee ever comparing two sites.

**The mechanism** (from Thomas Seeley's field work, written up in *Honeybee Democracy*, 2010):
- A few hundred scouts fly out and inspect possible cavities. They prefer roughly 40 litres of space, a small entrance near the bottom, a few metres off the ground.
- Back on the swarm cluster, a scout waggle-dances for her site. A better site gets a longer dance. Each time she goes back and returns, she dances less, and eventually she stops. Seeley called this dance attrition. It means no scout can keep pushing her site forever, so bad sites lose support on their own.
- Dancing recruits more scouts, so the best site gains visitors faster than the others.
- **The decision itself happens at the site, not on the swarm.** When a scout sees about 20 to 30 other scouts at the cavity at the same time (Seeley and Visscher's quorum figure), she switches modes. She flies back and starts "piping," a signal that tells the swarm to warm its flight muscles to about 35°C. Roughly an hour later the whole swarm lifts off.
- They also send stop signals: a scout head-butts a bee dancing for a rival site and buzzes at her (Seeley et al., *Science*, 2012). This is the same cross-inhibition neurons use when a brain chooses between options.

**The twist:** quorum is not consensus. The swarm doesn't wait for everyone to agree, only for enough bees to be at one site at once. That trades accuracy for speed, and a swarm hanging on a branch can't wait long. The failure mode will feel familiar: occasionally two sites reach quorum at nearly the same moment. The swarm takes off, gets pulled two ways, and settles back on the branch to try again. That's a split-brain followed by re-election, with no coordinator involved.

**Meanwhile:**
A drone's family tree follows the Fibonacci sequence.

**The mechanism:** honeybees are haplodiploid. A fertilized egg becomes a female (a worker or a queen). An unfertilized egg becomes a drone. So a drone has a mother and no father, but his mother had both parents, which is how he ends up with a grandfather.

Count back through the generations: 1 parent, 2 grandparents, 3 great-grandparents, then 5, 8, 13. Each female has two parents and each male has one, which produces exactly the Fibonacci numbers. Drones also carry only one set of chromosomes, so all of a drone's sperm is genetically identical.

That leads to an odd result. Two sisters with the same father share about 75% of their genes, more than the 50% a mother shares with her own daughter. In 1964 W. D. Hamilton used this to explain why workers give up reproducing and raise sisters instead. It became the classic textbook story of kin selection.

**The twist:** the queen undermines it. On her mating flights she mates with many drones, often 10 to 20. So most workers in a hive are half-sisters, and the average relatedness drops to around 0.3. The 75% argument mostly falls apart. Termites are a second problem for it: they have ordinary two-parent genetics and are just as social.

The better prediction came later. Ratnieks worked it out in 1988 and Ratnieks and Visscher confirmed it in 1989. Workers can lay unfertilized eggs that become drones. Because of the queen's many mates, a worker is more closely related to the queen's sons (her brothers) than to the sons of other workers, who are mostly half-nephews. So workers find eggs laid by other workers and eat them. This is called worker policing. The hive's peace is enforced, not simply inherited.

1. **Deeper:** how workers tell a queen-laid egg from a worker-laid one, and the "anarchist" bee strains that dodge the policing.
2. **Sideways:** why chickens and birds use a different sex-determination system (ZW), and what that means for breeding.
3. **Wildcard:** the 1990s British debate about whether the Isle of Wight disease ever really ended. I'm not fully sure of the details, so I'd flag what's solid and what isn't.
