**Meanwhile:**
In the 1700s nobody could read how hot a kiln was. A mercury thermometer fails at about 357°C, because the mercury boils, and the glass would soften long before stoneware heat anyway. Potters judged by the color of the glow and by gut feel.

Wedgwood's answer was to let clay measure clay. He fired small, standard cylinders of clay alongside the ware. Clay shrinks when heated and doesn't grow back, so each cylinder came out smaller the hotter it got. He slid the cooled cylinders into a tapered brass groove. The further one slid in, the hotter the fire had been. He presented this "pyrometer" to the Royal Society in 1782 and was elected a Fellow in 1783.

He also got the numbers badly wrong. When he converted his scale to Fahrenheit, he put the melting point of cast iron at about 17,977°F. The real figure is closer to 2,100–2,200°F. That exact number is the one usually quoted; I haven't checked his original paper myself. But his gauge was still useful. It was consistent from one firing to the next, even though his conversion to everyday degrees was off.

**The twist:** you probably use his idea's descendants every firing. Pyrometric cones, standardized by Edward Orton Jr. in the 1890s, don't measure temperature either. They measure *heatwork*, meaning temperature and time together, because that's what clay and glaze actually respond to. A slow firing to a lower peak can bend the same cone as a fast firing to a higher one. Like Wedgwood, potters measure what the heat *does* to clay instead of how hot the kiln *is*.

**Meanwhile:**
Orton's chart lists two temperatures for a cone 6 bend. With a final climb of 60°C per hour, cone 6 bends at about 1222°C. With a final climb of 150°C per hour, it needs about 1243°C. That's roughly 20 degrees apart, and the only difference is how fast the kiln got there.

The reason is that a firing isn't one event. It's a set of slow processes that take time:

- **Melting into glass:** feldspar and other fluxes melt into a sticky glass. It creeps into the pores between clay particles and dissolves some of the silica around it.
- **Shrinking and hardening:** the particles get pulled together. The pot shrinks and grows dense and hard. This is vitrification, and it's why a stoneware bowl holds water.
- **Speed:** heat makes all of this go much faster, but it never happens instantly. At a slightly lower temperature you get the same result if you wait longer.

So a cone is really a small block of glaze-like material going through the same process as your pot, and it's a clock as much as a thermometer. It's also why a "soak" works: holding the kiln at peak heat for 10–20 minutes lets glazes finish melting, bubbles rise out, and pinholes heal.

**The twist:** not everything in the kiln runs on heatwork. Quartz in the clay body changes its crystal shape at about 573°C, and it does that instantly and at the same temperature every time, however fast or slow you go. On the way down it shrinks suddenly. If one part of a bowl passes 573°C before another part does, the strain can crack it (potters call this "dunting"). So a firing runs on two kinds of clock. Some changes depend on *how long* the clay has had (vitrification, glaze melt). Others depend only on *what temperature* it reaches (quartz). Cooling is where the second kind gets you, which is why wide, flat bowls are the ones most likely to dunt.
