**The standard method for proving CAN messages meet their deadlines was wrong for 13 years, and cars shipped using it.**

In 1994 Ken Tindell and Alan Burns published response-time analysis for CAN, a way to calculate the worst-case latency of each message ID. It became the textbook method and was built into commercial tools (Volvo's Volcano tooling, as I recall). In 2007 Davis, Burns, Bril and Lukkien published "CAN schedulability analysis: Refuted, revisited and revised." The original analysis treated each message as if its worst case came from its first instance. A frame can't be preempted once it's on the bus, though. So a lower-priority frame that's already sending can delay a message long enough that its *next* instance is queued before the first one finishes. In some valid configurations the old math gave latencies that were too short, which made it look safe when it wasn't.

**The twist:** the bug only appears when a message's response time comes close to its own period, which is exactly when a bus is near full load and the analysis matters most. As long as there was spare bus capacity, the flaw stayed hidden.

**In 2022 a bar-tailed godwit just 5 months old flew from Alaska to Tasmania without stopping: about 13,560 km in roughly 11 days.**

It doesn't eat, drink or land the whole way, and probably sleeps only in short bursts while flying. To make that possible, godwits rebuild their own bodies before leaving. Piersma and Gill found in 1998 that they shrink their gizzard, intestines, liver and kidneys, then pack on fat until it's roughly half their body weight. The digestive system is dead weight during the flight, so they cut it down and regrow it after landing. They also time departure to catch tailwinds from passing storm systems.

**The twist:** this was a juvenile on its first migration, with no parent leading it. The route, the fuel budget and the weather timing seem to be built in from birth rather than learned.

**IQ scores in Norway went up for decades and then started falling, and nobody knows why.**

Average IQ scores rose about 3 points per decade through most of the 20th century; this is called the Flynn effect. Norway tests almost every young man at military conscription, and in that data the rise reverses for men born after about 1975. In 2018 Bratsberg and Rogeberg (published in PNAS) compared brothers within the same families, and younger brothers scored lower than older ones. That rules out the usual explanations about who is having children or who moved into the country. The cause is something in the environment that changed between one sibling's childhood and the next.

**The open question:** what it is. Candidates include changes in schooling, in reading habits and media, in what the tests actually reward, and in nutrition. None of them is proven. The rise was never fully explained either.

1) **Deeper**: what IQ tests measure that changes this fast
2) **Sideways**: the Lindy effect, about why old things tend to last longer

**For some things, every extra year they survive raises how long they're expected to keep going.**

That's the Lindy effect, named after Lindy's deli in New York. Albert Goldman wrote about it in 1964, Benoit Mandelbrot later put it in mathematical terms, and Nassim Taleb popularized it in *Antifragile* (2012). It only holds for things that don't wear out the way bodies do, such as books, ideas, technologies and institutions. If their lifespans follow a power-law distribution, expected remaining life grows in proportion to current age. A 40-year-old book is expected to last roughly another 40 years. J. Richard Gott used the same reasoning when he visited the Berlin Wall in 1969, eight years after it was built. He estimated it would stand another 2.7 to 24 years, with 50% confidence. It fell 20 years later, and he published the method in *Nature* in 1993.

**The twist:** age only counts as evidence when nothing is wearing the thing down. A silicon part fails from physical wear, so Lindy doesn't apply to it. A protocol is different. CAN dates from 1986, and that age is a reason to expect it to outlast most newer buses. Old code that has survived years of real use is evidence too, which makes rewriting it a bet against its track record.

1) **Deeper**: Gott's "Copernican" method, and when it gives silly answers
2) **Sideways**: why lithium cells wear out with use, and what that means for your controller's battery estimates
3) **Wildcard**: why the QWERTY keyboard story is mostly a myth
