**Meanwhile:**
The friction law from school, "friction doesn't depend on contact area," is wrong for exactly the two things you climb with: skin and rubber.

**How it works:** Amontons wrote the law down in 1699, and Leonardo had noted the same thing in his notebooks about 200 years earlier. It holds for hard, rough surfaces like metal on metal. Their real contact happens only at tiny peaks, and the total peak area grows in step with how hard you press. Skin and shoe rubber are soft and slightly sticky. They flow into the rock's texture and hold on through adhesion, so friction depends on how much surface is actually touching. Lab studies of skin friction find that grip grows more slowly than load. In the usual model friction goes with load to a power under 1 (often around 2/3), though the exact number depends on the study and on moisture. That's why an open hand on a sloper beats a tense, pointy grip: you're adding contact area, not just pushing harder.

**The twist:** if grip grows more slowly than load, then doubling your weight on a hold gives you less than double the friction. A heavier climber gets less grip per kilo than a lighter one, even with the same strength-to-weight ratio. On slopers, weight costs you twice: once in the strength it takes to hold it, and again in the physics of the hold itself.

Chalk doesn't add grip. At best it removes something that was costing you grip, and sometimes it costs grip itself.

**How it works:** climbing chalk is magnesium carbonate, and its main job is soaking up sweat. Skin friction doesn't keep rising as skin gets drier, though. It peaks at a middle level of moisture. A little moisture softens the outer layer of skin, so it flows into the rock and makes more real contact, which ties back to the contact-area point from before. Too much moisture forms a slippery film. Too little leaves skin stiff and glassy, with less contact. The lab results on chalk are mixed. Some studies measured higher friction with chalk and at least one measured lower, depending on the skin's starting state and the rock. I'm confident the results conflict, but I won't quote exact figures from memory. There's also a second effect: a loose layer of extra powder can act like tiny ball bearings between skin and stone. That's one reason brushing holds helps.

**The twist:** chalk works by pushing you toward the ideal moisture level, not by making you as dry as possible. If your hands sweat, chalk moves you toward the peak. If your skin is already dry, more chalk moves you past it. That's why some dry-skinned climbers use hydrating products, and why rechalking before every move can make things worse.
