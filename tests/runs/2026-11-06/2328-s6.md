Your miso is made with a mold that came from one of the most carcinogenic fungi on Earth.

Koji mold, *Aspergillus oryzae*, is a domesticated relative of *Aspergillus flavus*. That's the mold that makes aflatoxin, a potent natural liver carcinogen and the reason peanut and corn crops get tested. The two are so close genetically that many mycologists treat oryzae as a tamed lineage of flavus rather than a fully separate wild species. What sets them apart is the aflatoxin gene cluster: in koji strains it's broken by mutations and deletions, so it can't finish making the toxin. Nobody designed that. Over centuries, Japanese spore merchants kept propagating whichever cultures made good sake and miso and didn't make people sick. In the Muromachi period (around the 1400s, from memory), Kyoto koji makers were organized enough to run a guild with a monopoly on the trade. In 2006 the Brewing Society of Japan named *A. oryzae* the "national fungus."

The twist: the dangerous machinery is mostly still in the genome, just switched off. Some koji strains still carry pieces of the aflatoxin cluster, which is why industrial producers still screen their strains today. Domestication didn't take the toxin genes out. It kept the useful parts working and left the harmful pathway in place, unable to run.

Myth: old cathedral windows are thicker at the bottom because glass is a liquid that slowly flows. It isn't, and they aren't, at least not for that reason.

Medieval window glass was mostly made with the crown process: molten glass was spun into a wide disc that came out uneven in thickness. Glaziers often set the heavy edge at the bottom for stability, but not always. Some old panes are thicker at the top or the side. In 1998 Edgar Zanotto calculated, in the American Journal of Physics, how long room-temperature cathedral glass would take to sag visibly. The answer was far longer than the age of the universe. Glass is effectively a rigid solid.

The twist: the myth is wrong, but the question behind it is still open. Glass has the disordered atomic structure of a liquid and behaves like a solid, and physics still has no settled theory of what the glass transition actually is. In 1995 the Nobel laureate Philip Anderson called it "the deepest and most interesting unsolved problem in solid state theory." The windows are boring. The material is not.

The best-known financial bubble story has almost no evidence that it ruined anyone.

The popular version comes mostly from Charles Mackay's 1841 book *Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds*: a whole nation going bulb-crazy, houses traded for one tulip, ruined merchants, suicides. Mackay relied heavily on Dutch moralizing pamphlets printed right after the crash, religious propaganda warning against greed. When the historian Anne Goldgar went back to the archives for her 2007 book *Tulipmania*, she found no documented bankruptcies caused by tulips and no evidence of the suicides. The trade really did collapse, around early February 1637 in Haarlem. But it was a fairly small circle of merchants and craftsmen dealing mostly in tavern trading clubs, and many deals were essentially futures contracts for bulbs still in the ground. When prices crashed, most of those contracts were simply never settled. Haarlem's authorities let buyers walk away for a small fraction of the price, about 3.5% if I remember correctly. The economist Peter Garber also argued that prices for the rarest bulbs followed normal patterns for scarce new varieties.

The twist: the real bubble was the story. For almost 200 years after the crash, a moral fable did better than the data, because "crowds go mad" is easier to remember and retell than "some contracts went unenforced." The story you hear about a failure is often shaped by whoever had a lesson to sell.

1) Deeper: why the Semper Augustus tulip was so prized: its famous streaks came from a virus that was slowly killing it
2) Sideways: the South Sea Bubble of 1720, where Isaac Newton really did lose a fortune
