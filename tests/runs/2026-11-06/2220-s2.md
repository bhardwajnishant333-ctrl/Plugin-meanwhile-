**The longest forced win in a 7-piece chess endgame is mate in 549 moves. No human could find it, and under the 50-move rule a player who did would only get a draw.**

In 2012 Vladimir Makhnychev and Victor Zakharov used Moscow State University's Lomonosov supercomputer to solve every legal position with 7 pieces or fewer. The result, the Lomonosov tablebases, is about 140 TB. The 549-move record comes, as I remember it, from rook, bishop and knight against queen and knight. I'm confident about the number but less sure of the exact pieces.

The method runs backward. Start from every checkmate, step back one move, label those positions "mate in 1", and repeat. Nobody searches forward from the start. Each position gets its value from where it ends up.

The twist: tablebases proved that whole families of endgames are wins for one side even though they look like aimless shuffling. FIDE tried allowing extra moves for certain endgames in the 1980s, then went back to a flat 50 in 1992. The rule now protects how humans play, not what's actually true about the position.

**Redux's strictness wasn't originally about code style. It was the cost of a demo.**

In July 2015 Dan Abramov gave a talk at React Europe called "Live React: Hot Reloading with Time Travel," and he wrote Redux so the demo would work. Time travel needs three things. State changes only through plain action objects. Reducers are pure functions. State is never mutated. With those in place, replaying the action log from the initial state rebuilds any moment exactly. It's the tablebase idea in reverse: a position's value comes from its history instead of its future.

The twist: all the boilerplate people complained about was paying for that log. Zustand drops the requirement, so `set` can be called from anywhere, including async code and closures, and the log goes away. Its `devtools` middleware still connects to the Redux DevTools extension. Pass an action name as the third argument to `set`, or every update shows up as "anonymous". I'm not sure how well jumping between states works under the middleware, so check that before you count on it.

1) Deeper: why mutating state silently breaks React's re-render checks
2) Sideways: event sourcing, the same replay idea used to run banks
3) Wildcard: the chess engine that learned endgames without a tablebase

**Neural chess engines could reach a winning endgame and then fail to finish it. They weren't confused. They just didn't care how long the win took.**

AlphaZero (DeepMind, 2017) learned chess purely from self-play, with no opening book and no tablebase. It beat Stockfish 8 by 28 wins, 72 draws and no losses. Its open-source follow-up, Leela Chess Zero, showed a strange habit: in positions that were completely winning, it sometimes shuffled pieces instead of going for mate. Its value network predicts *whether* it wins, not *how fast*. A win in 5 and a win in 50 score the same, so it had no reason to hurry. Sometimes it shuffled into a 50-move draw.

The fix, as I understand it, was a "moves left" output added around 2020, so the engine also predicts how long the game will last and prefers shorter wins. I'm confident about the behavior and less sure of the exact date.

The twist: this is reward specification in its plainest form. What you score is what you get, and the leftover behavior fills whatever you didn't specify. Your refactor is being judged on "tests pass," too.

2) Sideways: reward hacking, where agents found the scorer's loopholes
