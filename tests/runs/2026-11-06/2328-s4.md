**Meanwhile:**
Before October 1987, SPX options had almost no smile. After the crash, the skew showed up and has never gone away.

**Mechanism:** Before the crash, implied vol across SPX strikes was roughly flat, which is what Black-Scholes assumes. On October 19, 1987, the S&P 500 fell about 20% and S&P futures fell about 29%. Jackwerth and Rubinstein (Journal of Finance, 1996) worked out how unlikely a 29% one-day drop is under a lognormal model with 20% annual vol: about a 27-sigma event, with a probability around 10^-160. The model said it couldn't happen, and it happened anyway. Within months, OTM puts were trading at much higher implied vols than ATM options. That downward slope has been part of index options ever since. (I'm confident about the before-and-after shape. I wouldn't quote exact pre-1987 skew numbers from memory.)

**Twist:** The skew isn't a law of nature. It's a price the market set after one bad day and kept charging for almost 40 years. When you put on an SPX vertical, the further-OTM put is priced on the steeper part of that curve. Part of what your spread costs, or collects, is the market's memory of one Monday in 1987.

1) **Deeper:** Breeden-Litzenberger (1978). Take the second derivative of option prices with respect to strike and you get the market's implied probability distribution. You can read the crash fear straight off your chain.
2) **Sideways:** LoRA works because fine-tuning has a surprisingly low "intrinsic dimension." That's the paper behind what your 8 A100s are doing right now.
3) **Wildcard:** Volmageddon, February 5, 2018. XIV lost about 96% in one evening, and its own prospectus had described how that could happen.

**Meanwhile:**
About 200 parameters got RoBERTa to roughly 90% of full fine-tuning performance on one task. That's 200 numbers, not 200 million.

**Mechanism:** Li et al. (Uber AI, 2018) defined "intrinsic dimension" as the smallest random subspace where training still reaches 90% of full performance. You freeze the model and train only a small vector d, which a fixed random matrix projects up into the full weight space. On MNIST, d of roughly 750 was enough. Aghajanyan, Zettlemoyer and Gupta (2020) ran the same test on pretrained language models. RoBERTa-large on the MRPC paraphrase task needed only about 200 dimensions. Two more findings matter here:
- **Bigger pretrained models had lower intrinsic dimension, not higher.**
- **The longer pretraining ran, the lower it went.**

LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) applied this directly. If the update lives in a small subspace, write it as a low-rank product ΔW = BA and train only B and A. On GPT-3 175B, they reported about 10,000x fewer trainable parameters and about 3x less GPU memory. Rank 1 or 2 on the attention projections was already competitive on some tasks. (Those numbers are from the papers. Your 7B setup may behave differently.)

**Twist:** Pretraining is what makes fine-tuning cheap. The base model has already built the features, so your run is mostly choosing among them, not learning new ones. For your run: if eval doesn't improve when you raise the rank from 8 to 64, rank isn't your bottleneck. Look at your data first. The paper's evidence points there.

1) **Deeper:** Why low rank sometimes fails. Later work found LoRA struggles more on tasks that need genuinely new knowledge, like continued pretraining on math or code, than on tasks that change behavior.
2) **Sideways:** Grokking. Small networks sit at near-perfect training accuracy for thousands of steps, then suddenly generalize. Weight decay is the suspected cause.
3) **Wildcard:** Long-Term Capital Management, 1998. Two Nobel laureates, about 25x leverage, and a model that treated a Russian default as nearly impossible.

**Meanwhile:**
LTCM's trades mostly converged in the end. The fund was gone before they did.

**Mechanism:** John Meriwether started Long-Term Capital Management in 1994. Myron Scholes and Robert Merton were partners, and both won the 1997 Nobel in economics for options pricing. Net returns were roughly 40%+ in 1995 and 1996 and about 17% in 1997. The core trades were convergence bets, such as buying cheap off-the-run Treasuries against expensive on-the-run ones. Each spread was tiny, so they levered it: about $4.7B of equity supporting about $125B of balance sheet (roughly 25x), plus derivatives with notional value above $1 trillion. They were also big sellers of long-dated equity index vol, enough that the Street called them the "central bank of volatility." Russia defaulted on August 17, 1998. Investors fled to safety, and every spread LTCM was betting would narrow widened at once, across countries and asset classes that their models treated as mostly unrelated. On August 21 alone they lost about $550M. By September the fund had lost about $4.6B, and the New York Fed organized 14 banks to put in $3.6B. Bear Stearns refused to join.

**Twist:** The failure wasn't a wrong forecast. Their risk models measured diversification using correlations from calm markets. In a crisis everyone exits the same trades together, those correlations jump toward 1, and liquidity disappears just when you need it. Being right on a 2-year horizon doesn't matter if you can't survive the next 2 weeks. Your SPX verticals have a defined max loss, which LTCM's book never had. The same lesson still applies across several spreads: in a selloff they behave like one big position. Count them as one when you size.

1) **Deeper:** The "central bank of volatility" trade. How LTCM sold 5-year index vol, and why vol sellers today still get caught by the same mechanics.
2) **Sideways:** Bitter Lesson, Sutton 2019. Seventy years of AI history says general methods plus compute beat hand-built cleverness. That bears on your 7B fine-tune too.
3) **Wildcard:** The 1983 Soviet false alarm. Stanislav Petrov's early-warning system reported five incoming US missiles. He judged it a malfunction, mainly because the attack was too small to make sense.
