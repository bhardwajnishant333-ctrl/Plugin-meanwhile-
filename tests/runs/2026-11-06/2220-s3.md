In 1712, Sweden had a February 30th, and a rolling migration that went wrong is the reason.

Catholic Europe switched from the Julian to the Gregorian calendar in one cutover. In October 1582, Thursday the 4th was followed by Friday the 15th. Sweden decided to do it gradually instead. From 1700 to 1740 it would skip every leap day, so the 11-day gap would close a little at a time with no big jump.

It skipped the leap day in 1700. Then, in the middle of the Great Northern War, it forgot to skip 1704 and 1708. For years Sweden's dates matched neither the Julian nor the Gregorian calendar. In 1712 the king rolled back by adding a 30th of February, which put Sweden back on Julian. It finally made a clean cutover in 1753, when February 17 was followed by March 1.

The gradual plan was the riskier one. It had dozens of points where something could fail, no one checking each step, and an in-between state that matched no other system. A single cutover can only go wrong once.

**Your indexes can be corrupted without a single changed byte, if the operating system's sort rules change underneath them.**

By default, Postgres asks the C library, glibc, how to order text. In August 2018, glibc 2.28 shipped a big update to its locale data, and the sort order changed for many strings in common locales like en_US.UTF-8, especially ones with punctuation or spaces. A B-tree index built under the old rules is suddenly out of order under the new ones. Lookups can miss rows that exist, and a unique index can let duplicates in. Postgres reports no error.

This matters for your upgrade because pg_upgrade copies or links index files as they are and does not rebuild them. If your PG 16 image runs on a different glibc than the PG 14 image, you can be exposed. The big change was in 2.28. I'm not sure whether later glibc versions are entirely safe.

Check it: run `ldd --version` in both images. To test an index, use `bt_index_check` from the amcheck extension. Watch out for one trap: PG 15+ warns about a "collation version mismatch", and `ALTER DATABASE … REFRESH COLLATION VERSION` makes the warning go away without fixing anything. The fix is REINDEX.

**Every UK tax return still carries a compatibility patch from 1752.**

In medieval and early-modern England, the legal year began on 25 March, Lady Day. It was one of the quarter days when rents and taxes fell due. In 1752 Britain moved to the Gregorian calendar and dropped 11 days: Wednesday 2 September was followed by Thursday 14 September. The Treasury would not accept a tax year 11 days short, so it pushed the tax year forward to match. 25 March plus 11 days put the start on 5 April.

Then came 1800. It was a leap year in the old Julian calendar but not in the Gregorian one, so the Treasury moved the start one more day, to 6 April. In 1900 the same mismatch came up again, and the date stayed put. As far as I know, no one has documented why.

So the date is a migration shim that no one removed. It got updated once, then the updating stopped, and it has been part of the system ever since.

**The sharpest idea from this session:** a migration can finish with no errors and still be wrong, when the data hasn't changed but the rules for reading it have. That's what happened with Sweden's calendar, the UK tax year and glibc collations.
