Your bike doesn't stay up because of gyroscopic forces or trail. In 2011, researchers proved it by building a bike that had neither and still balanced itself.

**Mechanism:** Kooijman, Schwab, Meijaard, Papadopoulos and Ruina published "A Bicycle Can Be Self-Stable Without Gyroscopic or Caster Effects" in *Science* (April 2011). They added a counter-rotating wheel next to each real wheel to cancel the gyroscopic effect. They also gave the bike a small *negative* trail, which removes the caster effect textbooks credit. Pushed riderless above a certain speed, it still caught itself when it started to fall. The stability came from where the mass sits, especially how the front assembly's center of mass is placed relative to the steering axis. When the bike leans, that mass pulls the bars toward the lean, so the wheels move back under the bike.

**Twist:** No single part is "the reason" a bike is stable. It comes from about two dozen coupled parameters working together, and each popular explanation was a factor that sometimes helps, not the cause. Your countersteer into a corner works the same way. You aren't overriding the bike. You're starting a fall that the bike's own geometry finishes.

**Meanwhile:**
On a fast descent, your bike is past the speed range where it balances itself. You're steering it upright the whole way down without noticing.

**Mechanism:** The standard model is the Whipple bicycle model. Francis Whipple worked it out in 1899, and Meijaard et al. published the checked benchmark version in *Proc. Royal Society A* in 2007. The model's motion breaks into three modes:
- **Weave:** a side-to-side wobble of steer and lean. It's unstable at low speed, which is why riding very slowly takes constant small corrections.
- **Capsize:** a slow fall into the lean. It becomes unstable *above* a certain speed.
- **Caster:** the front wheel straightening itself out. It stays stable.

The bike balances itself only between the weave speed and the capsize speed. For the benchmark bike that window is about 4.3 to 6.0 m/s, roughly 15 to 22 km/h. Your bike and body change those numbers, so treat them as the right size, not your exact window.

**Twist:** At 60 km/h you're far past the capsize speed. The bike is unstable, but the fall develops so slowly that tiny corrections you don't notice keep it upright. The thing that actually scares people at speed, speed wobble (shimmy), isn't in the Whipple model at all. It needs flexible tires and a frame that bends, which the model leaves out. The usual fix of pressing your knees against the top tube makes sense with that cause, but I wouldn't claim the physics of shimmy is fully settled.

**Meanwhile:**
The incremental models your agent is writing have their own "stable only in a window" problem, like the bike. If the lookback window is too small, data quietly goes missing. If it's too big, every run costs nearly as much as a full rebuild.

**Mechanism:** A typical dbt incremental model filters with `is_incremental()`, often written as `updated_at > (select max(updated_at) from {{ this }})`. If a row arrives late with a timestamp older than that max, it falls behind the filter, and later runs never pick it up. Nothing errors and the run stays green. The table just drifts away from the source. Deleted source rows have a similar problem: plain incremental logic doesn't remove them unless you handle deletes yourself.

The usual fix is a lookback, such as `max(updated_at) - interval '3 days'`, combined with a `unique_key` so the MERGE removes duplicates. On Snowflake, that runs into how storage works. Micro-partitions can't be changed in place (each is roughly 50 to 500 MB of uncompressed data), so a MERGE that touches one row rewrites the whole partition it lives in. A wide lookback on a table that isn't clustered by date can end up touching most partitions, and the "cheap" incremental run costs about as much as the rebuild it replaced.

**Twist:** The lookback size is a tuning setting, and you can measure the right value instead of guessing. Run something like `percentile_cont(0.99) within group (order by loaded_at - event_at)` on your sources and you'll see how late data really arrives. If the result is "usually 2 hours, sometimes 9 days", you need a scheduled `--full-refresh` as a backstop, the same way a rider corrects the slow capsize fall. dbt's `incremental_predicates` config can also limit how much of the target table the MERGE scans. When the agent finishes, check the filter it wrote for each model. That's where this drift would come from.

1) Deeper: how to test whether an incremental model has drifted (compare it against a full-refresh build in a dev schema)
2) Sideways: Lamport clocks and why "max timestamp seen" is a shaky watermark in distributed systems
3) Wildcard: how British Rail's 1840s "railway time" made the whole country agree on one clock

First, a correction: in my last options I said "British Rail" made the country agree on one clock. British Rail didn't exist until 1948. The railway that actually did it was the Great Western Railway, in 1840.

Britain ran on railway time for decades before the law caught up. Parliament didn't make Greenwich Mean Time legal time until 1880.

**Mechanism:** Before the railways, every town set its clocks by its own noon. Bristol ran about 10 minutes behind London, so a single timetable couldn't work. In November 1840 the GWR put all its stations on London time. In 1847 the Railway Clearing House recommended that every railway use GMT, and most did by the end of that year. From 1852, time signals from Greenwich went out over telegraph lines. Towns resisted for a while. Bristol's Corn Exchange clock still has two minute hands, one for local time and one for "railway" time. The Statutes (Definition of Time) Act 1880 then made official what trains, telegraphs and timetables had already been using for decades. The US followed a similar path: its railroads switched to standard time zones on 18 November 1883, and Congress didn't pass a law until 1918.

**Twist:** "What time is it?" turned out to be a choice about coordination, not a fact of nature. The group that most needed one shared answer, the people running the timetables, picked it, and everyone else gradually adopted it. Your data warehouse has the same choice: event time, load time or source-system time. A single canonical timestamp for a pipeline is a choice the team makes and has to write down. dbt can't work it out from the data.

1) Deeper: the "day of two noons", and how US cities split over adopting standard time
2) Sideways: leap seconds, and why the clock standards bodies voted in 2022 to drop them by 2035
