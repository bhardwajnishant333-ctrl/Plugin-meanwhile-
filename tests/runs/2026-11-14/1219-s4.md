**Meanwhile:**
It's easiest to corner a market in something nobody can store for long.

**How a corner works:** A futures short has promised to deliver the real thing at a set place by a set date. If you're long and you also own most of the supply that qualifies for delivery, the shorts can't get onions to hand over. They have to buy their contracts back from you at whatever price you name. That's the squeeze.

**Why onions were ideal:**
- **Small crop:** the onions held in storage over winter were a small pool compared with grain, so buying up most of them was realistic for two traders.
- **One delivery point:** delivery meant onions in Chicago. Owning the Chicago stock mattered more than owning the national crop.
- **Perishable:** onions keep for months, not years, so there is no stockpile of past crops to break the squeeze. Each season's contracts stand alone, and sellers can't wait you out because their onions are rotting.

Perishability also made the reversal brutal. When Kosuga and Siegel switched to short and released their stock, the holders had to sell before the onions spoiled, and that is how a bag ended up worth less than its sack. Accounts say they also had onions shipped out of Chicago to be re-cleaned and shipped back, so the supply looked bigger than it was. I'm less sure of those details.

**The twist:** This is the same mechanism as oil on April 20, 2020, when the May WTI contract settled at −$37.63. The contract was physically delivered at one place (Cushing, Oklahoma), and storage there was nearly full. If you can't store what you're contractually forced to take, the price can drop below the value of the packaging, and then below zero. 1956 onions were the first version of negative oil.

**Meanwhile:**
In March 1980, Tiffany & Co. ran a newspaper ad attacking two Texas oil heirs. Their crime was making silver too expensive.

**What happened:** Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt, with Saudi partners, spent 1979 buying physical silver and futures contracts, and they took delivery instead of rolling the contracts forward. Estimates of how much they controlled vary widely, and I wouldn't trust any single figure. Silver went from about $6/oz in early 1979 to about $50 in January 1980. People queued to melt down their grandmothers' silverware, and Tiffany's chairman called the Hunts' hoarding "unconscionable" in print.

Then COMEX changed the rules mid-game:
- **January 7, 1980:** "Silver Rule 7" sharply limited how many contracts any one trader could hold.
- **January 21:** silver futures went to liquidation-only. You could sell your contracts, but nobody could buy new ones.

With no new buyers allowed, the price had only one way to go. Paul Volcker's Fed was also squeezing credit at the time. On **March 27, "Silver Thursday,"** silver fell to around $10.80 and the Hunts missed a margin call from their broker, Bache. A bank consortium lent them roughly $1.1 billion to stop the failure from spreading. The brothers ended up in bankruptcy later that decade.

**The twist:** The real backstop against a corner isn't the market. It's whoever writes the rulebook. "Liquidation only" rhymes almost exactly with January 28, 2021, when Robinhood restricted GameStop to closing trades only, citing its clearinghouse deposit requirements. Each time the longs were right that the price would keep rising if buying stayed open, and each time the exchange or broker stopped the buying. There were also accusations in 1980 that some exchange board members held short positions when they voted for those rules. That was investigated, but I'm not sure what it concluded, so treat it as an allegation.

**Meanwhile:**
Whisper never hears less than 30 seconds of audio. A 4-second Common Voice clip reaches the model as 4 seconds of Welsh followed by 26 seconds of silence.

**How it works:** The encoder takes a fixed 30-second window, and anything shorter is padded to fill it. Longer audio is handled one 30-second window at a time. In OpenAI's original long-form method, the model predicts timestamp tokens, and the decoder uses the last complete timestamp to decide where the next window starts. The output is also capped at 448 tokens per window. A byte-level BPE vocabulary trained mostly on English probably splits Welsh into more pieces than English. I doubt you'll hit the cap with normal speech, but that's a guess, not something I've measured.

**Two traps for your run:**
- **Timestamps:** Most popular fine-tuning recipes (the Hugging Face blog one, for example) train with `<|notimestamps|>`. If yours does too, the model may get worse at the one skill long-form transcription depends on. Short clips will look fine and long BBC segments will drift, repeat or drop words at the window edges. I'm confident about the mechanism and less sure how badly it hits a 3-hour fine-tune. That's what your eval should measure.
- **Silence:** Whisper is known to produce fluent text over silence or music that nobody said. A 2024 study ("Careless Whisper", Koenecke et al.) found invented phrases in a small but real share of transcripts. Common Voice is mostly short clips, so your run is teaching on mostly padded silence.

**The twist, for the script Claude is writing right now:** If the eval only scores Common Voice test clips, it can't see either failure. Also check that the text normalizer keeps the Welsh circumflex, so that *tân* (fire) and *tan* (until) don't get merged and hide real errors.
