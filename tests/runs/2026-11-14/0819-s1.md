**Meanwhile:**
**That `*IDN?` you send your scope is older than SCPI. And the wire under it is older than the microprocessor.**

The history, in layers:
- **Wire:** HP built HP-IB in the late 1960s to connect its own instruments. It became **IEEE 488** in 1975. It is an 8-bit parallel bus on a 24-pin connector, with up to 15 devices and about 20 m of cable in total.
- **Grammar:** In 1987, **IEEE 488.2** fixed message formats, status registers and the common commands: `*IDN?`, `*RST`, `*OPC?`.
- **Vocabulary:** In 1990 a group of vendors published **SCPI**, a shared command tree (`MEAS:VOLT:DC?`) on top of 488.2. Before that, every vendor invented its own commands.

The clever part is the **three-wire handshake** (DAV, NRFD, NDAC). A byte only completes once *every* listener has released "not ready" and then "not accepted". So the bus runs at the speed of its slowest device, and no device ever misses a byte. That works without a clock or buffers, and with almost no logic in the device. In 1975 that mattered a lot.

It spread beyond the lab, too. The Commodore PET (1977) used IEEE-488 for its disk drives and printers, and the later C64's serial bus was a pared-down version of it.

**The twist:** SCPI survived because it is tied to the *message contract*, not the wire. The same `MEAS:VOLT?` runs over GPIB, USBTMC, raw sockets, VXI-11 or HiSLIP. Your USB adapter just moves the old bus onto a new transport. The physical layer has been replaced several times, and the language never had to change.

1) **Deeper:** how the handshake works step by step, and why an adapter can quietly break `SRQ` and serial poll
2) **Sideways:** SCPI's short forms (`MEAS:VOLT?` = `MEASure:VOLTage?`) and the parsing traps hidden in them
3) **Wildcard:** the Go triple ko said to have been played the night before Oda Nobunaga's death, and why Japanese rules can still void a game over it

**Meanwhile:**
**The three-wire handshake is fully reliable, but slow on purpose. Your USB adapter makes the opposite trade.**

**The handshake, per byte.** All the lines are open-collector and active-low, so any single device can hold a line asserted. NRFD and NDAC are wired-OR across the bus.
1. Each listener releases **NRFD** (not ready for data) when it's ready. The line only goes high once *every* listener is ready.
2. The talker puts the byte on DIO1–8 and asserts **DAV** (data valid).
3. The listeners assert NRFD again ("busy"), latch the byte, and release **NDAC** (not data accepted). NDAC only goes high once the *slowest* listener has the byte.
4. The talker drops DAV, the listeners re-assert NDAC, and the cycle repeats.

No device ever has to know the speed of any other device. That's why a 1975 printer and a 2020 scope can share one cable.

**SRQ is shared and anonymous.** Any device can pull SRQ low, and the controller only learns that *someone* wants service. To find out who, it runs a **serial poll**: it sends SPE (0x18), addresses each suspect as a talker, and reads one status byte from each. Bit 6 (RQS, value 64) marks the device that asked. Then it sends SPD (0x19). Reading the status byte also clears RQS.

**Where adapters break it:**
- **SRQ stops being an interrupt.** Many adapters, Prologix for example, expose SRQ as a command you poll (`++srq`, `++spoll`), not as an event pushed to you. Code written for a "wait for SRQ" callback on a real GPIB card can hang or spin on an adapter.
- **Latency adds up on every transaction.** Many adapters use FTDI USB-serial chips, whose latency timer defaults to 16 ms. A loop of short query/response pairs can spend most of its time waiting on that timer. I'm fairly sure, but not certain, which chip any given adapter uses.
- **Termination mismatches.** If the EOI line and the EOS character (`\n`) settings don't match between adapter and instrument, every read waits for its full timeout. It looks like a slow instrument when it's really a config problem.

**The twist:** with a USB adapter, a blocking `*OPC?` with a generous timeout is often more reliable than the "proper" SRQ-driven completion. It turns an event you might miss into a synchronous wait you can't miss. Event-driven design depends on the transport delivering events, and some don't.

1) **Deeper:** the 488.2 status model. `*ESE 1; *SRE 32; INIT; *OPC` makes the instrument raise SRQ when it finishes, and here's where the masks catch people out.
2) **Sideways:** LAN instruments on raw socket port 5025 hit the same small-message latency trap, this time through Nagle's algorithm and delayed ACKs
3) **Wildcard:** Shusaku's "ear-reddening move" (1846). An opponent's doctor reportedly saw it was decisive by watching the other player's ears.
