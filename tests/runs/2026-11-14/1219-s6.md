**The database Claude is porting you off of is older than the iPhone, older than Mac OS X, and arguably older than Swift's own designers' careers at Apple. And the port isn't really leaving it.**

Core Data shipped in Mac OS X 10.4 Tiger in April 2005. It's widely seen as a descendant of NeXT's Enterprise Objects Framework from the mid-1990s, which mapped objects onto databases for WebObjects apps. Apple got all of it when it bought NeXT in 1997. SwiftData arrived at WWDC 2023 for iOS 17 with `@Model` macros and Swift-native syntax. Underneath, it still runs on the Core Data stack.

You can see it on disk. Open a Core Data SQLite store and every entity gets a table named `Z` plus the entity name in capitals: `ZROUTE`, `ZASCENT`. Each table also has bookkeeping columns: `Z_PK` (primary key), `Z_ENT` (entity ID) and `Z_OPT` (a version counter for optimistic locking). A SwiftData store looks the same: same Z tables, same `Z_PK`.

**The twist:** Apple's WWDC23 migration guidance shows that SwiftData and Core Data can share one store. Your old route logs don't have to be "moved" into a new database, because they're already in the format SwiftData reads. What has to line up is your model definitions: names and attributes. So the risky part of the port is less about storage and more about whether a renamed property, like `grade` → `vGrade`, quietly makes SwiftData think it's a different attribute.

**Meanwhile:**
**Your fingerprints probably make you worse at holding crimps on smooth holds.**

In 2009, Peter Warman and Roland Ennos tested fingertips on smooth acrylic and published the results in the *Journal of Experimental Biology*. The usual story is that fingerprint ridges exist for grip, like tyre tread. They found the opposite. Ridges cut down the skin that actually touches the surface, and on smooth surfaces less contact means less friction.

That's because skin breaks a rule you learned in school. Amontons' law (1699) says friction depends on how hard you press, not on how much surface touches. That's roughly true for wood and steel. Skin is soft, so friction on skin depends a lot on how much it actually touches. Pressing harder doesn't scale friction up the way the law predicts.

Moisture works the same way. A little sweat softens the outer layer of skin so it fills more of the hold, and friction goes up. Too much sweat turns into a lubricating film, and friction drops. Grip peaks somewhere in the middle.

**The twist:** that's why really dry fingertips feel glassy, and why some climbers lick or breathe on their fingers before a slopey move. Chalk fixes sweaty hands, but it can't fix skin that's already too dry. Whether chalk ever makes grip *worse* is still debated, and I'm not confident about which studies settled what.

**Meanwhile:**
**Building a new road can make everyone's commute longer. Mathematically, not because of bad planning.**

The German mathematician Dietrich Braess proved it in 1968. Picture a city where drivers each pick the fastest route for themselves. Add a shortcut and everyone piles onto it. Once they all do, the new routes they've settled into are slower for everyone than the old ones were. No single driver can do better by switching back, so they're stuck. The selfish best choice and the best choice for the group come apart.

Real cities have seen this. Seoul tore down the elevated Cheonggye freeway around 2003–2005 and turned it into a stream park, and traffic didn't get worse the way people feared. Removing roads elsewhere has had similar results. Exact effects are hard to isolate, so treat the city examples as suggestive rather than clean proofs.

**The twist:** it isn't only about traffic. In 1991, Joel Cohen and Paul Horowitz showed the same paradox with springs and strings in *Nature*. Hang a weight from a chain of springs joined by a short string. Cut that string, and the weight *rises*. Cut a connection, and the system gets stronger. Electrical circuits show it too.

1) **Deeper:** how the "price of anarchy" puts a number on how much selfish routing can cost: at most 4/3 in the simplest traffic models.

**Meanwhile:**
**The worst-case cost of letting everyone be selfish fits into one clean number: 4/3.**

Start with the economist Arthur Pigou's example from 1920. There are two roads from A to B. Road 1 always takes 1 hour. Road 2 takes as long as the fraction of drivers on it: x hours, where x is that fraction. If everyone's on Road 2, it takes 1 hour.

Each driver reasons: "Road 2 is never slower than Road 1, so I'll take it." Everyone does, and everyone spends 1 hour.

Now suppose a planner sends half the drivers each way. Road 1 takes 1 hour. Road 2 takes 0.5 hours. The average is 0.75 hours. The selfish result costs 1 ÷ 0.75 = **4/3** of the best possible.

In 1999, Elias Koutsoupias and Christos Papadimitriou named this ratio the "price of anarchy." Around 2002, Tim Roughgarden and Éva Tardos proved that when travel time grows in a straight line with traffic, no network, however complicated, does worse than 4/3. Pigou's two-road toy is already the worst case. If travel time grows faster than that, for example with congestion that blows up, there is no limit.

**The twist:** Roughgarden and Tardos also proved this: the selfish equilibrium never costs more than the *optimal* routing of **twice the traffic**. Put another way, doubling capacity guarantees you do at least as well as perfect central coordination would on the original network. Throwing hardware at the problem is mathematically a substitute for coordination. That's part of why internet backbones over-provision bandwidth instead of centrally scheduling every packet. This area of research shared the 2012 Gödel Prize.

1) **Deeper:** the "selfish load balancing" version: servers and jobs, and why random assignment is surprisingly close to optimal.
2) **Sideways:** congestion pricing in Stockholm and London, which uses tolls to make the selfish choice and the group's best choice line up.
3) **Wildcard:** why 4/3 shows up again in a completely different place: the way traffic flows through a crowd exiting a stadium.
