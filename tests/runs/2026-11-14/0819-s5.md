**Meanwhile:**
**Your old thread pool was quietly protecting your database, and virtual threads take that protection away.**

Here's how it works. With a 200-thread Tomcat or executor pool, no more than 200 requests could be talking to the DB at once. The pool limited load without anyone deciding it should. Virtual threads (JEP 444, final in Java 21) are cheap enough to run one per request, so 5,000 concurrent claims requests now means 5,000 threads asking HikariCP for one of maybe 20 connections. They queue, and after `connectionTimeout` (30s by default) they start failing. The limit didn't go away. It moved to the connection pool, which gives you errors instead of a slow queue. The usual fix is to add the limit back on purpose, for example with a `Semaphore` around the DB or downstream calls.

There's a second catch specific to Java 21. A virtual thread that blocks inside a `synchronized` block "pins" its carrier, meaning the real OS thread underneath it. By default there are only about as many carriers as CPU cores. In July 2024 Netflix's tech blog described a production deadlock from exactly this: every carrier was pinned waiting on a lock, and the virtual thread holding the lock couldn't get a carrier to release it. JDK 24 removed most of this pinning (JEP 491), but you're on 21. A Java 8 monolith probably has plenty of old `synchronized` code: DAOs, caches, singletons.

**Why it matters for your migration:** a test suite won't show either problem. Both appear only under real concurrency. Before merging, a short load test with the JFR event `jdk.VirtualThreadPinned` enabled would show you where they are.

**Meanwhile:**
**One formula with three letters, and it works without knowing anything about how your traffic arrives.**

**L = λW**: the average number of items in a system equals their arrival rate times the average time each one stays. John Little published the general proof in 1961 (*Operations Research*, "A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW"). The unusual part is how little it assumes. It doesn't care about arrival patterns, service-time distributions or scheduling order. It only needs the system to be stable over the long run.

Here's what it means for your connection pool:
- 400 claims requests/sec × 50 ms holding a DB connection = **20 connections busy** on average. A pool of 20 runs right at its limit.
- Now the DB slows to 200 ms during a nightly batch job. 400 × 0.2 = **80 connections needed**. You still have 20, so the other 60 requests' worth of demand piles up in Hikari's wait queue every second.

With platform threads, the pool of 200 capped how big that pile could get. With virtual threads, nothing caps it. The pile becomes thousands of parked virtual threads, each holding its request's objects in heap, until timeouts start failing them in bulk.

**The twist:** latency *is* concurrency. A downstream slowdown doesn't just make requests slower. Through λW it directly multiplies how many requests are in flight at once. That's why a slow dependency so often turns into an outage, and why the throttle you add back should be sized from the formula: λ at peak × a W you can tolerate.

1) **Deeper:** why queues blow up nonlinearly once utilization passes about 80% (the Kingman formula)
2) **Sideways:** the same formula on a medieval siege: how long a starving castle lasts as food stock ÷ consumption rate, and why defenders expelled the "useless mouths"
3) **Wildcard:** why supermarkets with a single line feeding all the checkouts beat separate lines per checkout

**Meanwhile:**
**Going from 80% to 90% busy doesn't add 10% to your wait time. It more than doubles it.**

In 1961 J.F.C. Kingman published an approximation for waiting time in a single-server queue:

**Wait ≈ [ρ / (1−ρ)] × [(Ca² + Cs²) / 2] × service time**

- **ρ** is utilization. The term ρ/(1−ρ) is where the jump comes from: 50% → 1, 80% → 4, 90% → 9, 95% → 19, 99% → 99. Each step toward 100% costs more than the one before.
- **Ca and Cs** are the coefficients of variation of arrivals and service times, meaning how bursty or uneven they are. If they're perfectly regular, the middle term goes to zero and the queue nearly disappears. If they're erratic, the wait multiplies.
- Caveat: it's a heavy-traffic approximation for one server. A pool of 20 connections behaves better than one server at the same utilization, but the same sharp rise near 100% is still there.

For your claims service: the earlier Little's Law example had 20 connections busy out of 20, so ρ ≈ 100%. On this curve that means the wait just keeps growing. And claims traffic is very uneven: a quick status lookup next to a heavy settlement query with a big join. That raises Cs, which multiplies the wait again.

**The twist:** there are two ways to cut latency, and only one involves more capacity. Keeping utilization around 70% is the expensive way. **Cutting variability is the cheap way.** For example, give slow report queries their own small pool so they stop holding up fast lookups. Halving the variability term halves the wait without adding any hardware. That's also why "we're only at 85% CPU, we're fine" is a risky thing to say.

1) **Deeper:** bulkheads: why Netflix's Hystrix gave each dependency its own separate pool, and what that did to cascading failures
2) **Sideways:** siege trains had the same problem: why a medieval army's speed depended on its slowest ox cart, not its average one
3) **Wildcard:** why traffic jams with no cause, called "phantom jams", form on highways at the same kind of utilization threshold

**Meanwhile:**
**Philip II of Macedon made his army faster without speeding anything up. He took the slow parts out.**

A marching column moves at the speed of its slowest part. Ox carts are commonly cited at around 2 mph, and oxen can only work part of the day, so an army with a wagon train was limited by its animals and not by its soldiers. According to Frontinus (*Strategemata*), Philip banned carriages and allowed only one servant per ten foot soldiers, so the men carried their own gear. Donald Engels' 1978 study of Macedonian logistics argues this mobility is a large part of how Philip and then Alexander could outmarch their enemies.

Medieval siege trains had the same constraint, and engineers got around it the same way. Big trebuchets usually weren't hauled whole. The army carried the parts that were hard to source, such as ironwork, rope and skilled carpenters, and built the frame on site from local timber. Edward I's "Warwolf" at Stirling in 1304 is the famous example: it was built at the siege by a large team of carpenters. As the story goes, Edward wouldn't accept the garrison's surrender until he had seen it shoot.

**The twist, back in your codebase:** a request that fans out to five services is a column too. Its latency is set by its *slowest* call, not the average one. Jeff Dean and Luiz Barroso called this "The Tail at Scale" (CACM, 2013): if each call is slow 1% of the time, a request fanning out to 100 calls is slow about 63% of the time. Philip's fix carries over. Don't try to speed up the oxen. Take them out of the critical path: cache, run calls in parallel with timeouts, or move the slow call off the request entirely (async, like building the trebuchet on site). Virtual threads make parallel fan-out cheap. They do nothing for the slowest call.
