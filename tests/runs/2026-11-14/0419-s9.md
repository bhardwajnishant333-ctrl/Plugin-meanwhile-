In a narrow, shallow canal, more power doesn't make a boat much faster. It mostly makes the boat sink lower and drag harder.

A moving hull has to push its own volume of water aside. In open water, that water simply flows around it. In a channel barely wider and deeper than the boat, it has to rush backwards through the thin gap under and beside the hull. Fast-flowing water sits lower, so the water level around the boat drops and the boat squats, stern first, towards the bed. Open the throttle and you mostly get a bigger wash, a deeper squat and bank erosion. That's partly why the Canal & River Trust's limit is 4 mph, and why a narrowboat at full revs in a shallow pound barely gains on a walker.

There's an escape hatch, though, and the Victorians found it with horses. In the 1830s, William Houston's light passenger boats on the Paisley canal near Glasgow were towed hard enough to climb onto their own bow wave and ride it. Once over that hump, drag dropped and the wash nearly vanished. Fly-boats ran at roughly 9 to 10 mph, though I'd treat that figure as approximate. In August 1834, John Scott Russell watched a boat on the Union Canal near Edinburgh stop suddenly. The wave it had been pushing kept going on its own, a single smooth hump. He chased it on horseback for a mile or two. It was the first recorded soliton, a wave that holds its shape over long distances, and that same physics now carries pulses through optical fibre.

The ceiling is set by the channel, not the engine. Beyond a certain point you can't grind your way faster; the only way past is to change regime completely. Dragging the boat harder doesn't work. Riding over the wave does.

Russell never gave figures that exact, so I can't either. In his 1844 report to the British Association he described the wave as about thirty feet long and "a foot to a foot and a half in height", moving at "some eight or nine miles an hour". He lost it "after a chase of one or two miles" in the windings of the canal. Those ranges are his own words.

A wave that the leading scientists of the day said shouldn't exist was finally rescued by a computer, 130 years later.

Russell went home and built a wave tank in his garden. He found that a solitary wave's speed depends on its height: taller waves travel faster. George Airy, the Astronomer Royal, said that was wrong, and George Stokes doubted such a wave could keep its shape. Russell died in 1882 with the idea still treated as a curiosity. The mathematics slowly came round: Boussinesq in 1871, Rayleigh in 1876, then Korteweg and de Vries in 1895, whose equation (the KdV equation) is now the standard description. In 1965 Norman Zabusky and Martin Kruskal ran that equation numerically on a computer. They watched two of these waves pass straight through each other and come out unchanged, as if they were particles. They coined the word "soliton". One correction to my last message: soliton pulses in optical fibre were proposed in 1973 and shown at Bell Labs in 1980, but most commercial fibre links don't actually rely on them. In 1995 a team recreated Russell's wave on the Union Canal at the aqueduct now named after him.

The proof came from a simulation, not an experiment. A numerical run showed behaviour that nobody had predicted from the equation itself. It's an early case of code revealing physics rather than just checking it.
