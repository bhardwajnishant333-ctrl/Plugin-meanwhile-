**The "every seventh wave" rule is a myth, but sets are real, and the waves inside them don't move the way you think.**

Swell from a distant storm arrives as several trains with slightly different periods. Where they line up you get a set, and where they cancel you get a lull. The odd part is that in deep water the set's energy moves at only **half the speed** of the waves that carry it. Each wave appears at the back of the group, rides forward through it and dies at the front. You're watching waves pass through a group that moves more slowly than they do.

Speed also depends on period. A 15-second swell's energy covers about 42 km/h, so long-period swell outruns short-period swell and arrives first. In the 1960s Walter Munk used this to track swell from Southern Ocean storms all the way across the Pacific to Alaska. Before that, he and Harald Sverdrup used wave forecasting to help time the D-Day landings.

**The twist:** the bump you paddle for isn't the energy. The energy is the group, and it follows rules the individual waves don't. When the period on the forecast drops hour by hour, the storm is telling you how far away it is.

**On a phone GPU, wiping the screen before every frame can be cheaper than leaving it alone.**

Nearly every mobile GPU uses tile-based rendering. That covers Apple, Mali and Adreno. The GPU cuts the frame into small tiles and renders each one in fast on-chip memory, then writes the finished tile out to main memory. That last step is the costly one, because on a phone, moving data to and from main memory drains far more battery than the maths does.

If you clear the screen, the GPU just fills the tile with a colour on the chip. If you don't clear and keep last frame's pixels, it has to read every tile back in from main memory first. You end up paying for bandwidth just to load pixels you're about to paint over anyway.

**The twist for your port:** URP sets these load and store actions for you, so a single checkbox can quietly add a full round trip to memory. Common culprits are an intermediate texture, copying the depth or opaque texture, camera stacking and an MSAA resolve. On a desktop you'd barely notice. On a mid-range Android phone it shows up as heat and throttling twenty minutes into play. When Claude's done, open the Frame Debugger and count the render target switches.

1) **Deeper**: how to find each extra memory round trip in your URP asset and turn it off
2) **Sideways**: why your phone throttles itself, and why the throttled speed is the real benchmark
3) **Wildcard**: the Atari 2600's trick of "racing the beam": it had no frame buffer, so games drew each line just before the screen displayed it
