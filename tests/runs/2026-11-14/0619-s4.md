**At the big auction houses, some of the bids you watch the auctioneer take don't come from anyone in the room.**

It's called "chandelier bidding." When a lot has a reserve (a secret minimum price the seller agreed to), the auctioneer is allowed to call out bids "on behalf of the consignor" until the price gets close to that reserve. The auctioneer may nod toward the back of the room, but there's nobody there. It's legal and it's written into the conditions of sale. Christie's and Sotheby's both state that they can bid this way, but only *below* the reserve, and that the reserve won't be higher than the low estimate.

There's a second layer. Many big lots are guaranteed before the sale. Sometimes an outside party puts in an "irrevocable bid," which means they've promised to buy at a set price. If someone else outbids them, they're usually paid a fee out of the final price. So a lot can sell to someone who was paid to lose, and the paddle you see go up can be a hedge, not a sign of demand.

**The twist:** the bid sequence looks like a live record of demand, but it's partly made up by people who know where the floor is. In fraud terms, it's a graph where some of the edges were added by the platform itself and are legal. And the price data everyone uses to value art (indices, comparable sales) is built on those hammer prices.

**A fraudster doesn't need to hide its own features. It just needs enough normal neighbors, and your GNN will average it into looking normal.**

Most GNN layers (GCN, mean-pooling GraphSAGE) are low-pass filters. Each layer blends a node with its neighbors, which works when connected nodes share a label. Fraud graphs often break that. A fraud card links mostly to legitimate merchants and cardholders, so the fraud signal gets weaker with every hop.

Dou et al. named this in CARE-GNN (CIKM 2020) and split it into two kinds:
- **Feature camouflage:** the fraudster copies normal-looking features, like amounts, timing and text.
- **Relation camouflage:** the fraudster builds edges to legitimate nodes on purpose.

Their fix is to score each neighbor by how similar it looks under the predicted label, keep only the top-p most similar, and learn p separately for each relation type with reinforcement learning. Tang et al. (ICML 2022, BWGNN) looked at the same problem through the graph spectrum. Anomalies push signal energy toward high frequencies, and a low-pass GNN filters out exactly that part. Their answer is band-pass beta-wavelet filters.

**The twist:** an attacker can't easily erase the contrast between itself and its neighbors, even though it can hide inside the average. So the feature that gives fraud away is how a node *differs* from its neighborhood, not the neighborhood itself.

Two cheap checks to try once your run finishes:
- **Measure homophily on the fraud class.** Use `torch_geometric.utils.homophily` per edge type. If it's low, your aggregator is working against you.
- **Keep the node's own features apart from its neighbors'.** Use SAGEConv with `root_weight=True`, concatenate instead of summing, or add a feature for the gap between the node and its neighborhood mean.

1) **Deeper:** how CARE-GNN's neighbor filter works in practice, and why imbalance makes it worse (PC-GNN)
2) **Sideways:** money-mule rings show up as structure (cycles and fan-in/fan-out patterns) where node features show nothing
3) **Wildcard:** art provenance is a graph too, and forgers fake the edges (fake exhibition records, planted archive photos)

**Some of the most successful art forgers barely bothered faking the paintings well. They faked the paperwork, then planted it in the archives experts trust.**

**John Myatt and John Drewe (UK, 1980s–90s).** Myatt painted roughly 200 fakes of artists like Giacometti, Chagall and Ben Nicholson. Reportedly he sometimes used house emulsion paint thickened with K-Y jelly, so the paintings weren't the clever part. The clever part was Drewe. He got into archives at places like the Tate and the V&A and slipped in fake exhibition catalogues, invoices and photos showing the works. When an expert checked a painting's history, the archive backed it up. Drewe was convicted in 1999 and got six years.

**Wolfgang Beltracchi (Germany, exposed 2010).** He made up an imaginary prewar collection and stuck fake labels from the real dealer Alfred Flechtheim on the backs of his paintings. He also photographed his wife dressed as her own grandmother, posing beside the fakes, using an old camera and old-style photo paper. That gave the paintings a family history. A lab test brought him down: a supposed 1914 Campendonk had titanium white in it, and that pigment wasn't sold until decades later. He was convicted in 2011.

**The twist:** experts mostly judged the paintings by checking them against the records, so faking the records beat the experts. Fraud works the same way. A synthetic identity doesn't need a perfect fake card. It needs a backdated history: aged accounts, small purchases that look normal, links to real merchants. The thing that finally exposes it is something real that can't be faked to fit, like the titanium white. For you, that's anything the fraudster can't control, like how a device behaves physically or the order of real-world settlements.
