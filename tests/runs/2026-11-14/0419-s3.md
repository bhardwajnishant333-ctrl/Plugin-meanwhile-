Whether a ship capsizes depends less on how heavy it is than on the shape of the waterline, and the width counts far more than anything else.

**The mechanism:** Pierre Bouguer worked this out partly while stuck in the Andes on the French expedition to measure the shape of the Earth (1735–1744). He published it as *Traité du navire* in 1746. Euler reached similar results independently around the same time.

- When a ship heels, the underwater part of the hull changes shape, so the centre of buoyancy (B) shifts towards the low side.
- Draw a vertical line up through the new B. Where it crosses the ship's centreline is the **metacentre (M)**.
- If M is above the ship's centre of gravity (G), buoyancy and weight form a couple that rolls the ship back upright. If M is below G, the same couple rolls it further over. The distance GM, called the metacentric height, is the single number that decides which happens.
- Bouguer's key result is BM = I / V. Here I is the second moment of area of the waterplane (the hull's outline at the waterline) and V is the volume of water the ship displaces. For a box-shaped hull, I grows with the **cube** of the beam (the ship's width), so BM grows with the square of the beam divided by the draught (how deep it sits). Make a ship a little wider and it gets a lot more stable.

That is why the *Vasa*'s problem was plain geometry. A heavy second gun deck raised G, the hull was relatively narrow, and so GM was tiny or negative. No amount of skilled craftsmanship could fix that.

**The twist:** more stability is not always better. A ship with a large GM is "stiff": it snaps back upright quickly, with a short, violent roll. That throws sailors around, strains masts and rigging, and makes guns hard to aim. A ship with a small GM is "tender": it rolls slowly and comfortably, but closer to the edge. Naval architects deliberately aim for a moderate GM. So stability is a trade-off you tune, not a quantity to maximise.

1. **Deeper:** free surface effect, where loose water sloshing inside a hull quietly wipes out GM. It was a factor in the 1987 *Herald of Free Enterprise* ferry disaster.
2. **Sideways:** why tall buildings carry huge swinging weights (tuned mass dampers), such as the 660-tonne ball in Taipei 101
