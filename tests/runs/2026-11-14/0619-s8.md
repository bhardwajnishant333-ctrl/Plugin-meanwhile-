Chili heat probably evolved to fight a fungus, not to hurt you, and birds can't feel it at all.

Birds have a version of the TRPV1 receptor that capsaicin doesn't trigger (Jordt & Julius, *Cell*, 2002). They eat chilies and spread the seeds intact. Mammals chew and crush seeds, and they get burned. That much has been known for a while. In 2008 Joshua Tewksbury's team studied wild *Capsicum chacoense* in Bolivia and found the stronger driver (*PNAS*). Insects pierce the fruit, and *Fusarium* fungus gets in through the holes and rots the seeds. Capsaicin slows *Fusarium* down. Where insects and fungus were common, more plants were hot. Where they were rare, more were mild.

The twist is that the mild plants never died out. Heat costs the plant something. A follow-up study found that pungent plants lose more water, so in drier areas the mild ones set more seed. Each population keeps a mix of hot and mild plants, and the mix shifts with how much fungus is around. It's like a defense you only pay for where the threat is real, with the setting tuned by local conditions.

Your jar runs a relay, and each runner makes the jar too sour for itself to stay.

**The handoffs.** Carl Pederson mapped this order in sauerkraut from the 1930s to the 1960s. Pepper mashes have been studied less, but they seem to follow the same pattern.

1. **Salt picks who gets in.** At 2–5% salt, many spoilage bacteria stall, while lactic acid bacteria keep going.
2. **Leuconostoc mesenteroides goes first.** It makes lactic acid, acetic acid and a lot of CO2. That gas pushes out the oxygen, which keeps out the aerobic microbes. The early bubbling is Leuconostoc.
3. **The acid then shuts it down.** As the pH falls, Leuconostoc stops growing. More acid-tolerant lactobacilli take over, and *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* usually finishes the job. It makes lactic acid without much gas and pushes the pH down toward about 3.5.
4. **The safety line is pH 4.6.** *Clostridium botulinum* can't grow below it, and that number is the basis for US acidified-food rules.

**The twist:** when the bubbling stops, the ferment usually hasn't stopped. It has passed from the gassy first stage to the quiet acid-maker. Bubbles tell you who's working, not whether anything is. A pH strip is the only real way to tell when it's done.
