**Meanwhile:**
Google created protobuf, and it removed the `required` keyword from its own format because the keyword made schemas impossible to change safely.

**Mechanism:** In proto2, the parser rejects any message that is missing a required field. Mark an existing field required, and your new readers reject messages from old senders. Remove `required`, and your old readers reject messages from new senders. A rolling deploy runs old and new versions side by side, so neither change is ever safe. The official docs have a heading for this: "Required Is Forever." proto3 (v3.0, 2016) dropped `required` entirely. It also dropped presence tracking for scalar fields: default values aren't put on the wire, so an `int32` set to 0 and one never set look identical. That broke enough real code that `optional` came back, as an experiment in 3.12 (2020) and on by default in 3.15 (2021). Editions (2023) turned presence into a per-field setting, and explicit presence is the default again.

**Twist:** The wire carries only field numbers and wire types, never names. You can rename a field freely, but you can never reuse a field number. If a deleted number gets reused, an old binary silently decodes the new data as the old field. That's why `reserved` exists. For your 40 services this matters: a green test suite proves the all-new versions work together. It doesn't prove that old and new versions work together during the rollout. `buf breaking` checks for exactly that.

The tritone sub only works exactly because a piano is slightly out of tune. In pure tuning the two "shared" notes aren't actually shared.

**Mechanism:** G7 is G B D F and Db7 is Db F Ab Cb. The engine of each chord is its 3rd and 7th: B and F in G7, F and Cb (which is B) in Db7. These are the same two keys on the piano, with their roles swapped, and that's why the sub resolves to C so smoothly. B goes up to C, F goes down to E, and the bass slides from Db down to C.

The swap depends on one quirk of 12-TET. The tritone is exactly 600 cents, half an octave, so flipping it gives back the same interval. No other interval inside the octave does that.

Now tune the chords by the harmonic series, the way barbershop quartets do. A dominant 7th becomes 4:5:6:7, so its 3rd and 7th form a 7:5 ratio, or 1200·log₂(7/5) ≈ **582.5 cents**. In G7 that's B up to F. In Db7, F up to B is also 582.5 cents, which makes B up to F 1200 − 582.5 ≈ **617.5 cents**. The "same" pair of notes is 35 cents apart in the two chords, about a third of a semitone.

**Twist:** The tritone sub is partly a product of equal temperament. A band tuning each chord by ear, like a barbershop quartet or a string section, has to move those two "common" notes about 35 cents to go from G7 to Db7. Equal temperament makes the move cost nothing by putting both chords slightly out of tune. The trick bebop leaned on comes from that compromise: equal temperament makes the tritone exactly self-inverse, so two dominants a tritone apart share their guide tones exactly.

**Meanwhile:**
By default gRPC sets no deadline, so a call can wait forever. In Go, a single `context.Background()` in one handler is enough to make a slow dependency take down services far upstream.

**Mechanism:** When a client sets a deadline, gRPC sends it as the `grpc-timeout` header. grpc-go puts it on the server handler's `ctx`. If you pass that `ctx` to downstream calls, the remaining time travels with them automatically. Say the edge allows 200 ms, service A spends 50 ms, and A calls B: B gets about 150 ms. Once time runs out, every hop gets canceled.

Now picture one handler doing `ctx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(context.Background(), 5*time.Second)`. That breaks the chain. The edge gives up at 200 ms, but B keeps running for up to 5 s on a request nobody is waiting for. Add retries and it gets worse: if three layers each retry 3 times, the bottom service can see 3³ = 27 times the load. Meanwhile the abandoned work is still holding goroutines, connections and DB slots.

The slowdown creates the load that keeps things slow. Bronson et al. call this a *metastable failure* (HotOS 2021): the system stays broken even after the original trigger is gone.

**Twist:** A green test suite almost never catches this. Tests run with fast fakes and no contention, so a broken deadline chain looks the same as a working one. It only shows up under load. For 40 services, a quick audit is to grep handler code paths for `context.Background()` and `context.TODO()`. Any hit inside a request path is a likely break in the chain. Also check for a fresh `WithTimeout` that isn't derived from the incoming `ctx`. Your regenerated stubs won't fix this, because it lives in hand-written code.

**Meanwhile:**
gRPC's retry throttle has a cutoff built into its math. With a `tokenRatio` of 0.1, retries switch off on their own once about 9% of calls fail. That isn't a limit anyone configured. It's the point where the bucket starts draining.

**Mechanism:** You turn this on in the service config (gRFC A6). `retryPolicy` sets `maxAttempts` (capped at 5), the backoff settings and `retryableStatusCodes`. `retryThrottling` adds a token bucket per client channel, filled to `maxTokens`:
- Every failed RPC removes 1 token.
- Every success adds `tokenRatio`.
- Retries and hedges are allowed only while the bucket holds more than **maxTokens / 2**.

At a ratio of 0.1, the bucket breaks even when failures × 1 = successes × 0.1. That works out to a failure rate of 0.1 / 1.1 ≈ **9.1%**. Above that, the tokens drain to the halfway mark and the channel stops retrying. It keeps sending first attempts. When the backend recovers, successes refill the bucket and retries come back.

This is the "retry budget" idea from Google's SRE book (chapter "Handling Overload"), which also gives two rules:
- Cap retries at about 10% of requests per client.
- Retry at only one layer of the stack. Every other layer returns an "overloaded, don't retry" error.

The second rule is what removes the 3³ = 27x effect from the cascade.

**Twist:** The throttle runs per channel on the client, with no shared view. Each of your 40 services throttles itself, so a deep call chain where every hop has its own retry policy still multiplies load at each hop. The fix is choosing which layer retries, not tuning the number. Hedging is a sharper tool than retries: it sends a duplicate after `hedgingDelay` without waiting for a failure. Without throttling it doubles load at the worst moment. As far as I know, grpc-go still implements retries but not hedging, so check that before you rely on it.

1) **Deeper:** why a 9% failure rate can still be a disaster. Tail latency at fan-out: if one request fans out to 100 backends, each with a 1% chance of being slow, about 63% of requests hit at least one slow backend (Dean and Barroso, "The Tail at Scale," 2013).
