In bughouse, the same trade can be winning or losing depending on a board you aren't playing on.

Every capture goes across the table. When you take a piece, your partner gets it to drop. When your opponent takes one of yours, their partner gets it. So an even trade on your board hands one piece to your partner and one to your partner's opponent. Neither of you is really the one gaining anything. Whether the trade is good depends on which of those two other players can use a fresh piece better. If your partner is attacking, every trade is ammunition for them. If your partner is under attack, the same trade gives the player hunting their king one more knight to drop next to it. That's why teams shout requests across the table and sometimes "sit", stalling on the clock until the piece they need arrives.

Pawns change value too. You can drop one anywhere except the first and last ranks, including the 7th, one step from promoting. A pawn that has promoted turns back into a plain pawn when it's captured. The rough values players usually quote are knight, bishop and rook all about 2 and queen about 4. I'd treat those as folk wisdom, not something anyone has measured carefully.

The twist is that in bughouse, material has no fixed value. A piece is worth whatever the person who ends up holding it can do with it.

Classical game theory can't explain why two teammates would both pick the obviously better option.

The puzzle is called Hi-Lo. Two players each choose Hi or Lo without talking. If both pick Hi, each gets 2. If both pick Lo, each gets 1. If they pick differently, both get 0. Everyone picks Hi, but the standard theory can't justify it. Both-Hi and both-Lo are each a Nash equilibrium, meaning neither player gains by changing alone. "I'll pick Hi because I expect you to" only works if you already expect it, which is circular. Economists Michael Bacharach and Robert Sugden argued the gap is in the question being asked. People in a team don't ask "what's my best reply?" They ask "what's our best plan, and what's my part in it?" Bacharach called this team reasoning. His book on it, *Beyond Individual Choice*, came out in 2006, after he died.

Bughouse is that idea under clock pressure. Sitting while your clock runs, or refusing a trade that's good on your own board, is a bad move for you alone and a good one for the team. Players who think "my board, my game" make locally correct moves that lose the match.

The twist is that "rational" depends on who you count as the player. Change the unit from me to us and the irrational move becomes the right one.

A shared default can be worth more than any amount of reasoning.

In *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960), Thomas Schelling asked people to imagine this: you have to meet a stranger in New York tomorrow, with no time, no place and no way to get in touch. Where do you go? Most said the information booth at Grand Central Terminal. Asked for a time, almost everyone said noon. He tried simpler versions too. Asked to call "heads" or "tails" and match an unknown partner, a large majority said heads. I remember the count as about 36 out of 42, but I'm not certain of it. Schelling called these focal points: answers that stand out because each person knows the others will notice them too. Logic alone can't pick out noon over 3:17. Shared culture can. He won the economics Nobel in 2005, partly for this work.

One detail makes it better. His respondents were mostly from around New Haven, and Grand Central was where their trains came in. The "obvious" answer was local. A group of Londoners might well have chosen Times Square or somewhere else.

The twist is that coordinating without talking runs on shared background, not intelligence. That makes conventions a form of infrastructure. It's also why bughouse partners who've played together for years can stall on the clock or give up a piece without saying a word, while two strong strangers often can't.
