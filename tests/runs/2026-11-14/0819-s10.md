**There's a whole family of perfumes named after a plant that has almost no smell.**

"Fougère" is French for fern. Ferns barely smell of anything. In 1882 the perfumer Paul Parquet at Houbigant made *Fougère Royale*, a scent of what a fern *ought* to smell like: lavender, oakmoss, and a new ingredient called coumarin. Coumarin is the sweet, hay-like note found in tonka beans. William Henry Perkin first made it in a lab in 1868, so Fougère Royale was one of the first big perfumes built around a synthetic. It caught on so widely that "fougère" became a category. Most classic barbershop men's scents still come from that one bottle.

**The twist:** Parquet wasn't copying a smell. He was translating an idea, the green, cool, forest-floor feeling of a fern, into a language noses could read. You do the same thing when an idiom has no literal match in English: you rebuild the effect rather than the words. Most perfumes labelled "fern," "rain" or "fresh linen" work this way. They're believable fictions, not copies.

**Meanwhile:**
**The AI building your website comes from a machine invented to translate languages.**

Machine translation has been built three ways:

- **Rules (1950s onward).** Linguists wrote grammar rules and dictionaries by hand. In the 1954 Georgetown–IBM demo, a computer translated about 60 Russian sentences into English using six rules and around 250 words. The press was amazed. It fell apart on any real text.
- **Statistics (1990s–2016).** Instead of rules, you feed the computer huge amounts of text that humans have already translated, and it learns which phrases tend to match. IBM's early work learned from the Canadian Parliament's records, which are published in both English and French. Google Translate worked this way from 2006 until late 2016.
- **Attention (2017).** Google researchers published a design called the *Transformer*, tested on English–German and English–French. Its key idea is "attention": for each word it writes, the model looks back over the whole sentence and decides which words matter most. That's how it gets "casa branca" right as "white house", noticing that the Portuguese adjective comes after the noun and swapping the order.

**The twist:** that design turned out to work for almost anything, not just translation. It's the "T" in ChatGPT, and the same basic design runs the agent working on your site right now. In a very real sense, it's *translating*: your request in plain English goes in, and a website's code comes out, the way a sentence in one language turns into another. That makes you, a translator, unusually well placed to judge it. You already know a translation can be fluent and still subtly wrong.

**Meanwhile:**
**Smelling is easy for English speakers. Putting a smell into words is where they struggle.**

In a 2014 study, Asifa Majid and Niclas Burenhult tested English speakers and the Jahai, a hunter-gatherer people in Malaysia. English speakers named colours easily. Given everyday smells like cinnamon, they rambled and guessed. The Jahai named smells as quickly and consistently as colours, because their language has abstract smell words. Those words describe a quality, the way "red" does, rather than a source.

English mostly doesn't have that. We say "smells *like* lemon," naming a thing instead of the smell. So perfumers borrowed words from another sense: music. In the 1850s the perfumer Septimus Piesse arranged scents on a musical scale he called the "odophone," and the industry has spoken in *notes*, *chords* and *accords* ever since. Top notes, heart notes, base notes are a musical metaphor laid over the nose.

**The twist:** every perfume description is a translation from a language English barely has into one it does. You know how that goes. When the target language has no word, you reach for a metaphor and hope the reader feels the same thing. So when you write up your own blends, you're doing the job you're trained for, just from your nose into English.
