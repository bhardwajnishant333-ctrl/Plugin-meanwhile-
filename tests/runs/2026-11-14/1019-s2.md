**There are positions that are a forced win with perfect play and still a legal draw, and the official rulebook took that into account for a few years before giving up.**

The co-creator of Unix, Ken Thompson, did this in the late 1970s and 1980s. He built endgame databases with *retrograde analysis*: list every checkmate, step back one move at a time, and mark each position "won in N." It's a backward breadth-first search over every legal position. His results broke a long-held belief. Some endings people had written off as draws turned out to be wins, and some wins needed more than 50 moves. In the most famous case, Walter Browne (a strong GM) was challenged to beat the database with queen vs rook within 50 moves. As I recall, he failed on his first attempt, studied, and only just managed it on his second. I'm sure of that story in outline but not of every detail.

So FIDE patched the spec. In the late 1980s it extended the 50-move rule for certain endings, such as two bishops vs knight. More computation kept turning up longer wins, the exceptions list kept growing, and in 1992 FIDE dropped them all and went back to a flat 50 moves.

Today's numbers: the 7-piece Lomonosov tablebases (Moscow State University, 2012) include a forced mate in **549 moves**. Under tournament rules that position is a draw.

**The twist:** the modern Syzygy tablebases that Lichess uses don't count "moves to mate." They count *moves until the 50-move counter resets*. The Lichess explorer labels these positions **"cursed win"** and **"blessed loss"**. The ground truth didn't change. Programs had to start optimizing for the rule instead of for the mathematical truth. A spec that disagrees with reality but wins anyway should feel familiar to anyone migrating to App Router.

**A 19th-century "proof" stood for 130 years and turned out to be wrong, while a man who worked mostly alone during wartime got a huge endgame nearly right.**

**The refutation:** in 1851 Kling and Horwitz analyzed two bishops vs knight. They found a defensive setup, a knight sheltered near the corner with the king beside it, and concluded the ending was generally a draw. That was accepted for well over a century. In the early 1980s Thompson's databases showed it is generally a **win**, though it can take more than 50 moves. That's the exact ending behind FIDE's temporary rule extension. The fortress was real, but the defender can't always reach it. Kling and Horwitz had verified a local position and generalized it to a global claim.

**The survivor:** Alexei Troitsky spent years on two knights vs pawn, one of the hardest endings in the game. Two knights can't force mate against a bare king, but the defender's pawn gives the attacker a chance. Troitsky worked out how far the pawn must be blockaded for the win to work, now called the **Troitsky line**, and published it in the 1930s. He died during the siege of Leningrad in 1942. Tablebases later confirmed his analysis was overwhelmingly correct. He did it with no computer, just a board and decades of work.

**How studies die:** a study must have *exactly one* winning line. A second solution is called a **cook**, and an alternative move at a non-critical point is called a **dual**. Tablebases cooked many classic studies. I won't name specific ones, because I'm not confident which famous studies fell and which survived.

**The twist:** tablebases turned checking a study from a proof into a lookup. That pushed serious composers toward positions with more pieces, where no table exists, so the human search stays the only check. That's the same move your test suite makes: the bugs worth worrying about live in states nobody enumerated.

**Meanwhile:**
**Two knights can't mate a bare king. Give the defender one extra pawn and they often can. The defender's own material is what loses.**

**Why:** with two knights against a lone king, you can build a mating net, but the defender always has a way out. Either he avoids the corner, or the only way to close the net stalemates him. He has no other moves, so a stalemate is a draw. A pawn gives him moves. While his pawn can still push, he isn't stalemated, so the attacker can set up positions that would otherwise be illegal draws.

**The mechanism:**
1. One knight **blockades** the pawn so it can't advance.
2. King plus the other knight drive the enemy king into a corner.
3. At exactly the right moment, the blockading knight **lets go** and joins the mating net. The defender is forced to push the pawn instead of being stalemated, and mate lands before the pawn promotes.

**The Troitsky line** is the boundary for step 3: the pawn must be blockaded far enough back that it can't promote in time once the knight leaves. For White against a black pawn it's usually given as **a4, b6, c5, d4, e4, f5, g6, h4**. Blockade at or behind those squares and it's generally a win. The line zigzags because center and edge files are different races: on edge files the corner geometry changes the mating pattern, and on the d- and e-files the knight has more room. I'm confident of the line's existence and its general shape. Trust a tablebase over me on the exact squares.

**The twist:** these wins are long, often well over 50 moves, so many of them are exactly the "cursed wins" from earlier. Troitsky solved an ending that tournament rules frequently won't let you finish.
