**One number in your sightings table tells you how likely the next record is to be a species you've never logged, and the formula comes from Bletchley Park.**

At Bletchley, Alan Turing and I. J. Good needed to know how much of a codebook they hadn't seen yet. They found a simple estimate. Take the number of things you've seen exactly once, N1, and divide by your total observations, N. That gives the chance that your next observation is something new. Good published it in 1953 and credited Turing. A grid square with 400 records and 30 species logged only once has about a 30/400 = 7.5% chance that its next record is a new species for that square.

Ecology later built a cousin of this. Anne Chao's 1984 estimator puts a lower bound on how many species a square really holds: S_obs + f1² / (2·f2). Here f1 is the count of species seen once and f2 the count seen twice. Lots of singletons and few doubletons means a square is under-surveyed.

**The twist:** the rare records carry the information. Your common species tell you almost nothing about what you're missing. Your one-off sightings tell you nearly everything. The same idea shows up as "Good-Turing smoothing", which handled unseen words in pre-neural language models. One caveat: it assumes random sampling, and volunteers aren't random. People chase rarities and skip boring squares. Treat it as a guide, not ground truth.

**For about 60 years, the most famous example of evolution in progress was a gull, and the gull turned out not to fit.**

The classic story, popularised by Ernst Mayr in 1942: a chain of gull populations rings the North Pole, and each one interbreeds with its neighbour. Go all the way round and the two ends overlap in northern Europe as herring gull and lesser black-backed gull. There they mostly don't interbreed. That made it a "ring species": one species shading into two, so speciation could be seen across space instead of time.

In 2004, Liebers, de Knijff and Helbig sequenced mitochondrial DNA across the complex. Their paper was bluntly titled "The herring gull complex is not a ring species." The populations don't form one continuous chain of gene flow. They trace back to several separate lineages that split during Pleistocene glaciations, then spread out and met again. Some meetings led to hybrids. Others didn't. I'm confident of the headline result and less sure of the exact colonisation routes they proposed, so I won't spell those out.

**The twist:** this is why many authorities have since split the complex, e.g. American herring gull, Caspian gull, yellow-legged gull and (on some lists) Vega gull. It also means the ambiguous gulls you agonise over are real biology. You're often looking at lineages that met again and blur at the edges, plus actual hybrids. "I can't name this one" is sometimes the correct answer.

Almost certainly the cause: **`ApiError` doesn't implement `IntoResponse`.**

Axum only accepts a function as a handler if its return type implements `IntoResponse`. `Result<T, E>` only does that when **both** `T` and `E` do. `Json<Sighting>` is fine. `ApiError` derives `Debug` and `thiserror::Error`, and neither of those gives you `IntoResponse`. So `create_sighting` stops counting as a handler, and you get that unhelpful E0277.

Fix it in the module where `ApiError` lives:

```rust
use axum::{http::StatusCode, response::{IntoResponse, Response}, Json};
use serde_json::json;

impl IntoResponse for ApiError {
    fn into_response(self) -> Response {
        let status = match &self {
            // map your variants, e.g.:
            // ApiError::NotFound => StatusCode::NOT_FOUND,
            // ApiError::Validation(_) => StatusCode::UNPROCESSABLE_ENTITY,
            // ApiError::Db(sqlx::Error::RowNotFound) => StatusCode::NOT_FOUND,
            _ => StatusCode::INTERNAL_SERVER_ERROR,
        };
        // don't leak sqlx error text to clients on 500s; log it instead
        (status, Json(json!({ "error": self.to_string() }))).into_response()
    }
}
```

If it still fails after that, put `#[axum::debug_handler]` on `create_sighting` (it needs axum's `macros` feature). It swaps this error for one that names the real problem. The other usual causes are:
- The future isn't `Send`, e.g. a non-`Send` value like a `MutexGuard` or `Rc` is held across an `.await`.
- `NewSighting` is missing `#[derive(Deserialize)]`.
- `AppState` doesn't match the type you passed to `.with_state(...)`, or it isn't `Clone`.
